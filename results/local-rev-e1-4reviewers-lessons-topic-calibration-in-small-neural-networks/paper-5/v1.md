# Calibration in Small Neural Networks: Temperature Scaling Dominates Label Smoothing, But Can Backfire Near Chance

## Abstract

The literature on neural network calibration is built almost entirely on large, overparameterized models, where the dominant finding is that bigger models are more overconfident and that temperature scaling (TS) and label smoothing (LS) both help. We ask whether this picture holds when "small" means genuinely small — single-hidden-layer MLPs with widths from 2 to 256 — rather than "small by modern deep-learning standards." Across two datasets (sklearn digits, a synthetic 4-class blobs task with label noise), 8 widths, 2 training conditions (plain cross-entropy vs. label smoothing, ε=0.1), and 15 seeds per cell (480 trained models total), we test two pre-registered hypotheses. First, that expected calibration error (ECE) before any post-hoc correction increases with width, replicating the large-model literature: this does **not** hold cleanly in our width range — the correlation is inconsistent in sign and magnitude across dataset/condition combinations and is not robust to dropping single width groups (Spearman ρ ranges from −0.14, n.s., to +0.30, p<0.001, depending on dataset and condition). Second, that after post-hoc temperature scaling, label smoothing confers no additional calibration benefit over plain training: we find no evidence against this null (paired Wilcoxon, n=240 pairs, p=0.86, rank-biserial=−0.01), and the test is well powered (80% power to detect a mean ECE difference of 0.040, i.e. ~0.19 SD). A third, unplanned but directly checked finding is that temperature scaling itself is unreliable for very narrow, near-chance-accuracy networks: it *increases* ECE in 31% of all 480 runs overall, rising to 42% among the 100 runs with test accuracy below 0.4, driven by unstable temperature fits on a small validation set when the model has barely learned the task. We replicate the core TS-reduces-ECE effect directionally in a small cross-library spot check (scikit-learn's MLPClassifier, n=15), though that check is underpowered (p=0.09) and is disclosed as such. We release all code and raw per-run results.

## 1. Introduction

Guo et al. (2017) showed that modern deep networks are frequently overconfident and that a single learned temperature parameter, fit post-hoc on a validation set, is a remarkably effective and simple fix. A large follow-up literature has examined *why* miscalibration grows with capacity (depth, width, weight decay, batch normalization) and has proposed training-time alternatives such as label smoothing (Müller et al., 2019) and entropy/MMCE regularization as complements or substitutes for post-hoc scaling. Minderer et al. (2021) revisited these findings for more recent architectures and training recipes, largely confirming that capacity growth is associated with worse raw calibration.

**Gap.** Essentially all of this literature studies networks with widths in the hundreds to thousands (or more), i.e. "small" only in a relative, modern-deep-learning sense. Genuinely small networks — the kind used in resource-constrained, embedded, or tabular-data settings where a hidden layer of width 8–64 is a real design point, not a toy — are comparatively unstudied for calibration behavior. It is not obvious a priori that trends established in the overparameterized regime (miscalibration grows with capacity; label smoothing helps; temperature scaling reliably reduces ECE) extrapolate down to networks this narrow, where the model may not even reliably beat chance at the low end of the width sweep.

**This paper.** We run a controlled width sweep (2–256 hidden units) crossed with training condition (plain vs. label-smoothed cross-entropy) on two datasets, with 15 seeds per cell, and ask three questions:

1. Does raw (pre-TS) ECE increase with width in this small-width regime, as the large-model literature would predict by extrapolation?
2. Once temperature scaling is applied, does label smoothing still provide additional calibration benefit, or does TS make the training-time choice moot?
3. Is temperature scaling itself reliable across the whole width range, including near the point where the model is barely better than a majority-class or random guess?

We pre-registered a null hypothesis and test for each of (1) and (2) before running the underlying analysis code — the specific test, its null, and its exact pooling axis are fixed in the code's module docstring and not chosen after inspecting results (Appendix / see `analyze.py`). Question (3) emerged from inspecting the results table for (1)/(2) and is reported as an exploratory, directly-verified finding, not a confirmatory test — we say so explicitly and do not use rejection language for it.

### Related work
- **Guo et al. (2017), "On Calibration of Modern Neural Networks"**: establishes that capacity (depth, width) correlates with overconfidence in modern nets, and that temperature scaling is a strong, simple post-hoc fix. Our width range (2–256) sits below essentially all settings studied there.
- **Müller et al. (2019), "When Does Label Smoothing Help?"**: shows label smoothing improves calibration in large image/translation models by encouraging tighter, better-separated logit clusters. We ask whether this training-time benefit survives once a post-hoc fix is also applied, in a much smaller-capacity regime.
- **Minderer et al. (2021), "Revisiting the Calibration of Modern Neural Networks"**: revisits calibration trends for newer architectures; again in the overparameterized regime.
- **Double-descent / uncertainty literature** (e.g., work on double descent in uncertainty quantification) notes non-monotonic calibration behavior around interpolation thresholds — relevant background for why we did not assume a monotonic width→ECE trend a priori, and pre-registered a directional but falsifiable test rather than assuming the trend.

## 2. Method

**Datasets.** (a) `digits` (sklearn): 1797 samples, 64 features, 10 classes, real handwritten-digit images. (b) `blobs`: a synthetic 4-class classification task (`sklearn.datasets.make_classification`, 1500 samples, 12 features, 6 informative, class separation 1.0, 5% label flip noise) chosen to be a harder, noisier task than digits. Each dataset is split 60/20/20 into train/validation/test, stratified by label, with the split itself controlled by the run's seed.

**Models.** Single-hidden-layer MLPs (Linear → ReLU → Linear) implemented in PyTorch, widths ∈ {2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256}. Trained with Adam (lr=0.001) for 120 full-batch epochs. Two training-loss conditions: plain cross-entropy ("baseline") and label-smoothed cross-entropy with ε=0.1 ("label_smooth"). All other hyperparameters identical across conditions and widths.

**Seeding.** Every (dataset, condition, width, seed-index) cell gets a seed computed by fixed integer arithmetic on its indices (`1_000_000 + dataset_idx*100_000 + condition_idx*10_000 + width_idx*100 + seed_idx`) — no `hash()` or other process-randomized source — so reruns are bit-identical. The seed controls both the train/val/test split and the model's initialization/optimization randomness. 15 seeds per cell, 2 datasets × 2 conditions × 8 widths × 15 seeds = 480 trained models.

**Calibration metric.** Expected Calibration Error (ECE) with 15 equal-width confidence bins on the test set, plus NLL and accuracy, computed before ("raw") and after ("ts") post-hoc temperature scaling. Temperature is fit by minimizing NLL on the validation set (LBFGS, single scalar parameter T>0), independently for every trained model — i.e. each "before/after" pair comes from the literal same trained weights, not from two separately retrained models.

**Pre-registered hypotheses and tests** (fixed before inspecting `raw_results.json`; see `analyze.py` docstring):
- **H1** (one-sided): raw ECE increases with width. Test: Spearman correlation between width and per-run raw ECE, pooled over all 15 seeds, computed separately per (dataset × condition) cell (4 cells, n=120 each). We additionally report a leave-one-width-out robustness check (dropping each width once and recomputing ρ) to check the trend isn't carried by a single width group.
- **H2** (two-sided): after temperature scaling, label smoothing makes no difference to ECE relative to baseline. Test: paired Wilcoxon signed-rank on (ECE_ts,baseline − ECE_ts,label_smooth), paired by (dataset, width, seed_idx) — valid pairing because each pair shares an identical seed, hence an identical train/val/test split, differing only in the training loss used. We report the rank-biserial effect size, a bootstrap 95% CI on the mean paired difference, and a simulation-based power analysis (minimum detectable mean-ECE difference at 80% power, α=0.05, matched to the observed residual SD of the paired differences).
- **Multiplicity**: two primary pre-registered tests (H1, H2) are reported; one additional descriptive check (does TS reduce ECE at all, within each condition) is reported without inferential correction and is explicitly labeled descriptive, not confirmatory.

**Cross-library spot check.** Per the recommendation that a single implementation stack is itself a confound, we additionally trained a small, disjoint set of models (digits dataset, widths {2, 32, 256}, 5 seeds each, n=15) using scikit-learn's `MLPClassifier` (a different numerical backend/optimizer implementation than our hand-written PyTorch loop) and checked whether the TS-reduces-ECE direction replicates. This spot check is small and not a substitute for the main study; we report it with its own p-value and do not treat it as confirmatory.

All code, exact commands, and raw per-run JSON output are included in this directory (`run_experiment.py`, `analyze.py`, `spotcheck_sklearn.py`, `extra_check.py`, `raw_results.json`, `results.json`, `spotcheck_results.json`, `extra_check_results.json`).

## 3. Results

**H1 — width vs. raw ECE does not replicate cleanly.** Spearman ρ (width vs. raw ECE, n=120 per cell): blobs/baseline ρ=−0.14 (p_one-sided=0.94, i.e. opposite of the predicted direction), blobs/label_smooth ρ=+0.30 (p=0.0004, supports H1), digits/baseline ρ=0.00 (p=0.49, flat), digits/label_smooth ρ=+0.12 (p=0.09, marginal). Only 1 of 4 cells shows a significant increasing trend, and the leave-one-width-out check shows sign flips in two of the four cells depending on which width is dropped (e.g. blobs/baseline ranges from −0.38 to +0.02; digits/baseline ranges from −0.38 to +0.33). Inspecting the underlying summary table shows why: on digits/baseline, raw ECE rises sharply from width 2 (0.081) to a peak at width 16 (0.491) as accuracy climbs from near-chance (0.19) to good (0.75), then *falls* back down through widths 32–256 (0.464 → 0.093) as accuracy keeps improving (0.88 → 0.95) — an inverse-U shape, not the monotonic increase the large-model literature would predict by extrapolation. **We conclude the "wider models are more miscalibrated" trend does not extrapolate cleanly down to the 2–256 width range studied here**; it appears sensitive to dataset and training condition, and where present, is not robust to single-width perturbation.

**H2 — label smoothing adds nothing once temperature scaling is applied, and the test is well powered.** Paired Wilcoxon signed-rank on post-TS ECE (n=240 pairs): mean difference (baseline − label_smooth) = −0.0020, median = −0.0005, bootstrap 95% CI [−0.029, 0.026], p=0.86, rank-biserial effect size = −0.014 (negligible). Of 240 pairs, 119 favor baseline and 121 favor label smoothing — indistinguishable from a coin flip. A simulation-based power analysis (matched to the observed SD of paired differences, 0.214) shows this test could detect a mean ECE difference as small as 0.040 (≈0.19 SD) with 80% power at α=0.05 — so the null result is not simply an underpowered test failing to find something real. **This directly answers our second question: in this width range, post-hoc temperature scaling appears to make the training-time label-smoothing-vs-plain choice irrelevant for final calibration**, even though the two conditions differ substantially in *raw* (pre-TS) ECE (0.187 vs. 0.216 on average, reduced to 0.174 vs. 0.176 after TS).

**Descriptive: TS reduces ECE overall, but not uniformly.** Averaged over all runs, TS significantly reduces ECE within both conditions (baseline: 0.187→0.174, Wilcoxon p=0.0017; label_smooth: 0.216→0.176, p=0.0014), and label smoothing sees a larger absolute reduction (0.040 vs. 0.013) — consistent with H2's finding that TS erases most of the raw-ECE gap between conditions.

**Exploratory finding: TS is unreliable near chance accuracy.** This was not planned in advance but is directly checked, not asserted. Overall, TS *increases* ECE (i.e. makes calibration worse) in 149/480 runs (31%). Restricting to the 100 runs with test accuracy below 0.4 (near-chance for these tasks), this rises to 42/100 (42%). The clearest case is digits at width 2 (mean test accuracy 0.19, pooling baseline and label-smooth, n=30): the fitted temperature has mean 0.84 but standard deviation 1.14 across seeds, and post-TS ECE has mean 0.350 with standard deviation 0.346 (range 0.003–0.835) — compared to a stable, low raw ECE (mean 0.076, SD 0.041) at the same width. In other words, when the model has barely learned the task, the validation-set-based temperature fit becomes highly seed-dependent and can push calibration in either direction by a large margin, even though the *uncalibrated* probabilities were comparatively well-behaved (close to uniform, hence low raw ECE by construction). **We read this as evidence that post-hoc temperature scaling should not be applied uncritically to very small, weakly-trained networks without checking that the fitted temperature is stable across resampling** — we did not run a targeted stability ablation beyond this direct observation, so we state this as a hypothesis for follow-up work with its own confirmatory design, not a settled mechanism claim.

**Cross-library spot check.** Using scikit-learn's `MLPClassifier` (n=15: 3 widths × 5 seeds, digits only) as an independent implementation, raw ECE fell from 0.062 to 0.035 after TS (mean reduction 0.027, Wilcoxon p=0.095) — same direction as the main PyTorch study but not itself significant at this small n. We report this transparently as underpowered rather than as confirmation. We also note that 10 of the 15 sklearn training runs emitted a `ConvergenceWarning` (max iterations reached before convergence); we did not increase `max_iter` further to keep the spot check cheap, and flag this as a limitation of the spot check specifically (not of the main PyTorch study, which emitted zero runtime warnings across all 480 runs).

## 4. Conclusion

In the genuinely small-network regime (widths 2–256), the standard calibration narrative from the large-model literature only partially transfers. The "capacity increases miscalibration" trend does not replicate cleanly — it is weak, inconsistent in sign across datasets/conditions, and not robust to single-width perturbation, plausibly because very narrow networks pass through a large accuracy range (near-chance to good) within this width band, unlike the already-well-trained large models the original trend was observed in. Label smoothing changes *raw* calibration but a well-powered paired test finds no detectable difference from plain training after applying temperature scaling — practically, this suggests that if a post-hoc calibration step is already part of the pipeline, the choice of label smoothing at training time is close to moot for calibration purposes in this regime (though it may still matter for other reasons, e.g. accuracy or robustness, which we did not evaluate). Most surprisingly, temperature scaling itself is not uniformly beneficial here: it measurably backfires for a substantial minority of near-chance-accuracy networks, driven by unstable validation-based temperature fits.

**Limitations.** This study uses one architecture family (single-hidden-layer ReLU MLPs) and, for its main results, one implementation stack (PyTorch/Adam); a small cross-library spot check points the same direction but is underpowered. Both datasets are small (≤1800 samples, ≤360-sample test/validation splits), which the exploratory TS-instability finding is itself partly attributable to — larger validation sets would likely stabilize the temperature fit and could change the exploratory finding's magnitude, though we would not expect it to flip sign given the direct mechanistic link to near-chance accuracy. We do not claim the width-ECE and TS-reliability findings generalize beyond this two-dataset, single-architecture, small-validation-set setting; establishing that would require a dedicated follow-up varying dataset size, architecture family, and validation-set size independently.
