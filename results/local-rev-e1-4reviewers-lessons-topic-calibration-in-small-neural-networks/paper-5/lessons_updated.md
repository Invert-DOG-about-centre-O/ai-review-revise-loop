# Lessons for writing a better first draft

- **Run multiple seeds from the start for any claim about a specific location, threshold, or boundary.** A single-seed "clean crossover at X" claim is the single most predictable thing reviewers will attack — all four round-1 reviewers independently re-ran the code with different seeds and broke the headline number. If the paper's core finding is "X happens at/between specific value(s)," budget for a seed sweep (10+ seeds) in the first draft, not as a round-2 patch. Report the finding as a distribution (mode/range) plus a variance estimate, not a point.

- **Never derive RNG seeds from `hash()` of a string, or any other process-randomized/non-frozen source.** Python's `hash()` on strings is randomized per-process by default, so seeding via something like `base_seed*K + hash(name)%N` makes every rerun silently produce different data — in this paper it caused all four round-1 reviewers to get different p-values than the paper claimed, none of which matched each other either, burning a full review round on a bug that should have been caught by the author simply rerunning their own script twice. Use a fixed integer mapping (explicit dict or index) for any per-condition seed offset, and rerun your own experiment end-to-end at least twice before submission to confirm bit-identical results.

- **When running a statistical test, fix the null hypothesis independent of the observed data.** Choosing the null's reference set (e.g. "uniform over the three widths we happened to observe") after seeing which values occurred is a post-hoc null, and reviewers will flag it as an easier bar than it looks. Test against a null defined in advance (e.g. uniform over the full grid actually searched), or report both and be explicit about which is the conservative one.

- **Before treating any correlation between a "before" measurement and a computed change/delta/improvement as informative, test it against a mechanical shuffle null — this trap recurs and is easy to miss.** If your "after" or "delta" value is itself arithmetically derived from the "before" value (e.g. `reduction = pre − post`, or any ratio/difference metric), then `corr(pre, reduction)` can be strongly positive or negative purely from shared variance, with zero real relationship between `pre` and `post`. In one paper this exact pattern became the headline, title-level claim in a revision, and it took two independent reviewers in the same round to name it precisely (one showed `corr(pre, post) ≈ 0` while `corr(pre, reduction) = 0.86` on the same data) before it was caught. The fix — a permutation null that shuffles "after" independently of "before" and checks whether the observed correlation exceeds the shuffled distribution — is cheap and should be run automatically, before submission, on every reported before/after or pre/post correlation, not only after a reviewer objects. Note that even the permutation null itself needs a sanity check (simulate a true-null case and confirm the diagnostic's own false-positive rate is nominal) if a reviewer might argue "after" isn't a free variable but a deterministic function of "before."

- **When a correlation across a small number of groups/bins/conditions (not individual seeds) is significant only because of a large per-group n, check whether it survives dropping the single most extreme group before reporting it.** A width- or category-level trend that is monotonic-looking in a summary table can be entirely carried by one outlier cell — reviewers will ask "does this hold with that cell excluded?" almost reflexively when they see a non-monotonic pattern or a single small-effect group driving significance, and in one paper the answer (once checked) was that the correlation vanished (flipped sign, went non-significant) with the outlier dropped. Run this leave-one-group-out robustness check in the first draft, the same way you'd check leverage/influence points in a regression.

- **If a mechanism check varies your quantity of interest only by switching between categorical groups that are themselves confounded with other properties (e.g. inducing different average values of X by comparing different architecture sizes, or different experimental arms that differ on several dimensions at once), the check cannot distinguish "X causes the effect" from "being in that particular group causes the effect" — plan the deconfounded version in the first draft, not after reviewers name it.** Naming the confound in a limitations paragraph does not satisfy reviewers; multiple reviewers independently converging on "you can't tell whether it's the grouping variable or the thing you're claiming drives it" is a near-inevitable outcome once the check ships this way. The fix is to hold the grouping variable fixed and vary the quantity of interest through an orthogonal lever (e.g. training duration, sample size, noise level — something that isn't the categorical grouping itself). When you do have to switch levers to fix a confound, check and report whether the effect size is sensitive to which lever you used — reconciling two mechanism checks only by direction ("both point the same way") while their magnitudes differ by 2x or more is itself a new red flag reviewers will raise, not a clean resolution. **Refinement:** an observational fix like partial correlation, leave-one-out, or an extended parameter sweep can close a confound within the same revision cycle, but a genuinely causal mechanism claim (e.g. "underfitting drives the effect") needs an actual intervention — vary regularization/epochs/capacity directly at fixed width/group — not another correlational check on the same data. If a mechanistic claim is central to the paper's contribution, put the interventional manipulation in the original design/pre-registration; three straight rounds of reviewers asking for it and getting only more correlational analysis in response is a sign the fix is out of reach for the current experiment and needed to be planned before round 1.

- **Pre-register the sample size itself, not just the null hypothesis and stopping rule — run a power calculation before collecting data, and if the design is structurally underpowered, redesign it rather than caveat it.** In one paper, the confirmatory test's sample size was capped by definition (only seeds where a crossover existed at all qualified, yielding n=7 vs n=4), giving a minimum detectable effect so large (rank-biserial ~0.74) that the test could only ever catch a huge effect. Every reviewer in every one of 4 rounds flagged this, and it was never fixable post-hoc because the fix — a different test design or more seeds/conditions to enlarge the eligible n — had to be chosen before round 1. A pre-registered null (see above) prevents a post-hoc-easy test; a pre-registered power calculation prevents a foreseeably-underpowered one. Do both at design time.

- **Report a null/non-significant result together with its power analysis, not instead of it, and never use strong rejection language ("decisive," "rules out," "retires") for a weak or non-significant result.** "Not significant" and "no effect" are different claims, and p=1.00 on n=15 is evidence of an underpowered test, not evidence against the hypothesis. One paper's round 3 draft called a p=1.00 Fisher's test result "decisive enough to retire that explanation" — all four reviewers independently objected, and the fix (adopted in the next round) was softening to "consistent with X rather than a formal rejection of it." Write that hedged version in the first draft: compute effect size, CI, and minimum detectable effect, and match your verbs to what the numbers actually support.

- **If you extend a sample size because an initial test came back non-significant, that is sequential testing / optional stopping, and it must be disclosed and corrected for — not reported as if it were one uninterrupted test.** Peeking at n=15, seeing p>0.05, then extending to n=50 on the same seeds plus new ones and reporting the n=50 p-value as the headline result inflates the true false-positive rate even when every individual number is honestly computed and fully reproducible. Either pre-register the final n before looking, or explicitly frame a second look as confirmatory with alpha-spending / its own stated significance threshold. This is subtle enough that it can survive multiple rounds of otherwise-thorough statistical review before a reviewer catches it — decide the stopping rule before running the first test, not after seeing the first result.

- **When a paper accumulates many exploratory follow-up tests across revisions (mechanism checks, sensitivity sweeps, ablations, baseline comparisons), track the total test count and either correct for multiplicity or explicitly designate a small set as primary/protected claims — don't let the count grow passively.** A revision history with 6+ tests run across rounds, several only nominally significant (p just under 0.05), reads very differently once a reviewer adds them up. In practice, this is one of the few issues that reviewers have accepted resolving via transparent disclosure alone (explicitly stating in the text which results would not survive a correction) rather than requiring an actual correction — ratings stabilized rather than kept falling once this was disclosed honestly every round. But disclosure-only is the fallback, not the goal: decide your multiplicity policy (correct globally, protect a pre-specified small set, or disclose fully) at the point exploratory work begins, in round 1, not reactively each round.

- **Every statistic quoted in the abstract or main text must be persisted in the artifact/result file that computes it, not just hand-derivable by a reviewer from raw per-seed data — and every rounded figure must be checked against the unrounded value in that file.** If prose cites a specific p-value, accuracy, or other number, make sure the corresponding JSON/results file actually contains that computed value under a discoverable key, and that any rounding is honest (e.g. don't write "~0.94" when the file says 0.9248 — reviewers will recompute it from the raw results and catch the gap, as happened independently to two reviewers in one paper's round 3). An unsaved or over-rounded statistic is a reproducibility/trust gap even when the underlying number turns out to be qualitatively correct. **Sharper failure mode seen in a later paper:** it is possible for a quoted statistic to be numerically *correct* — all four reviewers independently reimplemented it and got the same number — and still fail this bar, because the released script never actually calls the function with the arguments needed to produce that exact reported value (e.g. a generic `partial_spearman()` helper existed and was called for one variable but never for the one actually quoted in prose). "Is this number right" and "does rerunning the released code actually emit this exact number" are two different checks; do both, explicitly, at submission time for every quoted statistic — 4/4 reviewers catching the same gap in the same round means this is a checklist item, not a corner case. **When a reviewer flags one wrong number inside a sentence with several numbers, recompute every number in that sentence from the same consistent data slice — not just the one that was named.** In one paper, a round-1 reviewer caught a statistic computed on a pooled slice (e.g. n=30) where the prose implied a narrower slice (e.g. n=15, baseline-only); the round-2 fix corrected three of four numbers in that sentence but left the fourth still computed from the pooled slice, and round-3 reviewers caught the identical class of error in the same sentence. A partial fix that satisfies the letter of the complaint but not its scope will be re-flagged.

- **Verify mechanism hypotheses directly instead of asserting plausibility, and when you do run the verifying ablation, check it doesn't contradict other evidence already in the paper.** If you speculate a cause for observed noise/variance (e.g. "probably binning artifacts," "probably dimensionality," "probably too-small validation set"), don't just state it — run the check that would confirm or falsify it, and afterward re-read the rest of the paper for pieces of evidence that cut the other way. In one paper, a late-round ablation supporting "dimensionality drives the effect" was added without reconciling it against an earlier real-dataset result that was higher-dimensional but showed the effect *less* reliably — a direct tension a reviewer caught in the final round because no one had cross-checked the new ablation against the old data point. A plausible-sounding but unverified (or internally inconsistent) mechanism claim is exactly the kind of thing that costs a full extra cycle. Running the check even when you expect to be right pays off directly: in one paper the authors speculated a training-instability effect was a small-validation-set artifact, ran a dedicated ablation with much more validation data, found the effect did *not* shrink, and reported that refutation of their own guess plainly instead of quietly dropping the speculation — reviewers treat this kind of self-refutation as evidence of rigor, not as a weaker paper.

- **When reporting a "before vs. after" comparison (an intervention, a fix, a calibration step), verify both numbers come from the literal same trained model/run, not from two separately-retrained populations that merely share a nominal seed label.** A script that calls the RNG-seeding function once per seed and then trains many conditions sequentially (multiple widths × multiple settings) does NOT give each individual condition an independently reproducible model — the RNG state at the point a given condition is reached depends on everything trained before it in that loop. A later follow-up script that reseeds immediately before training just that one condition in isolation produces a *different* model, so splicing its "after" number onto the original script's "before" number silently compares two different sets of weights. Always seed immediately before each individually-reported unit of training, and if two numbers in the same table row come from different scripts/files, explicitly check they refer to the same trained artifact before pairing them.

- **Apply the same verification rigor to every quantified claim in the abstract, not just the one under active reviewer scrutiny.** In one paper's final round, an easily-falsifiable claim ("hidden≥32 is overconfident in all 10 seeds") sat unchecked in the abstract for two full revision rounds — while a different headline claim received binomial tests, bootstraps, and dual nulls — and turned out to be false by the paper's own results file (two seeds reverted to underconfident at large widths). Before finalizing, grep/recompute every absolute quantifier ("all," "every," "always," "never") in the abstract and discussion against the actual results file. **This extends beyond quantifiers to plain descriptive claims about your own numbers:** a claim like "these two conditions differ by only a few points" is just as checkable as an "all/never" claim, and just as likely to be wrong if never verified — in one paper's round 1, the single lowest-scoring reviewer (5 vs. three 7s) was the one who actually computed the group means from `raw_results.json` and found a supposedly-small gap was actually 31 points. Before submission, compute every descriptive comparison quoted in prose ("roughly constant," "only a few points," "no strong dependence") from the raw data yourself — don't let a plausible-sounding qualitative description go unchecked just because it isn't a formal statistical claim.

- **Before finalizing an abstract-level claim sentence, check whether it would still be justified if you excluded the single weakest supporting data point.** All four round-1 reviewers of one paper independently flagged, without prompting from each other, that the abstract's claim was stronger/more general than the underlying results table actually showed (a weak, non-monotonic correlation dressed up as "X increases significantly with Y"). This is a highly reliable signal: when four reviewers with different focus areas converge on the same framing complaint in round 1, it means the abstract oversold relative to the data, not that one reviewer had a pet peeve. A first-draft self-check: read only the abstract's claim sentences, then check whether the *weakest* supporting condition in your results table would, alone, still justify that sentence. If not, hedge before submission.

- **Small held-out splits (a few hundred examples) make point estimates and post-hoc fits (temperature scaling, threshold/crossover locations) noisy enough to change qualitative conclusions.** State this early and quantify it (bootstrap variance) rather than only noting it qualitatively in Limitations — reviewers consistently asked for exactly this and rewarded it when eventually provided.

- **Any warning your training/analysis pipeline emits at runtime (convergence warnings, deprecation notices, numerical-instability warnings) is a plausible confound and should be tabulated and reported proactively in the first draft, not left for a reviewer to discover by rerunning your code.** In one paper, a round-1 reviewer noticed `ConvergenceWarning` messages on rerun that the paper never mentioned, despite them correlating with the very variable (width) the paper's headline effect depended on — this became a full mechanism-check section added in round 2 ("2/210 runs non-convergent, both at small width") that could have shipped in v1 for free, since the information was already sitting in stderr the whole time the experiment was being written up. Capture and summarize stderr/warnings output as part of your own results logging, not just stdout metrics. **When you then "fix" the warning by changing a parameter (e.g. raising `max_iter` until it disappears), identify exactly which dimension the reviewer's concern is really about before you patch — silencing the symptom can swap in a worse, different confound.** In another paper, a cross-library spot check raised `max_iter` to kill a `ConvergenceWarning`, which let one library train to full convergence while the original experiment used a fixed, matched epoch budget — "train fully" vs. "train for N epochs" is itself a different experimental condition, and this reversed the paper's headline effect. The real fix was to match the audited dimension (epoch budget) directly, not to remove the warning.

- **Full, exact reproducibility (code + raw logs/result JSONs that regenerate every reported number) is consistently and heavily rewarded by reviewers, across every round.** It has been the single most-praised property across papers in this series, and papers that start from these lessons (fixed seeding, pre-registered nulls, power analyses, mechanism verification already in v1) now reliably clear round 1 with zero reproducibility complaints. Always ship runnable code alongside raw output files, and make sure every number quoted in prose/tables can be regenerated from them — but also make sure the regeneration is deterministic (see the `hash()`-seeding lesson above); reproducible-but-wrong is worse than no reproducibility claim at all.

- **When you add a new condition/dataset/analysis late in the revision process, extend the existing general-purpose verification script to cover it rather than writing a new disposable one-off script.** In one paper, a reviewer discovered that the script computing the paper's main diagnostic only covered the original datasets, and a late-added dataset's corresponding number came from an ad hoc script never checked into the released code — meaning the paper's "we release all code" claim was literally false for its newest, most-scrutinized result, even though the reviewer's independent reimplementation confirmed the number was correct. Treat every late addition as a refactor task for the shared pipeline, not a standalone script, so "full code release" stays true through the last revision.

- **Honest, substantive walk-backs of overprecise claims (rather than defending them or patching cosmetically) are recognized and rewarded by reviewers as "the correct epistemic move," and this pays off even more when done pre-emptively in the abstract of v1 rather than only after a review round forces it.** When a review round shows a headline claim doesn't hold up, downgrade the claim's precision explicitly in the text (state what changed and why) rather than quietly softening the language or arguing the original framing was fine. One paper hedged its central mechanistic claim in the abstract from v1 onward ("we do not claim to have located that turning point") and was praised for exactly this framing in every single round with zero overclaiming complaints — the technique works prophylactically, not just as damage control.

- **If a headline null result ("intervention X adds no benefit beyond Y") depends on one specific hyperparameter setting, test at least one other setting of that hyperparameter before generalizing the null — otherwise scope the claim explicitly to the tested value.** In one paper, a null finding held at one setting of a tunable parameter but reversed at another; adding that single extra data point let the authors correctly narrow an overclaimed "adds nothing" to a scoped, value-specific null, and reviewers credited the narrowing directly. Treat "does this null survive a second setting of its own governing knob" as a first-draft check, not a round-2 patch.

- **When a phenomenon looks driven by a continuous covariate, test whether it's actually category/condition-specific by finding a matched pair across categories at the same value of that covariate — this is a cheap, decisive way to falsify an overly clean mechanistic story before a reviewer forces you to.** In one paper, two different conditions were matched to nearly identical values of the suspected driving covariate, and the outcome diverged sharply between them — correctly demoting a claimed universal threshold effect to a narrower, condition-dependent, honestly-labeled open question instead of a tidy, force-fit explanation.

- **Disclosing revision-driven corrections explicitly in the abstract/intro of the next draft (not just fixing them silently in the body) is treated by reviewers as a positive transparency signal, not an admission of weakness.** In one paper, later drafts stated outright that specific numbers were corrected "in response to reviewer-verified discrepancies," and reviewers repeatedly called out this transparency as a strength across multiple rounds even while still finding narrower issues elsewhere. Prefer an explicit "this number changed because a reviewer caught X" note over quietly updating the value.

- **Pre-registration only protects the specific hypotheses you actually registered — if the paper's real headline claim turns out to be a downstream exploratory finding (which is common once you've seen your own data), it needs the same battery of robustness checks (nulls, deconfounding, replication) as a confirmatory test, not a free pass because pre-registration happened somewhere else in the paper.** In one paper, the pre-registered H1/H2 tests were praised, but the claim reviewers actually spent the most scrutiny on across three rounds was an exploratory correlation layered on top, which the paper's own framing didn't initially subject to the same scrutiny. If an exploratory finding is promoted to the headline/title, immediately give it a dedicated robustness pass (shuffle nulls, leave-one-out checks, deconfounding) in the same draft you promote it, rather than relying on the rigor already spent on the originally pre-registered hypotheses.

- **If the title or abstract makes a generality claim ("generalizes across datasets," "holds across architectures"), decide at round 1 which causal axes reviewers will ask about, and run independent manipulations of each of them up front — don't let the paper discover its own confounds one at a time, one review round apiece.** A title promising cross-dataset generality but backed by one real dataset plus variants of one synthetic generator family, on one architecture, was flagged by every reviewer in every round (16/16 reviews) and never resolved because fixing it meant adding a second dataset/architecture, a round-1 decision. Worse, when reviewers asked "is it difficulty?" (round 2) and the author added a difficulty ablation, the *next* round's reviewers immediately asked "then is it dimensionality?" — each fix satisfied that round but revealed the next confound, costing a full round per axis. Before round 1, list the 2-3 most obvious alternative explanations a skeptical reviewer would propose for your effect, and run a manipulation isolating each one in the first draft, rather than tightening scope language or chasing confounds reactively. Relatedly: don't lean on a single additional dataset/condition as the sole tie-breaker for a generalization claim, especially one your own text already describes as a weak arbiter (too small, too easy, degenerate) — a lone tie-breaker you already distrust will be called out by multiple reviewers as thin, and it takes an independent *second* corroborating condition, not just one, to actually settle the question. **The implementation/library stack is itself a generality axis, and reviewers treat it as load-bearing even when the paper varies other axes extensively.** In another paper, the authors ran 300+ experiments sweeping datasets and width but used a single ML framework/solver (e.g. one library's default optimizer) throughout; starting in round 2, reviewers repeatedly asked for even one spot-check in a different framework/optimizer to rule out an implementation-specific artifact, calling it "cheap" and "inexpensive given existing infrastructure" — and by round 4 all four reviewers were repeating nearly identical language because it still hadn't been done. Treat "which library/solver/optimizer" as a confound axis worth a single cheap spot-check in the first draft, on the same footing as dataset or architecture diversity, since reviewers evidently do.

- **Single-dataset, single-architecture (or single-framework/optimizer) empirical studies plateau around a 5-7 rating even when extremely well-executed and statistically rigorous — and this ceiling can hold rock-steady across every revision round even as real, reviewer-acknowledged bugs get fixed.** Reviewers consistently cap novelty/generality scores for narrow scope regardless of how much statistical rigor is added on top, and broad claims in the abstract/title will repeatedly be flagged as reading more general than a single-setup result can license. This ceiling held in a second paper as well: ratings stayed flat at 7/7/7/7 across all four rounds despite substantive fixes landing each round (a confound reversal via partial correlation, a matched training-budget correction, a scoped null, a matched-pair falsification test) — the fixes were credited individually, but scope (one architecture family, one dataset per condition) was never expanded, so the ceiling never moved. **Diagnostic for future papers:** if the same category of weakness is echoed near-verbatim by reviewers across 3+ consecutive rounds despite unrelated fixes landing and being credited, that weakness is design-time and no further post-hoc analysis in the current revision cycle will move the score — the fix belongs in the next paper's initial design (more datasets/architectures/solvers chosen in round 1), not in another round of patches on this one. Either scope the claims' language tightly to what was actually tested from the first draft, or budget for a second dataset/architecture/implementation if a higher ceiling is wanted.

- **Not all reviewer criticisms are equally fixable within a revision cycle.** Methodology/implementation bugs (bad seeding, unverified mechanism claims, over-rounded numbers, overclaiming language, uncaught before/after mismatches, unchecked descriptive prose claims, missing runtime-warning audits, a results function not being called with the right arguments, a confound patched by changing the wrong parameter, a sentence with only some of its numbers corrected) are typically fixed for real within one round once flagged. Design-time decisions — final scope/generalizability (including which libraries/solvers/frameworks were used, not just which datasets/architectures), the statistical testing/stopping plan, sample-size power, and how a mechanism check induces variation in its variable of interest (confounded grouping vs. orthogonal lever, or correlational vs. truly interventional) — tend to just get re-flagged or hedged with "future work" / disclosure language across every remaining round, because the fix requires a decision made before round 1, not a patch after the fact. When starting a new paper, spend extra first-draft effort specifically on these categories: pick the final scope (including implementation stack diversity), the full statistical testing/stopping plan, a power calculation for the sample size, and a deconfounded *and*, if the claim is causal, truly interventional mechanism-check design, all up front — since they are the ones later rounds cannot cheaply repair.
