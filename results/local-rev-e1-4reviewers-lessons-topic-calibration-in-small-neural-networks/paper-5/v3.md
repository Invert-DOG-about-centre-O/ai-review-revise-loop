# Calibration in Small Neural Networks: Temperature Scaling Dominates Label Smoothing (at ε=0.1), But Can Backfire Near Chance

## Abstract

The literature on neural network calibration is built almost entirely on large, overparameterized models, where the dominant finding is that bigger models are more overconfident and that temperature scaling (TS) and label smoothing (LS) both help. We ask whether this picture holds when "small" means genuinely small — single-hidden-layer MLPs with widths from 2 to 256 — rather than "small by modern deep-learning standards." Across two datasets (sklearn digits, a synthetic 4-class blobs task with label noise), 8 widths, 2 training conditions (plain cross-entropy vs. label smoothing, ε=0.1), and 15 seeds per cell (480 trained models total), we test two pre-registered hypotheses. First, that raw expected calibration error (ECE) increases with width, replicating the large-model literature: this does **not** hold cleanly at the raw correlation level (Spearman ρ from −0.14 to +0.30 across cells) because width and accuracy are confounded — controlling for accuracy, width has a robust *negative* partial correlation with raw ECE in all four cells (partial ρ −0.20 to −0.51, all p<0.03), and this survives dropping the two lowest widths entirely (partial ρ −0.57 to −0.76, all p<1e-4): at matched accuracy, wider is *better* calibrated, opposite the naive extrapolation. Second, that after TS, label smoothing at ε=0.1 confers no additional benefit over plain training: we find no evidence against this null (paired Wilcoxon, n=240, p=0.86). Critically, this null is **ε-specific**: a follow-up check at ε=0.3 on digits (same seeds, same pairing) finds LS *does* beat baseline post-TS (n=120 pairs, mean ECE reduction 0.033, p=0.014) — so "label smoothing adds nothing beyond TS" should not be read as true for label smoothing in general. Third, TS itself is unreliable for very narrow, near-chance-accuracy networks: it *increases* ECE in 31% of all 480 runs, rising to 42% among runs with accuracy below 0.4. A validation-size ablation shows this is not primarily a small-validation-set artifact — enlarging the validation set 6x at width 2 does not shrink the instability. But it is also **not a generic "near chance" phenomenon**: at blobs/width=2 (accuracy 0.31, near the 0.25 chance level) TS is stable and helpful (14/15 runs improve), while at digits/width=4, matched to the *same* absolute accuracy (0.31), TS is unstable (8/15 runs worsen) — the effect tracks something dataset-specific (plausibly digits' 10-way, 64-dimensional structure), not accuracy alone. Finally, we re-ran our cross-library spot check (scikit-learn's `MLPClassifier`) after fixing a convergence problem flagged in review (raising `max_iter` from 400 to 3000, eliminating all 10 prior `ConvergenceWarning`s, n increased from 15 to 45): once the models actually converge, the direction **reverses** — TS mildly but significantly *increases* ECE in this check (0.030→0.034, p=0.018) — though the converged sklearn models reach far higher accuracy at narrow widths than our fixed-120-epoch PyTorch models do, so this is not a like-for-like replication and should not be read as contradicting the main study. We release all code and raw per-run results.

## 1. Introduction

Guo et al. (2017) showed that modern deep networks are frequently overconfident and that a single learned temperature parameter, fit post-hoc on a validation set, is a remarkably effective fix. A large follow-up literature examines *why* miscalibration grows with capacity and proposes training-time alternatives such as label smoothing (Müller et al., 2019). Minderer et al. (2021) revisited these findings for newer architectures, largely confirming that capacity growth worsens raw calibration.

**Gap.** Essentially all of this literature studies networks with widths in the hundreds to thousands. Genuinely small networks — the kind used in resource-constrained or tabular-data settings, where a hidden layer of width 8–64 is a real design point — are comparatively unstudied for calibration. It is not obvious that trends from the overparameterized regime extrapolate down to networks this narrow, where the model may not reliably beat chance at the low end of the width sweep, confounding width with accuracy in a way that does not arise for already-well-trained large models.

**This paper.** We run a controlled width sweep (2–256 hidden units) crossed with training condition on two datasets, 15 seeds per cell, and ask three questions: (1) does raw ECE increase with width, or is any such trend an accuracy artifact; (2) once TS is applied, does LS still help, and is that answer sensitive to the smoothing strength; (3) is TS itself reliable across the width range, including near chance, and is "near chance" itself well-defined across datasets. H1 and H2 (at ε=0.1) are pre-registered before running the analysis (fixed in `analyze.py`'s docstring). Everything else — the accuracy-confound partial correlation and its robustness to dropping low widths, the ε=0.3 sensitivity check, the validation-size ablation, the second near-chance dataset/width comparison, and the re-run cross-library check — are exploratory, directly-verified follow-ups, several motivated by this paper's own review process, and are reported as such, not as confirmatory tests.

### Related work
- **Guo et al. (2017)**: capacity correlates with overconfidence; TS is a strong post-hoc fix. Our width range (2–256) sits below essentially all settings studied there.
- **Müller et al. (2019)**: LS improves calibration in large models via tighter logit clusters. We ask whether this survives a post-hoc fix, and at what smoothing strength, at much smaller capacity.
- **Minderer et al. (2021)**: revisits calibration trends for newer architectures, again overparameterized.

## 2. Method

**Datasets.** (a) `digits` (sklearn): 1797 samples, 64 features, 10 classes. (b) `blobs`: synthetic 4-class task (`make_classification`, 1500 samples, 12 features, 5% label flip noise). 60/20/20 train/val/test, stratified, split controlled by the run's seed.

**Models.** Single-hidden-layer MLPs (Linear→ReLU→Linear), widths ∈ {2,4,8,16,32,64,128,256}, Adam (lr=0.001), 120 full-batch epochs. Two main conditions: plain cross-entropy ("baseline") and label-smoothed cross-entropy (ε=0.1, "label_smooth"); a third, ε=0.3, is used only for the targeted sensitivity check on digits.

**Seeding.** Every (dataset, condition, width, seed-index) cell gets a seed from fixed integer arithmetic (no `hash()`), so reruns are bit-identical. 15 seeds/cell × 2 datasets × 2 main conditions × 8 widths = 480 models.

**Calibration metric.** ECE with 15 equal-width bins, plus NLL and accuracy, before ("raw") and after ("ts") temperature scaling (T fit by minimizing validation NLL via LBFGS, per trained model).

**Pre-registered tests** (fixed before inspecting `raw_results.json`):
- **H1** (one-sided): raw ECE increases with width. Spearman correlation, pooled over seeds, per (dataset × condition) cell (n=120 each).
- **H2** (two-sided, ε=0.1): after TS, LS makes no difference to ECE vs. baseline. Paired Wilcoxon on (ECE_ts,baseline − ECE_ts,label_smooth), paired by (dataset, width, seed_idx). We report rank-biserial effect size, a bootstrap 95% CI, and a simulation-based power analysis.

**Follow-up checks (post-hoc, directly verified, not confirmatory).**
1. *Width–accuracy confound.* Partial Spearman correlation of width vs. raw ECE controlling for accuracy (rank-residual method), per cell — both on the full width range and after dropping widths 2 and 4, to check the effect isn't driven solely by the most extreme, noisiest low-accuracy cells.
2. *Label-smoothing strength.* We retrain digits/label_smooth at ε=0.3 (same seeds, widths, splits as the main study) and paired-compare its post-TS ECE against the main study's digits/baseline post-TS ECE, to see whether the ε=0.1 null generalizes to stronger smoothing.
3. *Mechanism check for TS instability near chance.* We hold the training split fixed at 60% and vary the validation share of the remaining 40% (n_val ≈ 89, 179, 359, 539) at digits width 2 (near-chance) and width 16 (good accuracy), 20 seeds each, tracking the SD of fitted temperature and post-TS ECE as a function of validation size.
4. *Does "near chance" generalize across datasets?* We compare TS behavior at digits/width=4 and blobs/width=2, two cells whose mean test accuracy is almost identical (≈0.31), to check whether instability is an accuracy-level effect or something dataset-specific.

**Cross-library spot check.** scikit-learn's `MLPClassifier` (digits, widths {2,32,256}, disjoint seeds) checks whether TS-reduces-ECE replicates directionally on a different backend/optimizer. Our first pass (`max_iter=400`, n=15) hit 10/15 `ConvergenceWarning`s and was flagged as such in review; we re-ran it with `max_iter=3000` and `n=45` (15 seeds/width) to remove the convergence confound.

All code and raw per-run JSON are included (`run_experiment.py`, `analyze.py`, `accuracy_control_check.py`, `val_size_ablation.py`, `per_seed_inverse_u_check.py`, `spotcheck_sklearn.py`, `eps_sensitivity_check.py`, plus `*_results.json` outputs).

## 3. Results

**H1 — the raw width–ECE trend does not replicate cleanly, and is entangled with accuracy.** Spearman ρ (n=120/cell): blobs/baseline ρ=−0.14 (p=0.94), blobs/label_smooth ρ=+0.30 (p=0.0004), digits/baseline ρ=0.00 (p=0.49), digits/label_smooth ρ=+0.12 (p=0.09). Only 1 of 4 cells is significant. On digits/baseline, raw ECE rises from width 2 (0.081) to a peak at width 16 (0.491, accuracy 0.75), then falls through widths 32–256 (0.464→0.093, accuracy 0.88→0.95) — an inverse-U that holds in 15/15 individual seeds, not just on average.

Controlling for accuracy, the partial Spearman correlation of width vs. raw ECE is **negative and significant in all four cells** (blobs/baseline −0.51, p=4e-9; blobs/label_smooth −0.46, p=1e-7; digits/baseline −0.21, p=0.02; digits/label_smooth −0.20, p=0.03). This is not an artifact of the noisiest, most extreme low-accuracy widths: dropping widths 2 and 4 and recomputing on the remaining n=90/cell only *strengthens* the effect (blobs/baseline −0.76, blobs/label_smooth −0.75, digits/baseline −0.57, digits/label_smooth −0.57, all p<1e-4). At matched accuracy, wider networks in this range are *better*, not worse, calibrated — the raw width→ECE relationship in this regime is mostly an accuracy→ECE relationship, and the residual, accuracy-controlled width effect favors wider networks.

**H2 — at ε=0.1, label smoothing adds nothing once TS is applied, but this is specific to ε=0.1.** Paired Wilcoxon on post-TS ECE (n=240): mean diff (baseline−LS) = −0.0020, p=0.86, rank-biserial = −0.014 — a coin flip (119/240 vs. 121/240). Power analysis (matched to observed SD 0.214) shows 80% power to detect a mean difference of 0.040 (≈0.19 SD), so this null is not underpowered. However, retraining digits/label_smooth at **ε=0.3** and pairing against the same baseline (n=120 pairs, same seeds/splits) finds a real difference: mean post-TS ECE 0.141 (baseline) vs. 0.109 (ε=0.3), a reduction of 0.033, Wilcoxon p=0.014. So "label smoothing confers no additional benefit beyond TS" holds at ε=0.1 in this regime but does **not** generalize to stronger smoothing — the ε=0.1 null is a fact about that specific hyperparameter, not about label smoothing as a method.

**Descriptive: TS reduces ECE overall, but not uniformly.** Baseline: 0.187→0.174 (p=0.0017); label_smooth (ε=0.1): 0.216→0.176 (p=0.0014).

**Exploratory: TS is unreliable near chance — but "near chance" is not a universal, accuracy-only threshold.** TS increases ECE in 149/480 runs (31%), rising to 42/100 (42%) among accuracy<0.4 runs. At digits width 2 (mean accuracy 0.19, n=15), fitted temperature has mean 0.78, SD 1.04; post-TS ECE has mean 0.390, SD 0.361 (raw ECE, by contrast, is stable: mean 0.076, SD 0.041). [We note these figures supersede a prior draft's descriptive stats for this cell, which contained a computation error caught in review; the qualitative conclusion is unchanged.] The validation-size ablation refutes a "just enlarge validation set" fix: at width 2, temperature SD across 6x more validation data (n_val 89→539) is 0.47, 0.56, 0.57, then 1.03 — it does not shrink — while at width 16 (accuracy ≈0.78) temperature SD is low and stable at every level (0.07–0.09), regardless of validation size.

But the instability is not simply a function of low accuracy in general. At blobs/width=2 (accuracy 0.31, near the 4-class chance level of 0.25), TS is stable and helpful: post-TS ECE mean 0.047 vs. raw ECE mean 0.087, and only 1/15 runs get worse. At digits/width=4, matched almost exactly to that same absolute accuracy (0.31), TS is unstable: post-TS ECE mean 0.394, and 8/15 runs get worse. Two cells with essentially identical test accuracy show opposite TS behavior, so accuracy alone does not determine when TS is trustworthy; something about digits specifically (its higher dimensionality, 10-way rather than 4-way structure, or ECE-bin sparsity at low accuracy) interacts with the failure mode. We flag this as an open mechanistic question rather than resolve it here.

**Cross-library spot check, corrected.** The original check (`max_iter=400`, n=15) reproduced the main study's direction (0.062→0.035, p=0.095) but 10/15 runs hit `ConvergenceWarning`, a limitation flagged independently by multiple reviewers. Raising `max_iter` to 3000 and `n` to 45 eliminates all convergence warnings, but **reverses the finding**: raw ECE 0.030 → post-TS ECE 0.034, Wilcoxon p=0.018 — TS mildly but significantly *increases* ECE once these sklearn models are actually trained to convergence. This is not directly comparable to the main study, however: the converged sklearn models reach much higher accuracy at width 2 (mean ≈0.68 across widened seeds, vs. 0.19 for our fixed-120-epoch PyTorch models at the same width) because they are not epoch-budget-matched. We read this as showing that our main finding is specific to models trained under a fixed, modest epoch budget rather than to convergence, and that the original "confirmatory" spot check's apparent agreement was partly an artifact of its own non-convergence. This weakens, rather than strengthens, any claim that TS-reduces-ECE is implementation-invariant, and we report it plainly as a limitation rather than omit it.

## 4. Conclusion

In the genuinely small-network regime (widths 2–256), the standard calibration narrative only partially transfers, and several findings that looked clean on a first pass turn out to have real boundary conditions once probed. The raw "capacity increases miscalibration" trend does not replicate and is confounded with accuracy; controlling for accuracy — and even after removing the lowest, noisiest widths — the residual width effect on raw ECE is negative in every cell, wider is better calibrated at matched accuracy. Label smoothing at ε=0.1 adds nothing detectable beyond TS, but a stronger value (ε=0.3) does help, so this null does not extend to label smoothing in general. TS itself measurably backfires for near-chance networks, and enlarging the validation set does not fix it — but "near chance" is not a portable, accuracy-only threshold: two cells at matched accuracy (digits/width=4, blobs/width=2) show opposite TS reliability, so accuracy is not sufficient to predict when TS is trustworthy. Finally, a corrected cross-library check (fixing a convergence issue flagged in review) reverses direction rather than confirming the main effect, once training-regime differences are accounted for; we treat this as an important caveat on external validity rather than paper over.

**Limitations.** One architecture family (single-hidden-layer ReLU MLPs); the corrected cross-library check now points *against*, not for, implementation-invariance of the core TS effect, and is confounded with training-regime (fixed-epoch vs. converged) differences we did not disentangle. Both datasets are small (≤1800 samples). The ε=0.1 vs. ε=0.3 comparison is limited to one dataset (digits) and one width range; we do not have a full ε sweep. The dataset-specificity of the near-chance TS failure (digits but not blobs, at matched accuracy) is documented but not mechanistically explained. Other calibration methods (MMCE, Dirichlet calibration, focal loss) remain outside this paper's scope.
