# Pre-Calibration ECE, Not Width, Predicts Whether Temperature Scaling Helps Small Networks

## Abstract

Guo et al. (2017) showed that large, modern deep networks tend to be overconfident and that temperature scaling reliably fixes this. Much less is known about the small end of the capacity spectrum. We pre-registered two questions in one-hidden-layer MLPs (widths 4–256) trained on sklearn digits (10 classes) and a synthetic nonlinear 3-class problem, 20 fixed-seed repeats per width per dataset (n=140/dataset): (1) does the post-hoc optimal temperature T* rise monotonically with width even in this small-capacity regime, and (2) does temperature scaling actually reduce expected calibration error (ECE) here? T* rises significantly with width on both datasets (Spearman ρ=0.27, p=5.5×10⁻⁴ on digits; ρ=0.93, p=1.9×10⁻⁶² on synthetic; Bonferroni-significant). But a follow-up check we ran after the confirmatory analyses shows the digits correlation is not a genuine width gradient: it is driven entirely by width 4 as an outlier — excluding width 4, ρ drops to −0.01 (p=0.53, n.s.). We therefore added a third, exploratory real dataset (sklearn breast_cancer, n=140 runs) to check which pattern is representative: there, the width–T* relationship survives dropping width 4 (ρ=0.36, p=2.6×10⁻⁵), matching synthetic's pattern rather than digits'. Calibration's practical benefit is regime-dependent, but the better predictor is not width — it is pre-calibration ECE. Across all three datasets, pre-ECE correlates positively with the ECE reduction from calibration (digits ρ=0.45, p=2.0×10⁻⁸; synthetic ρ=0.86, p=4.4×10⁻⁴³; breast_cancer ρ=0.47, p=3.7×10⁻⁹): already-well-calibrated small networks are the ones calibration is prone to hurt, regardless of width. A multi-model mechanism check (5 independently trained models per width, bootstrap-varying calibration-set size) confirms that reducing calibration-set-size noise does not rescue calibration for already-well-calibrated widths — the fraction of resamples in which scaling makes ECE worse than doing nothing stays high (76–89%) even at 240 calibration points, while it drops substantially (52%→30%) for the one poorly-calibrated width tested. We release all code, raw results, and the exploratory follow-ups.

## 1. Introduction

Modern deep networks are frequently overconfident, and Guo et al. (2017) popularized temperature scaling — dividing logits by a single scalar T fit on a held-out set — as a cheap, effective fix, evaluated on large architectures. Small networks (tens to low-hundreds of hidden units), common in resource-constrained and tabular deployments, are comparatively understudied.

**Q1 — Does the capacity–overconfidence relationship extend into the small-width regime?** **Q2 — Does post-hoc temperature scaling reliably help small networks?** Practical guidance almost universally recommends temperature scaling as a safe default, but its calibration set is often small in exactly the settings where small networks are deployed.

### Related work

Guo et al. (2017) established the modern baseline. Follow-up work proposes adaptive/margin-aware recalibration variants, generally on large vision/language models; recent surveys note current large models sometimes trend toward *under*confidence, complicating any simple "bigger = more overconfident" story. To our knowledge no prior study isolates the small-width end of the capacity axis with a pre-registered, multi-seed, multi-dataset design, nor asks whether pre-calibration miscalibration level — rather than width — is the operative predictor of calibration's benefit.

### Contributions

1. A pre-registered, fixed-seed, 20-seed × 7-width × 2-dataset study of width vs. T*, plus a third exploratory real dataset added specifically to adjudicate whether an apparent width effect is genuine or an outlier artifact.
2. A pre-registered test showing temperature scaling does not reliably reduce ECE for small networks, and a follow-up analysis (run after the confirmatory results, across all three datasets) showing **pre-calibration ECE, not width, is the consistent predictor** of whether calibration helps or hurts.
3. A mechanism check, now using 5 independently trained models per width (not 1), showing calibration-set-size noise reduction does not rescue scaling for already-well-calibrated widths.

## 2. Method

**Datasets.** (a) *digits*: sklearn's 1797-sample, 10-class digit dataset. (b) *synthetic*: 3000-sample, 3-class `make_classification` (20 features, flip_y=0.03). (c) *breast_cancer* (added exploratory, post-hoc): sklearn's 569-sample, 2-class real dataset, run with the identical pipeline, used only to check whether the digits width–T* pattern or the synthetic pattern is more representative of real data.

**Model.** One-hidden-layer MLP (`sklearn.neural_network.MLPClassifier`, ReLU, Adam, weight decay 1e-4, 300 iterations), widths ∈ {4,8,16,32,64,128,256}. Features standardized on train-split statistics.

**Splits and seeding.** 60/20/20 train/calibration/test, stratified. Every (dataset, width, seed-index) run gets an explicit integer seed from a fixed offset table (never `hash()`), reseeded before its own split/fit. 20 seeds × 7 widths × 2 datasets = n=140/dataset for the confirmatory experiments; breast_cancer reuses the identical seeding scheme with a distinct dataset offset, also n=140.

**Calibration.** T* minimizes NLL on the calibration split (`scipy.optimize.minimize_scalar`, bounds [0.05,20]) using `log(softmax_probs)` as pseudo-logits (exact, since softmax is shift-invariant). ECE uses 15 equal-width bins.

**Pre-registered confirmatory analyses (digits, synthetic only).** H1: one-sided Spearman(log₂ width, T*) per dataset, Bonferroni α=0.025. H2: one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank, pre-ECE > post-ECE, per dataset.

**Exploratory follow-ups (run after seeing the confirmatory results; not part of the pre-registered family).** (i) Re-running H1's correlation excluding width 4, on all datasets, after noticing digits' per-width T* means are non-monotonic and width-4 is the only sub-1.0 cell. (ii) Adding breast_cancer with the identical pipeline to check which pattern (digits-like or synthetic-like) generalizes. (iii) Spearman(pre-ECE, ECE reduction) and pre-ECE tercile breakdowns on all three datasets, to test whether pre-ECE is a better predictor of H2's outcome than width. (iv) A mechanism check extending the original single-model-per-width bootstrap (widths {4,16,64}, calibration sizes {30,60,120,240}, 200 draws) to 5 independently trained models per width (100 draws/cell/model) to separate calibration-set-size effects from single-model idiosyncrasy.

All code and raw outputs (`experiment.py`, `experiment_dataset3.py`, `experiment_mechanism.py`, `experiment_mechanism_v2.py`, `quick_analysis.py`, and corresponding `results_*.json`) are in this directory.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Width vs. optimal temperature (H1) — and why digits' correlation is misleading

| Dataset | ρ (all widths) | p | ρ (excl. width 4) | p |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| digits | 0.273 | 5.5×10⁻⁴ | **−0.008** | 0.53 (n.s.) |
| synthetic | 0.931 | 1.9×10⁻⁶² | 0.937 | 4.8×10⁻⁵⁶ |
| breast_cancer (exploratory) | 0.451 | 1.1×10⁻⁸ | 0.360 | 2.6×10⁻⁵ |

Digits' significant H1 result is **not a genuine width gradient**: per-width mean T* is 0.87, 1.10, 1.12, 1.20, 1.12, 1.03, 1.19 for widths 4→256 — non-monotonic across widths 8–256, and width 4 is the only cell with mean T*<1. Dropping width 4 collapses the correlation to essentially zero. This is exactly the failure mode reviewers flagged: a significant p-value from n=140 can reflect one outlier cell rather than a trend. Synthetic's correlation is unaffected by dropping width 4 (still ρ≈0.94) — a genuine, strong gradient. To check which pattern is more typical of real data, we ran the identical pipeline on breast_cancer: its width–T* correlation *survives* dropping width 4 (ρ=0.36, p=2.6×10⁻⁵), siding with synthetic. We now read the width-extends-into-small-networks claim as supported on 2 of 3 datasets tested, with digits as a specific counterexample rather than the norm — a more conservative and more accurate claim than v1's pooled framing.

### 3.2 Does temperature scaling help? Pre-ECE beats width as the predictor

| Dataset | Mean pre-ECE | Mean post-ECE | Wilcoxon p (pre>post) |
|---|---|---|---|
| digits | 0.0276 | 0.0309 | 0.99995 (no improvement) |
| synthetic | 0.0589 | 0.0441 | 2.2×10⁻⁸ (significant) |
| breast_cancer (exploratory) | 0.0316 | 0.0312 | 0.478 (no improvement) |

Two of three datasets show no net benefit from calibration. Reviewers independently asked whether this asymmetry is really about width, or about how miscalibrated the model already is pre-scaling. We tested this directly: Spearman(pre-ECE, pre-ECE − post-ECE) is positive and significant on **all three** datasets (digits ρ=0.45, p=2.0×10⁻⁸; synthetic ρ=0.86, p=4.4×10⁻⁴³; breast_cancer ρ=0.47, p=3.7×10⁻⁹) — the more miscalibrated a model already is, the more scaling helps it, essentially independent of dataset identity. On digits, Spearman(width, pre-ECE − post-ECE) is *negative* (ρ=−0.24, p=0.005): width predicts *less* benefit, the opposite sign from the naive "wider → needs more correction" intuition, because on digits pre-ECE happens to *fall* with width. Splitting digits by pre-ECE tercile makes this concrete: the top tercile (mean pre-ECE=0.048) shows a marginal improvement trend (Wilcoxon p=0.058) while the bottom two show none. On synthetic, the top pre-ECE tercile improves sharply (p=3.6×10⁻¹⁴) and the bottom tercile does not (p=0.9995). **Pre-calibration ECE, not width, is the variable that actually predicts whether temperature scaling helps** — width only matters insofar as it happens to correlate with pre-ECE in a given dataset, and that correlation's sign is not fixed (positive on synthetic, negative on digits).

### 3.3 Mechanism check: calibration-set size, now across 5 models per width

Extending the original single-model bootstrap to 5 independently trained digits models per width (100 draws/cell/model, cal-sizes 30–240) resolves the earlier ambiguity about model-specific idiosyncrasy:

| Width | pre-ECE | frac. worse @ cs=30 | frac. worse @ cs=240 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | 0.074 | 52.2% | 29.6% |
| 16 | 0.018 | 86.8% | 76.2% |
| 64 | 0.014 | 89.6% | 89.2% |

For the poorly-calibrated width (4, pre-ECE 0.074), more calibration data substantially reduces the harm rate. For the two already-well-calibrated widths (16, 64), the harm rate stays high (76–89%) regardless of calibration-set size — averaging over 5 models removes the single-model sign-flip artifact from v1 (where width 64's harm rate appeared to *rise* with more data) and confirms a cleaner, more robust version of the same conclusion: reducing T* estimation noise does not rescue calibration when a model is already close to calibrated, because the T* being estimated more precisely is still, on average, the wrong correction to apply.

### 3.4 Test accuracy (context)

Test accuracy rises with width before plateauing on all three datasets (digits: 0.844→0.980; synthetic: 0.748→0.839; breast_cancer accuracy also rises then plateaus), confirming these are genuinely underparameterized-to-adequate settings, not already saturated.

## 4. Conclusion

Within one-hidden-layer MLPs on three small classification tasks, the practical picture is more specific than "capacity–overconfidence extends to small networks": that extension holds cleanly on 2 of 3 datasets (synthetic, breast_cancer) once we control for the outlier-driven digits result, and the much more robust, consistent finding across all three datasets is that **pre-calibration ECE, not width, predicts whether temperature scaling helps**. Already-well-calibrated small networks are the ones most likely to be hurt by post-hoc scaling, and this risk is not resolved by using a larger calibration set — a multi-model mechanism check shows the harm rate for well-calibrated widths stays high (76–89%) even at 240 calibration points.

**Practical takeaway:** for small networks, measure pre-calibration ECE before applying temperature scaling. If it is already low (our data suggests roughly <0.02–0.03 in this MLP/bin-size setting), do not assume calibration helps — validate via cross-validation on the calibration split rather than applying it by default; width alone is not a reliable signal either way.

**Limitations.** Single architecture family (one-hidden-layer MLP), scalar temperature scaling only (no vector/matrix scaling, isotonic regression), three datasets (two real, one synthetic) — the breast_cancer addition strengthens but does not settle generalization, since it is also a single, comparatively easy binary problem. The pre-ECE-predicts-benefit finding and the width-4-outlier correction were identified after seeing the confirmatory results and are exploratory, not independently pre-registered; the underlying association is nonetheless consistent across three datasets and a plausible mechanism check, but a fresh pre-registered replication targeting pre-ECE directly (rather than width) would strengthen the claim further. The mechanism check's 76–89% harm-rate estimates come from bootstrap-resampling one fixed pool of digits data across 5 models per width, not from independently sampled populations. Generalization beyond MLPs (small CNNs/transformers) and beyond 2–10-class problems is untested.
