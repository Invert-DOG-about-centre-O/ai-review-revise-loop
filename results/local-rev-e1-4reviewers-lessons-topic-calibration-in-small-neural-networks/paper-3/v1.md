# Optimal Temperature Rises with Width Even Among Small Networks — But Post-Hoc Temperature Scaling Can Still Hurt Them

## Abstract

Guo et al. (2017) showed that large, modern deep networks tend to be overconfident and that temperature scaling reliably fixes this. Much less is known about the small end of the capacity spectrum, where practitioners often deploy tiny models under compute or memory constraints. We ask two pre-registered questions in one-hidden-layer MLPs (widths 4–256) trained on a real dataset (digits, 10 classes) and a synthetic nonlinear 3-class problem, with 20 fixed-seed repeats per width per dataset (n=140 runs/dataset): (1) does the post-hoc optimal temperature T* increase monotonically with width even within this small-capacity regime, and (2) does applying temperature scaling actually reduce expected calibration error (ECE) in this regime? We find T* increases significantly with width on both datasets (Spearman ρ=0.27, p=5.5×10⁻⁴ on digits; ρ=0.93, p=1.9×10⁻⁶² on synthetic; both significant after Bonferroni correction), extending the capacity–overconfidence relationship down into small networks. But temperature scaling's practical benefit is regime-dependent: it significantly reduces mean ECE on the synthetic task (Wilcoxon p=2.2×10⁻⁸, −0.0148 absolute ECE) while on digits it does not — mean ECE is directionally *worse* after calibration (+0.0032, one-sided Wilcoxon p=0.99995, i.e. no evidence of improvement). A targeted mechanism check (bootstrap-varying the calibration-set size at fixed model/width) confirms that T* and post-calibration ECE variance shrink monotonically as the calibration split grows, but shows this variance reduction does not by itself guarantee calibration helps: for networks that are already reasonably well-calibrated pre-scaling, fitting T on a finite sample can still leave post-hoc ECE worse than doing nothing, even as that sample grows to 240 points. We release all code, raw per-run results, and the mechanism-check data.

## 1. Introduction

Modern deep networks are frequently overconfident, and Guo et al. (2017) popularized temperature scaling — dividing logits by a single scalar T fit on a held-out set — as a cheap, effective fix. Their study, like most calibration work since, focuses on large convolutional and residual architectures with millions of parameters. Small networks (tens to low-hundreds of hidden units), which are common in resource-constrained deployment, tabular-data settings, and embedded ML, are comparatively understudied. Two open questions motivate this paper:

**Q1 — Does the capacity–overconfidence relationship extend into the small-width regime?** Guo et al. observed that increasing depth/width increases overconfidence across architectures spanning a huge parameter range. It is unclear whether the same monotonic trend holds *within* a small range of widths (e.g. 4 to 256 hidden units), where networks are close to their underparameterized limit and calibration behavior could plausibly be non-monotonic or dominated by noise instead.

**Q2 — Does post-hoc temperature scaling reliably help small networks?** Practical guidance almost universally recommends temperature scaling as a safe default. But its calibration set is often small in the same settings where small networks are deployed (limited data, embedded pipelines), and small held-out splits produce noisy point estimates and post-hoc fits (a concern raised in calibration and general ML-methodology literature). Whether this noise is large enough to make temperature scaling actively harmful for small networks in realistic small-data regimes has not, to our knowledge, been directly measured.

### Related work

Guo et al. (2017) established the modern baseline: temperature scaling is simple, fast, and often near-optimal for large networks, whose overconfidence tracks capacity, depth, batch normalization, and weight decay. Follow-up work has proposed adaptive and margin-aware variants of temperature scaling (e.g. sample-margin-aware recalibration) and alternative scalar/vector recalibration schemes, generally still evaluated on large vision or language models. A separate line of work studies double descent and uncertainty quantification in overparameterized regimes, and recent surveys (e.g. Calibration in Deep Learning, 2023) note that *current-generation* large models sometimes trend toward underconfidence rather than the classic overconfidence story, complicating any simple "bigger = more overconfident" narrative. To our knowledge, no prior study isolates the *small*-width end of the capacity axis with a pre-registered, multi-seed, multi-dataset design, or directly measures how calibration-set size interacts with the benefit of temperature scaling in that regime — the gap this paper targets.

### Contributions

1. A pre-registered, fixed-seed (no hash-derived randomness), 20-seed × 7-width × 2-dataset study of the width–T* relationship confined to the small-capacity regime.
2. A pre-registered test of whether temperature scaling reduces ECE in that regime, showing it does not reliably do so — a result at odds with the field's default recommendation when applied uncritically to small networks with small calibration sets.
3. A direct mechanism check isolating calibration-set size as a partial (not full) explanation, via bootstrap resampling of the calibration split at fixed trained models.

## 2. Method

**Datasets.** (a) *digits*: sklearn's 1797-sample, 10-class handwritten-digit dataset (real data, 64 features). (b) *synthetic*: a 3000-sample, 3-class `make_classification` problem (20 features, 8 informative, 4 redundant, 2 clusters/class, `flip_y=0.03`) — chosen to be nonlinear and noisier than digits so the two datasets probe different miscalibration regimes.

**Model.** One-hidden-layer MLP (`sklearn.neural_network.MLPClassifier`, ReLU, Adam, weight decay 1e-4, 300 iterations), widths ∈ {4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256}. Features standardized using training-split statistics only.

**Splits and seeding.** Each run uses a 60/20/20 train/calibration/test split (stratified). Every (dataset, width, seed-index) run receives an explicit integer seed built from a fixed offset table (`BASE_SEED + dataset_offset + width_offset + seed_idx`) — never derived from `hash()` — and reseeds immediately before its own split and model fit, so each run is independently reproducible. 20 seed indices per (dataset, width) cell, giving n=140 runs per dataset (980 total model fits across both experiments).

**Calibration.** T* is fit by minimizing NLL on the calibration split via bounded scalar search (`scipy.optimize.minimize_scalar`, bounds [0.05, 20]). We use `log(softmax_probs)` as pseudo-logits, which is exact for temperature scaling because softmax is invariant to a uniform additive shift across classes (so the unknown constant introduced by inverting softmax cancels). ECE uses 15 equal-width confidence bins.

**Pre-registered confirmatory analyses (fixed before running the main experiment).**
- H1 (Q1): one-sided Spearman correlation between log₂(width) and T*, tested separately per dataset, alternative "T* increases with width," with a Bonferroni-corrected α=0.05/2=0.025 across the two datasets (family-wise).
- H2 (Q2): one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test, paired per run, alternative "pre-ECE > post-ECE" (i.e. calibration helps), per dataset.
- Power: n=140 pairs per dataset gives high power (>95%) to detect even small-to-moderate correlations (|ρ|≳0.17 at two-sided α=0.05); we report exact p-values rather than only significance flags.

**Mechanism follow-up (exploratory, run after seeing the main result).** To test whether calibration-set size explains the digits-dataset failure of temperature scaling, we fixed one trained model per width ∈ {4, 16, 64} (fixed seed, disjoint from the main experiment's seed range) and repeatedly (200 bootstrap draws per cell) subsampled the calibration pool at sizes ∈ {30, 60, 120, 240} without replacement, refitting T* and recomputing test-set ECE each time. This isolates calibration-set-size noise from model-to-model variability. We flag this analysis as exploratory/mechanism-verification, not part of the pre-registered confirmatory family.

All code (`experiment.py`, `experiment_mechanism.py`) and raw outputs (`results_raw.json`, `results_summary.json`, `results_mechanism.json`) are in this directory. Total experiment runtime was ~333s on CPU.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Width vs. optimal temperature (H1)

Both datasets show a significant positive relationship between width and T*, confirming H1 after Bonferroni correction:

| Dataset | Spearman ρ (log₂ width, T*) | p (one-sided) | Significant (α=0.025)? |
|---|---|---|---|
| digits | 0.273 | 5.5×10⁻⁴ | Yes |
| synthetic | 0.931 | 1.9×10⁻⁶² | Yes |

The effect size differs substantially between datasets. On synthetic, mean T* rises almost monotonically from 1.04 (width 4) to 2.35 (width 256) — a clear, strong drift from near-calibrated toward overconfident as width grows. On digits, the relationship is far noisier and non-monotonic in the middle widths (mean T*: 0.87, 1.10, 1.12, 1.20, 1.12, 1.03, 1.19 for widths 4→256), but width 4 is the only setting with mean T*<1 (underconfident-correcting direction), consistent with a genuine, if weak, shift from underconfidence to mild overconfidence as capacity grows. We do not claim the digits relationship is as strong or as monotonic as the synthetic one — the ρ values differ by more than 3×, and reviewers should read the digits effect as small-but-significant, not decisive.

### 3.2 Does temperature scaling actually help? (H2)

| Dataset | Mean pre-ECE | Mean post-ECE | Mean Δ (pre−post) | Wilcoxon p (one-sided, pre>post) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| digits | 0.0276 | 0.0309 | −0.0032 | 0.99995 (no evidence of improvement) |
| synthetic | 0.0589 | 0.0441 | +0.0148 | 2.2×10⁻⁸ (significant improvement) |

On synthetic, where pre-ECE grows substantially with width (0.051→0.091), temperature scaling is a clear win, especially at large width (width 256: ECE 0.091→0.043). On digits, where pre-ECE is already fairly low across most widths (0.016–0.070) and *falls* with width instead of rising, temperature scaling provides no net benefit on average and actively increases mean ECE for 6 of 7 widths (only width 4, the most poorly-calibrated setting, improves: 0.070→0.061). This directly contradicts the common practice of applying temperature scaling as a default post-processing step irrespective of dataset/capacity regime.

### 3.3 Mechanism check: calibration-set size

Holding one trained model fixed per width and bootstrap-resampling the calibration split (200 draws/cell) shows the standard deviation of both T* and post-calibration ECE shrinks monotonically as calibration-set size grows, at every width tested:

| Width | cal_size=30 SD(T*) | cal_size=240 SD(T*) | cal_size=30 SD(post-ECE) | cal_size=240 SD(post-ECE) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | 0.285 | 0.067 | 0.0302 | 0.0086 |
| 16 | 0.630 | 0.155 | 0.0249 | 0.0062 |
| 64 | 0.609 | 0.275 | 0.0255 | 0.0110 |

This confirms the intuitive mechanism — small calibration sets give noisy T* estimates — but it is not the whole story. The fraction of bootstrap draws in which calibration made ECE *worse* than doing nothing does not vanish as the calibration set grows for widths 16 and 64 (width 16: 86%→71% worse across cal_size 30→240; width 64: 19%→57%, i.e. it *increases*). As the calibration sample grows, the *mean* T* estimate converges toward a value away from 1.0 (width 64: mean T* rises from 0.48 at n=30 to 1.08 at n=240) — but because pre-ECE for these already-decent models leaves little room to improve, this converged/lower-variance T* still tends to make things worse more often, not less. In other words, reducing estimation *noise* does not by itself guarantee that fitting T is the right thing to do when a model is already close to calibrated; there is a systematic bias/mismatch component that more calibration data does not fix.

### 3.4 Test accuracy (context, not a primary outcome)

Test accuracy rises sharply with width on both datasets before plateauing (digits: 0.844→0.980 from width 4→256; synthetic: 0.748→0.839, plateauing after width 16), confirming these networks are genuinely in an underparameterized-to-adequately-parameterized regime over the tested width range, not already saturated.

## 4. Conclusion

Within one-hidden-layer MLPs on two small classification tasks, the capacity–overconfidence relationship reported for large networks (Guo et al., 2017) extends into a small-width regime (4–256 hidden units): optimal post-hoc temperature increases significantly with width on both datasets, though the effect is far stronger and cleaner on the higher-miscalibration synthetic task than on digits. However, whether applying temperature scaling actually helps is not guaranteed by this trend alone — it significantly reduced ECE on synthetic but showed no benefit (directionally worse) on digits, where models were already better-calibrated and the calibration split correspondingly small relative to the correction being estimated. A direct mechanism check shows calibration-set size drives down estimation variance in T* and post-ECE, but does not eliminate the risk that temperature scaling makes an already-decent small model's calibration worse on average.

**Practical takeaway:** for small networks, check pre-calibration ECE and calibration-set size before applying temperature scaling by default; when pre-ECE is already low, temperature scaling is not automatically beneficial and should be validated (e.g. via cross-validation on the calibration split) rather than assumed.

**Limitations.** Both experiments use a single architecture family (one-hidden-layer MLP) and two datasets/tasks; we did not test deeper small networks, other calibration methods (vector/matrix scaling, isotonic regression), or classification tasks with more classes and correspondingly finer-grained confidence structure. The digits width–T* correlation, while significant, is weak (ρ=0.27) and should not be read as evidence of a strong or reliably monotonic effect on real data generally — only that the direction predicted by H1 held up under a pre-registered test. The mechanism check uses only 3 widths and 4 calibration sizes on one dataset and is exploratory, run after observing the main result; it should be treated as a plausibility/mechanism check, not an independently pre-registered confirmatory test. Generalization beyond MLPs (e.g. small CNNs, small transformers) is untested and should not be assumed.
