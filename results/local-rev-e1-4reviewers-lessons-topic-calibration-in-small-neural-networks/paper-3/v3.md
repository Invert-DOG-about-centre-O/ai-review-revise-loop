# Pre-Calibration ECE, Not Width, Predicts Whether Temperature Scaling Helps Small Networks

## Abstract

Guo et al. (2017) showed that large, modern deep networks tend to be overconfident and that temperature scaling reliably fixes this. Much less is known about the small end of the capacity spectrum. We pre-registered two questions in one-hidden-layer MLPs (widths 4–256) trained on sklearn digits (10 classes) and a synthetic nonlinear 3-class problem, 20 fixed-seed repeats per width per dataset (n=140/dataset): (1) does the post-hoc optimal temperature T* rise monotonically with width even in this small-capacity regime, and (2) does temperature scaling actually reduce expected calibration error (ECE)? T* rises significantly with width on both datasets, but a follow-up check shows digits' correlation is driven entirely by width 4 as an outlier (excl. width 4: ρ=−0.01, n.s.), while synthetic's is a genuine gradient (ρ≈0.94 either way). A third exploratory dataset (breast_cancer) sides with synthetic (ρ=0.36 excl. width 4, p=2.6×10⁻⁵), so the width–T* link is supported on 2 of 3 datasets, not universally. On whether scaling helps: two of three datasets show no net ECE benefit, and pre-calibration ECE correlates with the ECE reduction on all three (digits ρ=0.45; synthetic ρ=0.86; breast_cancer ρ=0.47, all p<10⁻⁷). A reviewer-prompted diagnostic, however, shows this magnitude correlation is largely a mechanical artifact: because reduction = pre-ECE − post-ECE, correlating pre-ECE with the reduction is partly guaranteed by shared variance even under a null where post-ECE is randomly shuffled relative to pre-ECE. Permutation tests confirm the observed correlations do **not** exceed this mechanical null on any of the three datasets (permutation p = 1.00, 0.85, 1.00). We therefore downgrade the magnitude-correlation claim to descriptive rather than causal. The more defensible evidence for "well-calibrated models get hurt" comes from a separate, non-magnitude-based mechanism check (5 independently trained models per width, bootstrap-varying calibration-set size): the fraction of resamples in which scaling makes ECE *worse* than doing nothing stays high (76–89%, stable across calibration-set sizes well within cross-model noise, SD≈0.10–0.17) for two already-well-calibrated widths, while it drops substantially (52%→30%) for the one poorly-calibrated width tested — a binary indicator not subject to the same part–whole artifact. We release all code, raw results, and the exploratory follow-ups, including the new confound diagnostic.

## 1. Introduction

Modern deep networks are frequently overconfident, and Guo et al. (2017) popularized temperature scaling — dividing logits by a single scalar T fit on a held-out set — as a cheap, effective fix, evaluated on large architectures. Small networks (tens to low-hundreds of hidden units), common in resource-constrained and tabular deployments, are comparatively understudied.

**Q1 — Does the capacity–overconfidence relationship extend into the small-width regime?** **Q2 — Does post-hoc temperature scaling reliably help small networks?** Practical guidance almost universally recommends temperature scaling as a safe default, but its calibration set is often small in exactly the settings where small networks are deployed.

### Related work

Guo et al. (2017) established the modern baseline. Follow-up work proposes adaptive/margin-aware recalibration variants, generally on large vision/language models; recent surveys note current large models sometimes trend toward *under*confidence, complicating any simple "bigger = more overconfident" story. To our knowledge no prior study isolates the small-width end of the capacity axis with a pre-registered, multi-seed, multi-dataset design, nor asks whether pre-calibration miscalibration level — rather than width — predicts calibration's benefit, nor checks whether that predictor is itself a statistical artifact of how "benefit" is computed.

### Contributions

1. A pre-registered, fixed-seed, 20-seed × 7-width × 2-dataset study of width vs. T*, plus a third exploratory dataset to adjudicate whether an apparent width effect is genuine or an outlier artifact.
2. A pre-registered test showing temperature scaling does not reliably reduce ECE for small networks, and a follow-up showing pre-calibration ECE correlates with benefit across all three datasets — **and a confound check (new in this revision) showing that correlation is largely mechanical**, prompted independently by two reviewers who flagged the part-whole structure of reduction = pre−post.
3. A mechanism check using 5 independently trained models per width, whose *binary* harm-rate outcome is not subject to the same mechanical artifact and remains the most defensible evidence that already-well-calibrated small networks are disproportionately hurt by scaling.

## 2. Method

**Datasets.** (a) *digits*: sklearn's 1797-sample, 10-class digit dataset. (b) *synthetic*: 3000-sample, 3-class `make_classification` (20 features, flip_y=0.03). (c) *breast_cancer* (exploratory, post-hoc): sklearn's 569-sample, 2-class dataset, identical pipeline, used to check whether digits' or synthetic's width–T* pattern is more representative.

**Model.** One-hidden-layer MLP (`sklearn.neural_network.MLPClassifier`, ReLU, Adam, weight decay 1e-4, 300 iterations), widths ∈ {4,8,16,32,64,128,256}. Features standardized on train-split statistics.

**Splits and seeding.** 60/20/20 train/calibration/test, stratified. Every (dataset, width, seed-index) run gets an explicit integer seed from a fixed offset table (never `hash()`). 20 seeds × 7 widths × 2 datasets = n=140/dataset for confirmatory experiments; breast_cancer reuses the identical scheme, also n=140.

**Calibration.** T* minimizes NLL on the calibration split (`scipy.optimize.minimize_scalar`, bounds [0.05,20]) using `log(softmax_probs)` as pseudo-logits. ECE uses 15 equal-width bins.

**Pre-registered confirmatory analyses (digits, synthetic only).** H1: one-sided Spearman(log₂ width, T*), Bonferroni α=0.025. H2: one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank, pre-ECE > post-ECE.

**Exploratory follow-ups (run after seeing confirmatory results).** (i) H1 excluding width 4, all datasets. (ii) Adding breast_cancer. (iii) Spearman(pre-ECE, ECE reduction) and pre-ECE tercile breakdowns, all datasets. (iv) A mechanism check extending the original single-model bootstrap (widths {4,16,64}, cal-sizes {30,60,120,240}) to 5 independently trained models per width (100 draws/cell/model).

**New in this revision — mechanical-confound diagnostic.** Because reduction = pre-ECE − post-ECE, Spearman(pre-ECE, reduction) can be large purely from shared variance even if pre- and post-ECE are statistically independent draws. We test this directly: for each dataset, we compute corr(pre-ECE, post-ECE) and construct a null distribution for Spearman(pre-ECE, reduction) by randomly permuting post-ECE against pre-ECE 20,000 times (breaking any real pairing while preserving both marginals), then ask whether the observed correlation exceeds this null.

All code and raw outputs (`experiment.py`, `experiment_dataset3.py`, `experiment_mechanism.py`, `experiment_mechanism_v2.py`, `quick_analysis.py`, `check_confound.py`, and corresponding `results_*.json`) are in this directory.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Width vs. optimal temperature (H1) — and why digits' correlation is misleading

| Dataset | ρ (all widths) | p | ρ (excl. width 4) | p |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| digits | 0.273 | 5.5×10⁻⁴ | **−0.008** | 0.53 (n.s.) |
| synthetic | 0.931 | 1.9×10⁻⁶² | 0.937 | 4.8×10⁻⁵⁶ |
| breast_cancer (exploratory) | 0.451 | 1.1×10⁻⁸ | 0.360 | 2.6×10⁻⁵ |

Digits' significant H1 result is **not a genuine width gradient**: per-width mean T* is 0.87, 1.10, 1.12, 1.20, 1.12, 1.03, 1.19 for widths 4→256 — non-monotonic across widths 8–256, and width 4 is the only cell with mean T*<1. Synthetic's correlation is unaffected by dropping width 4. Breast_cancer's correlation *survives* dropping width 4, siding with synthetic. We read the width-extends-into-small-networks claim as supported on 2 of 3 datasets, with digits a specific counterexample — a more conservative claim than v1's pooled framing. We note (per reviewer feedback) that a single tie-breaking dataset is a thin basis for "which pattern generalizes"; we treat this as suggestive, not settled (see Limitations).

### 3.2 Does temperature scaling help? Pre-ECE correlates with benefit — but the correlation is largely mechanical

| Dataset | Mean pre-ECE | Mean post-ECE | Wilcoxon p (pre>post) |
|---|---|---|---|
| digits | 0.0276 | 0.0309 | 0.99995 (no improvement) |
| synthetic | 0.0589 | 0.0441 | 2.2×10⁻⁸ (significant) |
| breast_cancer (exploratory) | 0.0316 | 0.0312 | 0.478 (no improvement) |

Two of three datasets show no net benefit. In v2 we reported that Spearman(pre-ECE, pre-ECE − post-ECE) is positive and significant on all three datasets and read this as evidence that pre-ECE, not width, predicts benefit. **Two reviewers independently identified a serious problem with this test**: since the outcome variable is a difference that contains pre-ECE as a term, a positive correlation is partly guaranteed by shared variance, not necessarily by any real coupling between how miscalibrated a model starts and how much it improves. We ran the diagnostic described in Section 2 to check this directly:

| Dataset | corr(pre-ECE, post-ECE) | Observed ρ(pre-ECE, reduction) | Permutation-null mean [95% CI] | Permutation p |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| digits | 0.635 | 0.453 | 0.631 [0.53, 0.72] | 0.9998 |
| synthetic | −0.032 | 0.865 | 0.879 [0.85, 0.91] | 0.847 |
| breast_cancer | 0.466 | 0.473 | 0.700 [0.63, 0.77] | 1.000 |

The observed correlation does **not** exceed the mechanical null on any dataset — if anything it sits at or below the null mean. This means the magnitude relationship we highlighted in v2 is largely explained by the part-whole structure of the statistic itself, not by a genuine signal that low-pre-ECE models are disproportionately harmed. We retract the strong causal framing of this result. What the diagnostic does leave us with: on synthetic, corr(pre-ECE, post-ECE) ≈ 0 — post-ECE is essentially unpredictable from pre-ECE, consistent with scaling doing real, dataset-dependent work rather than just tracking the starting point; on digits and breast_cancer, corr(pre-ECE, post-ECE) is moderate-positive (0.64, 0.47), meaning post-ECE partially tracks pre-ECE, which is itself consistent with scaling not reliably closing the gap for already-good models. We regard these correlational patterns as descriptive, not as evidence that pre-ECE causally determines benefit.

The tercile breakdowns (digits high-pre-ECE tercile Wilcoxon p=0.058; synthetic high tercile p=3.6×10⁻¹⁴, low tercile p=0.9995) point the same qualitative direction but are exploratory, uncorrected for the roughly ten significance tests reported across 3.1–3.2, and the digits marginal result would not survive even a simple Bonferroni correction at that scale. We flag this multiplicity explicitly, as one reviewer requested.

### 3.3 Mechanism check: a harm-rate test that avoids the magnitude-correlation artifact

The mechanism check sidesteps the part-whole problem above because it does not correlate a magnitude difference with pre-ECE; it reports, per width, the *fraction* of bootstrap calibration-set resamples in which post-ECE > pre-ECE (scaling made things strictly worse), independent of how large that difference is. Using 5 independently trained digits models per width (100 draws/cell/model, cal-sizes 30–240):

| Width | pre-ECE | frac. worse @ cs=30 | frac. worse @ cs=240 | cross-model SD @ cs=240 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | 0.074 | 52.2% | 29.6% | 0.27 |
| 16 | 0.018 | 86.8% | 76.2% | 0.17 |
| 64 | 0.014 | 89.6% | 89.2% | 0.12 |

For the poorly-calibrated width (4), more calibration data substantially reduces the harm rate. For the two already-well-calibrated widths, the harm rate stays high regardless of calibration-set size. One reviewer asked whether width 64's near-flat 89.6%→89.2% is real or noise: given the cross-model SD of ≈0.12 at cs=240 (SE of the 5-model mean ≈0.05), a 0.4-point difference is indistinguishable from noise — we do not read anything into that specific flatness, only into the fact that the rate never drops meaningfully from its ≈90% starting point, unlike width 4's clear decline. This binary, per-width comparison is not immune to every confound (it still compares different widths' fixed models, and pre-ECE varies with width by construction), but it does not suffer the specific part-whole artifact identified in 3.2, and we consider it the paper's best remaining evidence that well-calibrated small models are disproportionately harmed by scaling.

### 3.4 Test accuracy (context)

Test accuracy rises with width before plateauing on all three datasets (digits: 0.844→0.980; synthetic: 0.748→0.839; breast_cancer similar), confirming these are genuinely underparameterized-to-adequate settings, not already saturated.

## 4. Conclusion

Within one-hidden-layer MLPs on three small classification tasks: the capacity–overconfidence extension holds on 2 of 3 datasets (synthetic, breast_cancer) once the outlier-driven digits result is corrected. On whether calibration helps, our v2 claim that "pre-calibration ECE, not width, predicts benefit" was **too strong** — a reviewer-prompted permutation diagnostic shows the magnitude correlation behind that claim does not exceed a mechanical null on any dataset, so we no longer treat it as a genuine predictive relationship. The more defensible, artifact-resistant evidence is the binary harm-rate mechanism check: already-well-calibrated widths are hurt by scaling in 76–89% of resamples regardless of calibration-set size, while a poorly-calibrated width is not. We now state the practical claim at that (weaker, better-supported) level rather than as a general "pre-ECE predicts benefit" rule.

**Practical takeaway:** for small networks, do not apply temperature scaling by default. If pre-calibration ECE is already low, treat scaling as likely to *increase* ECE rather than decrease it, and validate via cross-validation on the calibration split before deploying it; width alone is not a reliable signal either way, and the specific numeric pre-ECE threshold we floated in v2 (<0.02–0.03) should be read as descriptive of this dataset pool, not a validated cutoff.

**Limitations.** Single architecture family, scalar temperature scaling only, three datasets (two real, one synthetic) — breast_cancer is a single, comparatively easy binary problem and remains a thin arbiter between the digits- and synthetic-like patterns, as multiple reviewers noted. The width-4-outlier correction and the mechanism check are exploratory but hold up under scrutiny; the pre-ECE magnitude-correlation finding does not, and we have downgraded it accordingly rather than defending it further. We did not run a fresh pre-registered replication targeting either pre-ECE or the harm-rate result directly — doing so, ideally on additional small real tabular datasets rather than another synthetic or easy binary set, remains the clearest next step reviewers converged on. Exploratory tests in 3.1–3.2 are not corrected for their full multiplicity beyond the two pre-registered H1/H2 tests. The mechanism check's harm-rate estimates come from bootstrap-resampling one fixed pool of digits data across 5 models, not independently sampled populations. Generalization beyond MLPs and beyond 2–10-class problems is untested.
