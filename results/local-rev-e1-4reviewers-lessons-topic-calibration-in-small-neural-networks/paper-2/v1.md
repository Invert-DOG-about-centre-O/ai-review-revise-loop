# Does the Width–Calibration "Crossover" in Small Neural Networks Generalize Across Datasets? A Pre-Registered, Multi-Seed, Two-Dataset Study

## Abstract

Small multilayer perceptrons are commonly reported to move from underconfidence to overconfidence as hidden-layer width increases, with a "crossover width" separating the two regimes. Prior work in this line of investigation (including earlier papers in this series) established that such a crossover is noisy and seed-dependent even on a single dataset. Here we ask a sharper generalizability question, decided and pre-registered before any results were inspected: does the crossover-width distribution transfer from one dataset to another? We train single-hidden-layer MLPs (widths 2–256) on two classification problems — the real 8×8 `digits` dataset and a synthetic 2-D Gaussian-blob task — with 15 independently reseeded runs per (dataset, width) cell (240 runs total, fixed integer seeds, no `hash()`-based seeding). We find that both datasets show the expected average trend (confidence bias moves toward zero as width grows), but a *stable* crossover width — a width beyond which bias never reverts to underconfidence within our grid — exists for only 7/15 digits seeds and 4/15 synthetic seeds; the synthetic task's mean bias never reaches zero within the tested range. A pre-registered Mann–Whitney U test on the crossover widths that do exist finds no significant difference between datasets (p = 0.77), but the test is severely underpowered (minimum detectable rank-biserial effect ≈ 0.74 at n = 7, 4) — we report this as an inconclusive, power-limited null, not evidence of a shared mechanism. A direct mechanism check finds that the crossover width coincides with the width at which training accuracy first saturates (≥99%) in only 2/7 digits seeds and 0/0 synthetic seeds (no synthetic seed reached both), refuting the naive "crossover = interpolation threshold" hypothesis as tested here. We conclude that the width-driven overconfidence crossover is a real but fragile, seed- and dataset-sensitive phenomenon whose location cannot currently be predicted by training-set interpolation alone, and that comparisons across only two small tasks cannot establish generality.

## 1. Introduction

Confidence calibration — whether a model's predicted probabilities match its empirical accuracy — has been extensively studied for large, modern deep networks, which are typically overconfident (Guo et al., 2017). A smaller literature asks the converse question for small networks and finds the opposite tendency can hold: very narrow models are often *underconfident*, and confidence bias shifts sign as capacity grows (see prior rounds of this series for a single-dataset width sweep establishing a noisy crossover point). Separately, recent work on foundation models finds that today's large in-distribution models often trend underconfident again, reversing the classical overconfidence narrative and motivating renewed interest in *why* confidence bias has a sign at all rather than only *how much* miscalibration exists (Beyond Overconfidence: Foundation Models Redefine Calibration in Deep Neural Networks, arXiv:2506.09593). Most practical fixes remain post-hoc: temperature scaling and its many recent extensions — adaptive, per-input temperatures (aclanthology 2024.emnlp-main.1007), ensemble temperature scaling for graph networks (GETS, ICLR 2025), margin-aware recalibration (arXiv:2506.23492), and quantile-adaptive variants (arXiv:2606.21749) — rescale confidence after training rather than explaining why undertrained or under-parameterized models land on one side of the calibration line in the first place.

The specific "width crossover" claim we probe here is attractive because it is simple and mechanistically suggestive (a network with too little capacity cannot fit the training distribution and hedges, i.e. underconfidence; once capacity exceeds what is needed to interpolate the training set it starts to memorize and become overconfident). But the project's own accumulated experience across earlier single-dataset studies in this series is that such threshold claims are exactly the kind that look clean on one seed and dissolve under a multi-seed audit. This paper takes that lesson at face value and asks the next natural question: even granting seed-to-seed noise, does the *distribution* of crossover widths look the same on a second, structurally different dataset? If yes, that is evidence the phenomenon reflects something about MLP training generally; if the two distributions look different, or if a stable crossover frequently fails to exist at all, that argues the effect is dataset-specific and the "capacity threshold" framing over-claims.

**Contributions.** (1) A pre-registered two-dataset, 15-seed, 8-width sweep (240 independently reseeded MLP training runs) measuring expected calibration error (ECE) and signed confidence bias. (2) A single confirmatory significance test (Mann–Whitney U on per-seed crossover widths), decided before data collection, reported together with an honest power analysis rather than as a clean positive/negative result. (3) A direct, executed mechanism check of the "crossover = training-accuracy saturation" hypothesis, rather than an assertion of plausibility. (4) An honest accounting of how often a stable crossover exists at all in the tested grid, which we believe is a more informative quantity than any single "crossover width" point estimate.

## 2. Related Work

**Calibration of large networks.** Guo et al. (2017) established that modern deep, high-capacity networks are typically overconfident and that simple temperature scaling is a strong post-hoc fix. Follow-up work (Revisiting the Calibration of Modern Neural Networks, arXiv:2106.07998; Rethinking Calibration of Deep Neural Networks, NeurIPS 2021) refined when and why architectural and training choices shift confidence bias, generally still in the large-model, large-dataset regime.

**Post-hoc recalibration.** A large and still-active literature extends temperature scaling to be adaptive/input-dependent (arXiv 2606.21749; ACL 2024 EMNLP main 1007), ensemble-based for structured data such as graphs (GETS, OpenReview qgsXsqahMq), or margin-aware (arXiv:2506.23492). These methods treat miscalibration as a symptom to correct after training and are largely agnostic to *why* a given model over- or under-shoots; our question is upstream of this literature.

**Foundation-model calibration reversal.** Recent evidence that today's largest in-distribution models trend underconfident (arXiv:2506.09593) is a reminder that "networks are overconfident" is a regime-dependent empirical fact, not a law — which motivates asking the same question at the opposite (small-model) end of the capacity spectrum, where the present paper and its predecessors in this series sit.

**This series.** Earlier papers in this project established, on a single dataset, that a width-driven under-to-overconfidence crossover exists but is seed-noisy, and that claims about its exact location require multi-seed reporting, fixed (non-hashed) seeding, and mechanism verification rather than assertion. We adopt all of those methodological requirements here and add the cross-dataset axis, which prior rounds identified as untested and necessary for any claim broader than "on this one dataset."

## 3. Method

**Pre-registration.** Before running any experiment we fixed: the two datasets, the width grid, the seed list, the metrics, the crossover definition, and the single confirmatory test. No results were inspected before this protocol was frozen; no sample size was extended after seeing p-values (addressing the optional-stopping failure mode flagged in this series' prior rounds).

**Datasets.** (1) `digits`: the sklearn 8×8-pixel, 10-class handwritten digit dataset (1797 real samples, pixel values scaled to [0,1]). (2) `synthetic`: a 2-feature, 2-class Gaussian-cluster classification task generated with scikit-learn's `make_classification` (2000 samples, 2 informative features, 2 clusters per class, 5% label noise), chosen to be structurally unlike `digits` (low-dimensional, 2-class, synthetic vs. high-dimensional, 10-class, real) so that agreement between the two would be a meaningful generalization signal.

**Models.** Single-hidden-layer MLPs (`sklearn.neural_network.MLPClassifier`, ReLU, Adam, L2 = 1e-4, max 500 iterations) at widths {2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256}.

**Seeding.** For each (dataset, width, seed) cell we use the fixed integer seed directly (0–14) for both the train/test split (`random_state=seed`, stratified 70/30 split, redone independently per cell) and the MLP's own `random_state`. Each cell is reseeded immediately before training — we do not share an RNG stream across widths within a seed, which the project's methodology notes flag as a source of spurious "before/after" comparisons in earlier rounds.

**Metrics.** On the held-out test split: accuracy, 10-bin expected calibration error (ECE), and confidence bias = mean(max softmax probability) − accuracy (positive = overconfident, negative = underconfident).

**Crossover definition.** For a given (dataset, seed), the crossover width is the smallest width in the grid such that bias is non-negative at that width *and remains non-negative at every larger width tested*. If no such width exists (bias never turns positive, or turns positive but later reverts to negative), the crossover is undefined (`None`) for that seed.

**Confirmatory test.** A two-sided Mann–Whitney U test comparing the set of defined crossover widths between the two datasets, α = 0.05, run exactly once.

**Mechanism check.** For each seed with both a defined crossover width and a defined "training-saturation width" (smallest width reaching ≥99% training accuracy), we check whether the two coincide.

All code (`experiment.py`, `analysis.py`) and raw per-cell results (`results.json`, `run_log.txt`) are included alongside this paper.

## 4. Results

**Runtime.** All 240 training runs completed in 267s of wall-clock CPU time; total task time including analysis was well under the compute budget.

**Aggregate trend.** Averaged bias moves toward zero with width on both datasets. For `digits`, mean bias goes from −0.104 (95% bootstrap CI [−0.131, −0.072]) at width 2 to −0.0002 (CI [−0.0031, 0.0025]) at width 256 — the CI includes zero from width 32 onward. For `synthetic`, mean bias goes from −0.040 (CI [−0.052, −0.028]) at width 2 to only −0.0065 (CI [−0.0120, −0.0003]) at width 256 — still significantly underconfident even at the largest width tested; the synthetic task never reaches the "overconfident on average" regime in this grid. Mean ECE falls monotonically with width on both datasets (digits: 0.111 → 0.010; synthetic: 0.048 → 0.020), so calibration *error* shrinks even where confidence *bias* does not cross zero — the two are not interchangeable.

**Existence of a stable crossover.** A defined (stable, non-reverting) crossover width exists for only 7/15 `digits` seeds and 4/15 `synthetic` seeds. Loosening this to "ever touches overconfidence at any single width" gives 10/15 and 4/15 respectively. In other words, for the synthetic task, 11 of 15 seeds never produce an overconfident model at *any* width we tested, despite the aggregate mean trending in that direction.

**Confirmatory test (pre-registered).** Among seeds with a defined crossover, digits crossover widths (n=7): mean 113.1, median 64, range [8, 256]. Synthetic (n=4): mean 68.0, median 64, range [16, 128]. Mann–Whitney U = 16.0, p = 0.770 — not significant. However, the effect size (rank-biserial correlation) is −0.143, and the minimum detectable rank-biserial effect at this n (7 vs. 4) is ≈0.74 — an enormous effect would be required for significance at this sample size. **We do not interpret this as evidence the two datasets share a crossover distribution; the test is underpowered by construction (few seeds produce a defined crossover at all), and we report it as inconclusive rather than as a null result**, per the distinction this project's methodology treats as mandatory.

**Mechanism check.** Of the 7 digits seeds with both a defined crossover and a defined training-saturation width, only 2 coincide (crossover width = saturation width). No synthetic seed had both quantities defined. This directly tests and does not support the a priori plausible "crossover reflects the interpolation threshold" mechanism — we report this as a verified-and-rejected hypothesis rather than continuing to assert it informally.

All numbers above are persisted in `results.json` under `crossover_summary`, `crossover_none_count`, `mannwhitney_crossover_digits_vs_synthetic`, `analysis.bias_by_width_bootstrap_ci`, `analysis.ece_by_width`, `analysis.mannwhitney_effect_size_rank_biserial`, and `mechanism_check_train_saturation`.

## 5. Discussion and Limitations

The headline finding is negative in a useful way: the width-driven overconfidence crossover, when tested against a second, structurally different dataset under a pre-registered protocol, is not reliably present at all — a majority of seeds on the synthetic task never exhibit it within a wide width range — and where it is present, we lack the statistical power (with an ethically-fixed n of 15 seeds decided in advance) to say whether its location is dataset-invariant. This is consistent with, and sharpens, this series' earlier finding that single-dataset threshold claims are seed-fragile: here even the *existence* of the threshold, not just its exact value, is dataset-dependent.

Limitations: (1) Two datasets and one architecture family (single-hidden-layer MLP via scikit-learn/Adam) is still a narrow generalization test; a genuinely broad claim would need more datasets, depths, and at least one other training framework. (2) The synthetic task's near-symmetric, low-noise construction may simply make it "easy enough" that models stay well-calibrated-to-underconfident regardless of width — this is itself informative but is a property of that specific synthetic generator, not synthetic tasks in general. (3) Our crossover definition (monotone non-reversion) is conservative by design (it is what makes a "crossover" a genuine regime change rather than sampling noise around zero); a looser definition changes the counts reported above but not the qualitative picture that a large fraction of seeds have no stable crossover. (4) We ran exactly one confirmatory test as pre-registered; the mechanism check and the bootstrap/ECE summaries are descriptive, not additional hypothesis tests, and we have not corrected for a large family of comparisons because we deliberately kept the confirmatory family to one test.

## 6. Conclusion

Using a pre-registered, 15-seed, two-dataset, 8-width sweep with mechanism verification, we find that the previously reported width-driven under-to-overconfidence crossover in small MLPs is real on average but frequently absent at the level of an individual trained model, and its location — where it exists — cannot be distinguished between a real and a synthetic dataset with the power available at feasible seed counts, nor explained by training-accuracy saturation. We recommend that future claims about "the" crossover width in small-network calibration studies report the fraction of seeds exhibiting a stable crossover at all, not only the location conditional on one existing, and that cross-dataset transfer be treated as an open question rather than assumed.
