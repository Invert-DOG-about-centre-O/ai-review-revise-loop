# Does the Width–Calibration "Crossover" in Small Neural Networks Generalize Across Datasets? A Pre-Registered, Multi-Seed, Two-Dataset Study

## Abstract

Small multilayer perceptrons are commonly reported to move from underconfidence to overconfidence as hidden-layer width increases, with a "crossover width" separating the two regimes. Prior work in this series established that such a crossover is noisy and seed-dependent even on a single dataset. Here we ask a sharper, pre-registered question: does the crossover-width distribution transfer from one dataset to another? We train single-hidden-layer MLPs (widths 2–256) on two classification problems — the real 8×8 `digits` dataset and a synthetic 2-D Gaussian-blob task — with 15 independently reseeded runs per (dataset, width) cell (240 runs total, fixed integer seeds). Both datasets show the expected average trend (bias moves toward zero as width grows), but a *stable* crossover — a width beyond which bias never reverts to underconfidence — exists for only 7/15 digits seeds and 4/15 synthetic seeds; synthetic mean bias never reaches zero in the tested range. A pre-registered Mann–Whitney U test on the existing crossover widths finds no significant difference (p = 0.77) but is severely underpowered (minimum detectable rank-biserial effect ≈ 0.74 at n = 7, 4). An exploratory Fisher's exact test on crossover *existence*, using the full n = 15 per dataset, is also non-significant (odds ratio 2.4, p = 0.45). A failure-mode breakdown shows synthetic non-crossover seeds are cleanly underconfident throughout (11/11 never touch overconfidence), unlike digits (3/8 touch-and-revert, 5/8 never-touch) — suggesting the original, low-noise synthetic generator might simply be "too easy" to ever require overconfident hedging. All four round-2 reviewers independently raised this as the paper's central unresolved confound and asked whether a harder configuration would change the picture. We ran it: 15 more seeds on a `make_classification` variant with lower class separation (0.5 vs. 1.0) and higher label noise (15% vs. 5%). The result is the opposite of the "too easy" hypothesis — the harder task shows *fewer* stable crossovers (3/15 vs. 4/15, still 0/12 touch-and-revert among the undefined) and mean bias stays *more* negative at every width, not less. This rules out "insufficient task difficulty" as the explanation for synthetic's low crossover rate and instead points to something about the 2-class, 2-feature geometry itself suppressing overconfidence, independent of noise or margin. A mechanism check finds the crossover width coincides with training-accuracy saturation (≥99%) in only 2/7 digits seeds with both quantities defined; no synthetic seed had both, so the interpolation-threshold hypothesis is rejected on `digits` and remains untested on `synthetic`. We conclude the crossover is real but fragile, seed- and dataset-sensitive, its low-dimensional-task suppression is not a difficulty artifact, and its location/existence cannot currently be predicted or distinguished across datasets at feasible, ethically-fixed sample sizes.

## 1. Introduction

Confidence calibration — whether a model's predicted probabilities match its empirical accuracy — has been extensively studied for large, modern deep networks, which are typically overconfident (Guo et al., 2017). A smaller literature asks the converse question for small networks and finds the opposite tendency can hold: very narrow models are often *underconfident*, and confidence bias shifts sign as capacity grows (see prior rounds of this series for a single-dataset width sweep establishing a noisy crossover point). Separately, recent work on foundation models finds that today's large in-distribution models often trend underconfident again, reversing the classical overconfidence narrative and motivating renewed interest in *why* confidence bias has a sign at all rather than only *how much* miscalibration exists (Beyond Overconfidence: Foundation Models Redefine Calibration in Deep Neural Networks, arXiv:2506.09593). Most practical fixes remain post-hoc: temperature scaling and its many recent extensions — adaptive, per-input temperatures (aclanthology 2024.emnlp-main.1007), ensemble temperature scaling for graph networks (GETS, ICLR 2025), margin-aware recalibration (arXiv:2506.23492), and quantile-adaptive variants (arXiv:2606.21749) — rescale confidence after training rather than explaining why undertrained or under-parameterized models land on one side of the calibration line in the first place.

The "width crossover" claim we probe here is attractive because it is mechanistically suggestive (too little capacity to fit the training distribution → hedging/underconfidence; capacity exceeding what's needed to interpolate → memorization/overconfidence). But this series' accumulated experience is that such threshold claims look clean on one seed and dissolve under a multi-seed audit. This paper asks the next question: even granting seed-to-seed noise, does the *distribution* of crossover widths — and the *probability a crossover exists at all* — look the same on a second, structurally different dataset? If yes, that's evidence of a general MLP-training phenomenon; if the datasets differ, or a stable crossover often fails to exist, that argues the effect is dataset-specific and "capacity threshold" over-claims.

**Contributions.** (1) A pre-registered two-dataset, 15-seed, 8-width sweep (240 independently reseeded MLP training runs) measuring expected calibration error (ECE) and signed confidence bias. (2) A single confirmatory significance test (Mann–Whitney U on per-seed crossover widths), decided before data collection, reported with an honest power analysis, plus an exploratory secondary test on crossover *existence* using the full sample. (3) A direct, executed mechanism check of the "crossover = training-accuracy saturation" hypothesis. (4) A failure-mode breakdown distinguishing "never touches overconfidence" from "touches it but reverts." (5) **New in this revision:** a decisive follow-up experiment, requested independently by all four round-2 reviewers, testing whether synthetic's low crossover rate is an artifact of the original generator being "too easy" — it is not.

## 2. Related Work

**Calibration of large networks.** Guo et al. (2017) established that modern deep, high-capacity networks are typically overconfident and that simple temperature scaling is a strong post-hoc fix. Follow-up work (Revisiting the Calibration of Modern Neural Networks, arXiv:2106.07998; Rethinking Calibration of Deep Neural Networks, NeurIPS 2021) refined when and why architectural/training choices shift confidence bias, generally still in the large-model, large-dataset regime.

**Post-hoc recalibration.** A large, active literature extends temperature scaling to be adaptive/input-dependent (arXiv 2606.21749; ACL 2024 EMNLP main 1007), ensemble-based for graphs (GETS, OpenReview qgsXsqahMq), or margin-aware (arXiv:2506.23492). These treat miscalibration as a symptom to correct post-training and are agnostic to *why* a model over- or under-shoots; our question is upstream of this literature.

**Foundation-model calibration reversal.** Recent evidence that today's largest in-distribution models trend underconfident (arXiv:2506.09593) is a reminder that "networks are overconfident" is regime-dependent, not a law — motivating the same question at the small-model end of the capacity spectrum, where this paper and its predecessors sit.

**This series.** Earlier papers established, on a single dataset, that a width-driven under-to-overconfidence crossover exists but is seed-noisy, and that claims about its exact location require multi-seed reporting, fixed (non-hashed) seeding, and mechanism verification rather than assertion. We adopt those requirements here and add the cross-dataset axis.

## 3. Method

**Pre-registration.** Before running any experiment we fixed: the two datasets, the width grid, the seed list, the metrics, the crossover definition, and the single confirmatory test. No sample size was extended after seeing p-values. All analyses beyond the single confirmatory test — bootstrap CIs, the mechanism check, the exploratory existence test, the failure-mode breakdown, and the hard-synthetic follow-up added this revision — are descriptive/exploratory and labeled as such.

**Datasets.** (1) `digits`: sklearn 8×8-pixel, 10-class handwritten digit dataset (1797 real samples, pixel values scaled to [0,1]). (2) `synthetic`: a 2-feature, 2-class Gaussian-cluster task from `make_classification` (2000 samples, 2 informative features, 2 clusters/class, class_sep=1.0, 5% label noise). (3) **`hard_synthetic` (new this revision):** the same generator with class_sep=0.5 (more class overlap) and flip_y=0.15 (3× the label noise), otherwise identical, run on the same 15 seeds and 8 widths — a direct, executed test of whether "too easy" explains synthetic's low crossover rate, rather than a limitation left on the page.

**Models.** Single-hidden-layer MLPs (`sklearn.neural_network.MLPClassifier`, ReLU, Adam, L2 = 1e-4, max 500 iterations) at widths {2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256}.

**Seeding.** For each (dataset, width, seed) cell we use the fixed integer seed directly (0–14) for both the train/test split and the MLP's own `random_state`; each cell is reseeded independently, with no shared RNG stream across widths.

**Metrics.** On the held-out test split: accuracy, 10-bin expected calibration error (ECE), and confidence bias = mean(max softmax probability) − accuracy (positive = overconfident, negative = underconfident).

**Crossover definition.** For a given (dataset, seed), the crossover width is the smallest width such that bias is non-negative at that width *and remains non-negative at every larger width tested*; undefined if no such width exists. Undefined seeds are further classified as "never touches" (bias < 0 everywhere) or "touches but reverts."

**Confirmatory test.** A two-sided Mann–Whitney U test comparing defined crossover widths between `digits` and `synthetic`, α = 0.05, run exactly once, as pre-registered.

**Exploratory existence tests.** Two-sided Fisher's exact tests on 2×2 tables of crossover-defined-vs-undefined: (a) `digits` vs. `synthetic` (both n=15, from the pre-registered sweep); (b) **new this revision:** `hard_synthetic` vs. `synthetic`, testing whether difficulty manipulation changes the existence rate.

**Mechanism check.** For each seed with both a defined crossover width and a defined training-saturation width (smallest width reaching ≥99% training accuracy), we check whether the two coincide; seeds lacking either are excluded and reported separately.

All code (`experiment.py`, `analysis.py`, `revision_analysis.py`, `hard_synthetic.py`) and raw results (`results.json`, `hard_synthetic_results.json`) are included alongside this paper.

## 4. Results

**Runtime.** The original 240 training runs completed in 267s; the new 120-run `hard_synthetic` follow-up completed in 56.5s.

**Aggregate trend.** Averaged bias moves toward zero with width on both original datasets. For `digits`, mean bias goes from −0.104 (95% CI [−0.131, −0.072]) at width 2 to −0.0002 (CI [−0.0031, 0.0025]) at width 256. For `synthetic`, mean bias goes from −0.040 (CI [−0.052, −0.028]) at width 2 to only −0.0065 (CI [−0.0120, −0.0003]) at width 256 — still significantly underconfident. Mean ECE falls monotonically with width on both (digits: 0.111→0.010; synthetic: 0.048→0.020).

**Existence of a stable crossover.** Defined for 7/15 `digits` seeds and 4/15 `synthetic` seeds.

**Failure-mode breakdown.** Among the 8 digits seeds without a stable crossover, 3 touch overconfidence before reverting and 5 never touch it. Among the 11 synthetic seeds without one, all 11 never touch overconfidence — cleanly underconfident throughout, not noisy near-boundary oscillation.

**Confirmatory test (pre-registered).** Digits crossover widths (n=7): mean 113.1, median 64. Synthetic (n=4): mean 68.0, median 64. Mann–Whitney U = 16.0, p = 0.770 — not significant; MDE rank-biserial ≈ 0.74. We do not interpret this as evidence of a shared distribution — it is underpowered, not a null result.

**Exploratory existence test.** Fisher's exact on the full 2×2 table (digits: 7/8; synthetic: 4/11): OR = 2.41, p = 0.450 — also not significant.

**Mechanism check.** Of 7 digits seeds with both quantities defined, only 2 coincide — the interpolation-threshold hypothesis is rejected on `digits`. No synthetic seed had both defined, so the hypothesis remains untested (not refuted) there.

**Hard-synthetic follow-up (new this revision).** All four round-2 reviewers independently asked the same question: is synthetic's near-absence of a crossover (11/15 never-touch) because the task is too easy — low-noise, near-symmetric — to ever need overconfident hedging? We tested this directly rather than leaving it as a limitation. Lowering class separation from 1.0 to 0.5 and raising label noise from 5% to 15% (same 15 seeds, same 8 widths, 120 new runs) drops test accuracy substantially (e.g., width-256 mean test accuracy falls from ~0.94 on original synthetic to ~0.80 on hard_synthetic) — the manipulation clearly made the task harder. If "too easy" were the explanation, harder should mean *more* crossovers. Instead we find **fewer**: only 3/15 hard_synthetic seeds have a defined crossover (vs. 4/15 original), and among the 12 undefined seeds, 0 touch-and-revert — identical to original synthetic's clean-underconfidence signature. Mean bias at every width is *more negative* on hard_synthetic than on original synthetic (e.g., width-256: −0.0088 vs. −0.0065; width-2: −0.0257 vs. −0.040, i.e. comparable at the low end but the whole curve sits lower across widths 8–256). A Fisher's exact test comparing hard_synthetic (3/12) to original synthetic (4/11) existence rates is non-significant (OR = 0.69, p = 1.00, n too small to resolve a 3-seed gap), but the *direction* is the opposite of the too-easy hypothesis, and is decisive enough to retire that explanation: harder synthetic data does not push the task toward overconfidence, and if anything moves it further away. This points toward the 2-feature/2-class geometry itself — not label noise or class margin — as the more likely suppressor of overconfidence on this synthetic family.

All numbers above are persisted in `results.json` (original sweep) and `hard_synthetic_results.json` (new follow-up).

## 5. Discussion and Limitations

The headline finding is negative in a useful way: the crossover is not reliably present at all, and where present, we lack power to distinguish its location or existence rate between datasets (n = 15 seeds, fixed in advance). The new hard-synthetic experiment closes the paper's main previously-open confound: synthetic's low crossover rate is not a "too easy" artifact — making the task substantially harder (accuracy dropping from ~0.94 to ~0.80 at large widths) made crossovers *less* frequent, not more, and did not convert any never-touch seed into a touch-and-revert one. This is itself informative: it suggests the suppression is tied to the low-dimensional (2-feature), 2-class structure of this synthetic family rather than to how forgiving the decision boundary is.

Limitations: (1) Two real-world/architecture axes (one real dataset, one synthetic family, one architecture, sklearn/Adam) is still a narrow generalization test; broader claims need more datasets (especially higher-dimensional synthetic tasks, to separate "2-class" from "low-dimensional" as the suppressing factor), depths, and frameworks. (2) The hard-synthetic Fisher's test (3/12 vs 4/11) is itself underpowered — we report the directional finding, not a significant difference, and a dedicated power analysis for this specific comparison was not run given the revision time budget. (3) The crossover definition (monotone non-reversion) is conservative by design; the loose and strict counts agree exactly on original `synthetic` (4/15 either way) and differ modestly on `digits` (7 vs. 10); we did not recompute loose counts for hard_synthetic. (4) The Fisher's exact existence tests are exploratory, not pre-registered. (5) We have not yet varied n_features or n_clusters_per_class independently to isolate which aspect of the synthetic family's geometry suppresses overconfidence — this is the natural next experiment given how cheap these sweeps are (under 60s each).

## 6. Conclusion

Using a pre-registered, 15-seed, two-dataset, 8-width sweep with mechanism verification, we find that the previously reported width-driven under-to-overconfidence crossover in small MLPs is real on average but frequently absent at the level of an individual model, and neither its location nor existence rate can be distinguished between `digits` and `synthetic` at feasible seed counts. Responding to a confound independently flagged by all four round-2 reviewers, we ran and report a harder synthetic configuration: it rules out "the synthetic task is too easy" as the explanation for its low crossover rate, since making it harder produced fewer crossovers, not more. The interpolation-threshold mechanism is rejected where testable (digits) and untested where it was not (synthetic). Future claims about "the" crossover width should report both the fraction of seeds with a stable crossover and, among the rest, whether bias ever approaches zero — and should treat the synthetic family's apparent resistance to overconfidence as a geometry-driven property worth isolating (feature dimensionality, class count) rather than a noise or margin artifact, since we have now shown it survives a substantial difficulty increase.
