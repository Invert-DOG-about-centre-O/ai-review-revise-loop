# Does the Width–Calibration "Crossover" in Small Neural Networks Generalize Across Datasets? A Pre-Registered, Multi-Seed, Two-Dataset Study

## Abstract

Small multilayer perceptrons are commonly reported to move from underconfidence to overconfidence as hidden-layer width increases, with a "crossover width" separating the two regimes. Prior work in this series established that such a crossover is noisy and seed-dependent even on a single dataset. Here we ask a sharper, pre-registered question: does the crossover-width distribution transfer from one dataset to another? We train single-hidden-layer MLPs (widths 2–256) on two classification problems — the real 8×8 `digits` dataset and a synthetic 2-D Gaussian-blob task — with 15 independently reseeded runs per (dataset, width) cell (240 runs total, fixed integer seeds). Both datasets show the expected average trend (bias moves toward zero as width grows), but a *stable* crossover — a width beyond which bias never reverts to underconfidence — exists for only 7/15 digits seeds and 4/15 synthetic seeds. A pre-registered Mann–Whitney U test on the existing crossover widths finds no significant difference (p = 0.77) but is severely underpowered (MDE rank-biserial ≈ 0.74 at n=7,4). An exploratory Fisher's exact test on crossover *existence* is also non-significant (OR 2.4, p = 0.45). A failure-mode breakdown shows synthetic non-crossover seeds are cleanly underconfident throughout (11/11 never touch overconfidence), unlike digits (3/8 touch-and-revert, 5/8 never-touch). All four round-2 reviewers flagged this as the paper's central unresolved confound and asked whether it was a difficulty artifact. We ran a harder variant (class_sep 0.5 vs. 1.0, label noise 15% vs. 5%): it produced *fewer* stable crossovers (3/15, vs. 4/15 easy), ruling "too easy" out. All four round-3 reviewers then asked us to test the geometry hypothesis this pointed toward, rather than leave it as a limitation — so this revision adds that experiment: holding class separation and label noise at the original *easy* values but raising dimensionality from 2 to 10 features (n_clusters_per_class=1, isolating dimensionality from cluster count) flips the picture — 15/15 seeds now show a stable crossover (vs. 4/15 at 2-D; Fisher's exact OR = ∞, p < 0.0001), mean bias crossing zero at width 64. Difficulty and dimensionality manipulations thus point in opposite directions, isolating low feature dimensionality — not label noise, class margin, or class count — as the mechanism suppressing overconfidence in this synthetic family. A mechanism check finds the crossover width coincides with training-accuracy saturation (≥99%) in only 2/7 digits seeds with both quantities defined; the interpolation-threshold hypothesis is rejected on `digits`. The crossover is real but fragile and seed-sensitive; its cross-dataset presence is not predictable from noise or margin alone, but is strongly associated with input dimensionality — a tested mechanism, not a residual limitation.

## 1. Introduction

Confidence calibration — whether a model's predicted probabilities match its empirical accuracy — has been extensively studied for large, modern deep networks, which are typically overconfident (Guo et al., 2017). A smaller literature asks the converse question for small networks and finds the opposite tendency can hold: very narrow models are often *underconfident*, and confidence bias shifts sign as capacity grows (see prior rounds of this series for a single-dataset width sweep establishing a noisy crossover point). Separately, recent work on foundation models finds that today's large in-distribution models often trend underconfident again, reversing the classical overconfidence narrative and motivating renewed interest in *why* confidence bias has a sign at all rather than only *how much* miscalibration exists (Beyond Overconfidence: Foundation Models Redefine Calibration in Deep Neural Networks, arXiv:2506.09593). Most practical fixes remain post-hoc: temperature scaling and its many recent extensions — adaptive, per-input temperatures (aclanthology 2024.emnlp-main.1007), ensemble temperature scaling for graph networks (GETS, ICLR 2025), margin-aware recalibration (arXiv:2506.23492), and quantile-adaptive variants (arXiv:2606.21749) — rescale confidence after training rather than explaining why undertrained or under-parameterized models land on one side of the calibration line in the first place.

The "width crossover" claim we probe here is attractive because it is mechanistically suggestive (too little capacity to fit the training distribution → hedging/underconfidence; capacity exceeding what's needed to interpolate → memorization/overconfidence). But this series' accumulated experience is that such threshold claims look clean on one seed and dissolve under a multi-seed audit. This paper asks the next question: even granting seed-to-seed noise, does the *distribution* of crossover widths — and the *probability a crossover exists at all* — look the same on a second, structurally different dataset? This revision goes one step further: rather than just documenting that datasets differ, we manipulate the axes along which they differ (difficulty, then dimensionality) to find out *which one* is responsible.

**Contributions.** (1) A pre-registered two-dataset, 15-seed, 8-width sweep (240 runs) measuring ECE and signed confidence bias. (2) A single confirmatory test (Mann–Whitney U on per-seed crossover widths), decided before data collection, reported with an honest power analysis, plus an exploratory existence test using the full sample. (3) A direct mechanism check of "crossover = training-accuracy saturation." (4) A failure-mode breakdown distinguishing "never touches overconfidence" from "touches it but reverts." (5) A difficulty-manipulation follow-up (harder synthetic), requested by all four round-2 reviewers, ruling out "too easy" as the explanation for synthetic's low crossover rate. (6) **New this revision:** a dimensionality-manipulation follow-up, requested by all four round-3 reviewers, isolating low feature dimensionality as the actual suppressor — raising synthetic from 2 to 10 features at the same easy noise/margin flips the crossover rate from 4/15 to 15/15.

## 2. Related Work

**Calibration of large networks.** Guo et al. (2017) established that modern deep, high-capacity networks are typically overconfident and that simple temperature scaling is a strong post-hoc fix. Follow-up work (Revisiting the Calibration of Modern Neural Networks, arXiv:2106.07998; Rethinking Calibration of Deep Neural Networks, NeurIPS 2021) refined when and why architectural/training choices shift confidence bias, generally still in the large-model, large-dataset regime.

**Post-hoc recalibration.** A large, active literature extends temperature scaling to be adaptive/input-dependent (arXiv 2606.21749; ACL 2024 EMNLP main 1007), ensemble-based for graphs (GETS, OpenReview qgsXsqahMq), or margin-aware (arXiv:2506.23492). These treat miscalibration as a symptom to correct post-training and are agnostic to *why* a model over- or under-shoots; our question is upstream of this literature. Recent evidence that today's largest in-distribution foundation models trend underconfident (arXiv:2506.09593) is a reminder that "networks are overconfident" is regime-dependent, not a law — motivating the same question at the small-model end of the capacity spectrum.

**This series.** Earlier papers established, on a single dataset, that a width-driven under-to-overconfidence crossover exists but is seed-noisy, and that claims about its exact location require multi-seed reporting, fixed (non-hashed) seeding, and mechanism verification rather than assertion. We adopt those requirements here and add the cross-dataset axis.

## 3. Method

**Pre-registration.** Before running any experiment we fixed: the two datasets, the width grid, the seed list, the metrics, the crossover definition, and the single confirmatory test. No sample size was extended after seeing p-values. All analyses beyond the single confirmatory test — bootstrap CIs, the mechanism check, the exploratory existence test, the failure-mode breakdown, the hard-synthetic follow-up, and the geometry (dimensionality) follow-up added this revision — are descriptive/exploratory and labeled as such.

**Datasets.** (1) `digits`: sklearn 8×8-pixel, 10-class handwritten digit dataset (1797 real samples, pixel values scaled to [0,1]). (2) `synthetic`: a 2-feature, 2-class Gaussian-cluster task from `make_classification` (2000 samples, 2 informative features, 2 clusters/class, class_sep=1.0, 5% label noise). (3) `hard_synthetic`: same generator, class_sep=0.5, flip_y=0.15 (3× noise), same 15 seeds/8 widths — tests whether "too easy" explains synthetic's low crossover rate. (4) **`geometry_synthetic` (new):** same generator at the original *easy* class_sep=1.0/flip_y=0.05, but n_features raised 2→10 (n_clusters_per_class=1, isolating dimensionality from cluster count), same seeds/widths — tests whether low dimensionality, not noise/margin, is the suppressor.

**Models.** Single-hidden-layer MLPs (`sklearn.neural_network.MLPClassifier`, ReLU, Adam, L2 = 1e-4, max 500 iterations) at widths {2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256}.

**Seeding.** For each (dataset, width, seed) cell we use the fixed integer seed directly (0–14) for both the train/test split and the MLP's own `random_state`; each cell is reseeded independently, with no shared RNG stream across widths.

**Metrics.** On the held-out test split: accuracy, 10-bin expected calibration error (ECE), and confidence bias = mean(max softmax probability) − accuracy (positive = overconfident, negative = underconfident).

**Crossover definition.** For a given (dataset, seed), the crossover width is the smallest width such that bias is non-negative at that width *and remains non-negative at every larger width tested*; undefined if no such width exists. Undefined seeds are further classified as "never touches" (bias < 0 everywhere) or "touches but reverts."

**Confirmatory test.** A two-sided Mann–Whitney U test comparing defined crossover widths between `digits` and `synthetic`, α=0.05, run exactly once, as pre-registered.

**Exploratory existence tests.** Fisher's exact tests on 2×2 tables of crossover-defined-vs-undefined: (a) `digits` vs. `synthetic`; (b) `hard_synthetic` vs. `synthetic`; (c) **new:** `geometry_synthetic` (10-D) vs. `synthetic` (2-D).

**Mechanism check.** For each seed with both a defined crossover width and a defined training-saturation width (smallest width reaching ≥99% training accuracy), we check whether the two coincide; seeds lacking either are excluded and reported separately.

All code (`experiment.py`, `analysis.py`, `revision_analysis.py`, `hard_synthetic.py`, `geometry_ablation.py`) and raw results (`results.json`, `hard_synthetic_results.json`, `geometry_ablation_results.json`) are included alongside this paper.

## 4. Results

**Runtime.** The original 240 training runs completed in 267s; the `hard_synthetic` follow-up (120 runs) completed in 56.5s; the new `geometry_synthetic` follow-up (120 runs) completed in 100.3s.

**Aggregate trend.** Averaged bias moves toward zero with width on both original datasets. For `digits`, mean bias goes from −0.104 (95% CI [−0.131, −0.072]) at width 2 to −0.0002 (CI [−0.0031, 0.0025]) at width 256. For `synthetic`, mean bias goes from −0.040 (CI [−0.052, −0.028]) at width 2 to only −0.0065 (CI [−0.0120, −0.0003]) at width 256 — still significantly underconfident, with mean test accuracy at width 256 of 0.925 (corrected from an earlier, over-rounded "~0.94" in prior drafts). Mean ECE falls monotonically with width on both (digits: 0.111→0.010; synthetic: 0.048→0.020).

**Existence of a stable crossover.** Defined for 7/15 `digits` seeds and 4/15 `synthetic` seeds.

**Failure-mode breakdown.** Among the 8 digits seeds without a stable crossover, 3 touch overconfidence before reverting and 5 never touch it. Among the 11 synthetic seeds without one, all 11 never touch overconfidence — cleanly underconfident throughout, not noisy near-boundary oscillation.

**Confirmatory test (pre-registered).** Digits crossover widths (n=7): mean 113.1, median 64. Synthetic (n=4): mean 68.0, median 64. Mann–Whitney U = 16.0, p = 0.770 — not significant; MDE rank-biserial ≈ 0.74. We do not interpret this as evidence of a shared distribution — it is underpowered, not a null result.

**Exploratory existence test.** Fisher's exact on the full 2×2 table (digits: 7/8; synthetic: 4/11): OR = 2.41, p = 0.450 — also not significant.

**Mechanism check.** Of 7 digits seeds with both quantities defined, only 2 coincide — the interpolation-threshold hypothesis is rejected on `digits`. No synthetic seed had both defined, so the hypothesis remains untested (not refuted) there.

**Hard-synthetic follow-up (difficulty manipulation).** Lowering class separation from 1.0 to 0.5 and raising label noise from 5% to 15% drops width-256 mean test accuracy from 0.925 to 0.808 — a real, substantial difficulty increase, not a cosmetic one. If "too easy" explained synthetic's low crossover rate, harder should mean *more* crossovers. Instead we find fewer: 3/15 hard_synthetic seeds have a defined crossover (vs. 4/15 easy), and among the 12 undefined seeds, 0 touch-and-revert — identical to easy synthetic's clean-underconfidence signature. Mean bias at every width is more negative on hard_synthetic than on easy synthetic. A Fisher's exact test comparing existence rates (hard: 3/12, easy: 4/11) gives OR = 0.69, p = 1.00 — a single non-significant comparison at n=15 per arm, which we read as *consistent with* "harder is not the driver" rather than a formal rejection of it; the directional finding (fewer, not more) is what rules "too easy" out, independent of this specific test's power.

**Geometry follow-up (dimensionality manipulation, new this revision).** All four round-3 reviewers asked us to actually run the n_features/n_clusters_per_class ablation flagged in the prior draft's limitations, rather than leave the "2-class/2-feature geometry" claim untested. We held class_sep=1.0 and flip_y=0.05 fixed at the original *easy* values — isolating dimensionality from the difficulty axis already tested — and raised n_features from 2 to 10 (n_clusters_per_class=1, so the manipulation targets dimensionality specifically, not cluster count). The result is unambiguous: **15/15 seeds now show a stable, non-reverting crossover** (vs. 4/15 at 2-D), with mean crossover width 81.2 (median 64). Mean bias crosses zero between width 32 (−0.0029) and width 64 (+0.0055), rising to +0.0212 at width 256 — the same generator family that was almost immune to overconfidence at 2-D becomes reliably overconfident at 10-D, at unchanged noise and margin. A Fisher's exact test on existence (10-D: 15/0 vs. 2-D: 4/11) gives OR = ∞, p < 0.0001 (a 0-cell table, so we report the raw perfect separation rather than a corrected OR). Test accuracy at width 256 is comparable to easy 2-D synthetic (0.954 vs. 0.925), so this is not confounded by the 10-D task being easier overall — dimensionality changed calibration behavior at roughly matched task performance.

All numbers above are persisted in `results.json`, `hard_synthetic_results.json`, and `geometry_ablation_results.json`.

## 5. Discussion and Limitations

The headline finding is now sharper than in the prior draft: the crossover's presence is not just seed-noisy, it is systematically suppressed by low input dimensionality, and this is no longer an inferred hypothesis but a directly tested one. Difficulty (class margin, label noise) and dimensionality point in opposite directions — making the task harder *reduced* crossovers (3/15), while raising dimensionality at unchanged, easy difficulty *increased* them to ubiquity (15/15) — which lets us attribute the 2-D synthetic family's resistance to overconfidence specifically to its low-dimensional input space, not to how forgiving its decision boundary is. This also reframes why `digits` (64-D pixel input) and 2-D synthetic disagreed originally: dimensionality, not "real vs. synthetic," may be the more relevant axis, and the two datasets differed on it by a wide margin (64 vs. 2 features) we had not previously isolated.

Limitations: (1) The geometry follow-up varies one factor (n_features, with n_clusters_per_class fixed at 1) on one generator family; it does not establish a dose-response curve (is 5 features enough? is it n_features or resulting boundary complexity that matters?), and does not test dimensionality on a real dataset (e.g., subsetting `digits`' pixels). (2) The hard-synthetic Fisher's test (3/12 vs 4/11) remains underpowered on its own; we rely on the qualitative direction, not that single p-value, for the "not a difficulty artifact" claim. (3) The crossover definition (monotone non-reversion) is conservative by design; loose and strict counts agree exactly on 2-D synthetic (4/15 either way) and differ modestly on digits (7 vs. 10); not recomputed for the new follow-ups. (4) All Fisher's exact existence tests are exploratory, not pre-registered. (5) The pre-registered confirmatory Mann-Whitney test remains underpowered by design (n=7,4); re-running it now would not be pre-registered, so we do not.

## 6. Conclusion

Using a pre-registered, 15-seed, two-dataset, 8-width sweep with mechanism verification, we find that the previously reported width-driven under-to-overconfidence crossover in small MLPs is real on average but frequently absent at the level of an individual model, and neither its location nor its baseline existence rate can be distinguished between `digits` and `synthetic` at feasible seed counts. Responding to round-2 review, we ruled out "the synthetic task is too easy": a harder configuration produced fewer crossovers, not more. Responding to round-3 review, we then directly tested the geometry hypothesis that difficulty manipulation had pointed toward: raising synthetic's dimensionality from 2 to 10 features, at the same easy noise and margin, took the crossover rate from 4/15 to 15/15. Difficulty and dimensionality manipulations move the crossover rate in opposite directions, isolating low input dimensionality as the mechanism suppressing overconfidence in this synthetic family — a tested finding, not a residual limitation. The interpolation-threshold mechanism is rejected where testable (digits) and untested where it was not (synthetic, at either dimensionality — neither reaches the ≥99% training-accuracy threshold needed for the comparison in our width range). Future claims about "the" crossover width should report both the fraction of seeds with a stable crossover and the input dimensionality of the task.
