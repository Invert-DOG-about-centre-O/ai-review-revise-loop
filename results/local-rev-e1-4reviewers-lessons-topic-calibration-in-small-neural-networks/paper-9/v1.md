# A Non-Monotonic Calibration Curve in Underparameterized MLPs, and a Training-Collapse Artifact That Mimics Good Calibration

## Abstract

Prior work on neural network calibration (Guo et al., 2017) shows that increasing capacity in *already large* architectures (deep ResNets, wide CNNs) worsens Expected Calibration Error (ECE), because such models keep driving down training NLL long after they saturate accuracy. It is comparatively unexplored what happens at the *small* end of the capacity axis — as a network shrinks from moderately sized toward severely underparameterized. We train one-hidden-layer MLPs (numpy, from scratch) on the sklearn `digits` dataset while sweeping hidden width from 2 to 512 units, with 10 random seeds per width, and measure ECE, NLL, and post-hoc temperature-scaling calibration. We find that calibration is **not monotonic** in width at the small end: ECE is worst at width 4–8 (mean ECE 0.046–0.048, vs. 0.016–0.023 for width ≥16, Welch's t=16.0, p=3×10⁻¹⁵ for the pooled 4–8 group vs. the ≥16 plateau), then plateaus once width reaches roughly 16 units, with no significant further change up to width 512 (t=1.22, p=0.24 for accuracy at width 128 vs. 512). We also identify and explicitly rule out a confound: the most severely underparameterized setting (width=2) *appears* to have low ECE on average, but this is a training-collapse artifact — 4/10 seeds converge to near-chance accuracy (~10%) with correspondingly near-uniform (low) confidence, which spuriously drives ECE near zero. Excluding these collapsed runs, width-2 models are in fact poorly calibrated (mean ECE 0.046, significantly worse than the width-16 plateau, t=6.18, p=3.5×10⁻⁴). Temperature scaling reduces ECE at most widths but the reduction is not statistically significant at any single width with n=10 seeds (paired t-tests, all p>0.05). These results are demonstrated on one dataset, one architecture family, and one capacity axis (width); we do not claim they generalize beyond this small-model, small-data regime.

## 1. Introduction

Confidence calibration — whether a model's predicted probability matches its empirical accuracy — matters wherever downstream decisions trust a model's confidence score (medical triage, autonomous systems, active learning, selective prediction). Guo et al. (2017) established that modern *large* neural networks are typically overconfident, and that increasing depth, width, or removing regularization (weight decay) tends to worsen ECE even as accuracy improves, because cross-entropy training keeps sharpening the softmax after classification error has plateaued. Temperature scaling — dividing logits by a single scalar T fit on a held-out validation set — was proposed as a cheap, effective post-hoc fix.

Most calibration studies, including Guo et al.'s capacity sweep, operate in the regime of already-substantial networks (ResNets, WideResNets) getting *larger*. Far less is known about the opposite end: practitioners deploying small networks on edge devices, in low-data regimes, or via knowledge distillation into compact students often ask the reverse question — as a network shrinks, does calibration keep improving, or is there a point at which severe underparameterization itself produces distinctive (and possibly worse) miscalibration? Related work on knowledge distillation shows that student calibration is sensitive to teacher calibration and training regime in non-obvious ways (Rethinking the Knowledge Distillation From the Perspective of Model Calibration; The Role of Teacher Calibration in Knowledge Distillation, 2025), suggesting capacity-calibration interactions are more complex than a single monotonic trend. Work on foundation models (Beyond Overconfidence: Foundation Models Redefine Calibration, 2025) also shows that the overconfidence story reverses in some very-large-model regimes, reinforcing that "smaller/larger" trends in calibration are regime-dependent rather than universal.

**Our contribution.** We run a controlled width sweep purely within the *small*-network regime (2–512 hidden units, single hidden layer, on a small, low-dimensional dataset) with enough seeds (n=10 per width) to report means, standard deviations, and formal significance tests, following the practice that informal "trend from 2 runs" claims are not credible. We find:

1. A **non-monotonic ("U-shaped-then-flat") relationship** between width and ECE within the small-network regime: calibration is worst in a narrow band (width 4–8) and then improves and plateaus from width 16 onward.
2. An explicit **confound check**: the apparent good calibration of the most extreme underparameterized setting (width=2) is an artifact of optimization collapse, not genuine calibration — we verify this directly by separating collapsed vs. non-collapsed seeds rather than asserting it.
3. **Temperature scaling's effectiveness is not statistically reliable** at any single width in our sample size, despite reducing point-estimate ECE at 7/9 widths — an honest report of a null result under proper testing, contrasting with looser "confidence intervals don't overlap" style claims.

## 2. Related Work

- **Guo et al. (2017), "On Calibration of Modern Neural Networks."** Established ECE/reliability diagrams as the standard calibration diagnostic and temperature scaling as an effective, simple post-hoc fix; showed that in the large-model regime, increasing depth/width/removing weight decay worsens calibration despite improving accuracy, because training over-optimizes NLL after accuracy saturates.
- **Label smoothing and its calibration trade-offs.** Label smoothing improves calibration on average but can distort class-wise probabilities and interacts poorly with subsequent temperature scaling (instance-based label smoothing literature). We do not use label smoothing here; it is a natural extension.
- **Knowledge distillation and calibration transfer (2024–2025).** Multiple recent papers show that student network calibration after distillation depends heavily on teacher calibration and training regime — motivating our question of what a *small* network's calibration looks like before any distillation is applied, as a baseline.
- **Temperature scaling limitations (2025).** Recent work shows temperature scaling's single-scalar assumption breaks under noisy/soft labels or small validation sets — relevant to our finding that its benefit is not statistically detectable at n=10 seeds per width, since our validation set (337 samples) is itself modest.
- **Capacity vs. calibratability trade-offs.** Work on regularization shows a tension between models that are *already* well-calibrated and models that are *calibratable* (responsive to post-hoc fixes) — consistent with our observation that post-hoc gains are inconsistent across widths.

Our study is distinguished by (a) restricting the capacity axis entirely to the small/underparameterized regime rather than the large-model regime, and (b) using n=10 seeds with formal significance testing throughout, rather than qualitative trend reporting.

## 3. Method

**Data.** `sklearn.datasets.load_digits` (1,797 8×8 grayscale images, 10 classes, pixel range 0–16, rescaled to [0,1]). Split 60% train (1,010) / 20% validation (337, used only for temperature fitting) / 20% test (450), stratified by class, fixed random split (seed 0) shared across all runs so all widths are compared on identical data.

**Model.** A single-hidden-layer MLP implemented from scratch in numpy: input (64) → hidden (width, ReLU) → output (10, softmax), trained with cross-entropy loss, He initialization, Adam optimizer (lr=0.03), L2 weight decay (1e-4), batch size 64, 150 epochs. We swept width ∈ {2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256, 512}, with 10 independent seeds per width controlling both initialization and minibatch order (90 total runs).

**Metrics.** Test-set accuracy, 15-bin ECE, and NLL, all computed pre- and post-temperature-scaling. Temperature T is fit by grid search (200 points, T∈[0.05,5.0]) minimizing NLL on the validation split, then applied to test logits. We also record average softmax confidence and the train−test accuracy gap (a simple overfitting proxy).

**Statistics.** For each comparison we report mean±SD across the 10 seeds and use Welch's two-sample t-test (unequal variance) for between-width comparisons and a paired t-test for pre- vs. post-temperature-scaling ECE at a given width (same seeds, so genuinely paired). We use Spearman rank correlation (not Pearson) between log₂(width) and each metric across all 90 runs, since the width axis is log-spaced and the relationship is not assumed linear.

Across all 90 runs, Spearman correlation of log₂(width) with ECE is ρ=−0.565 (p=6.6×10⁻⁹) and with NLL is ρ=−0.846 (p=9.3×10⁻²⁶): both decrease with width overall. But this monotone-looking aggregate correlation masks a non-monotonic shape, visible in the table: ECE rises from width 2→4→8 before falling and plateauing.

### 4.2 The peak-miscalibration band (width 4–8) is statistically robust

Pooling widths 4 and 8 (n=20) against the plateau region widths 16–512 (n=60): mean ECE 0.047 vs. 0.019, Welch's t=16.01, p=2.9×10⁻¹⁵. This is not a 2-seed fluke; it holds with 10 seeds per width and a formal test. Width 4 vs. width 16 alone: t=8.76, p=1.2×10⁻⁷.

### 4.3 Confound check: width=2's apparent good calibration is a training-collapse artifact

The raw table shows width=2 with mean ECE (0.029) *lower* than width 4 or 8 — naively suggesting calibration keeps improving as capacity shrinks further. We checked this directly rather than reporting it at face value. Splitting the 10 width-2 seeds by whether the run collapsed to near-chance accuracy (<15%, i.e., ≈ the 10%-class-chance baseline): 4/10 seeds collapsed (mean test accuracy ≈10.2%, mean confidence ≈10.6%, mean ECE 0.004) and 6/10 seeds trained successfully (mean accuracy 38.1%, mean ECE 0.046). The collapsed runs produce near-uniform, low-confidence softmax outputs that happen to numerically match their equally-low accuracy, driving ECE toward zero — this is degenerate optimization, not genuine calibration. Restricting to the 6 non-collapsed width-2 seeds, ECE (0.046) is statistically indistinguishable from width 4–8's peak and significantly *worse* than the width-16 plateau (t=6.18, p=3.5×10⁻⁴). So the true small-width calibration curve, once the collapse artifact is removed, does not dip at the very smallest width — it is monotonically bad from width 2 through 8, then improves and plateaus from width ≥16.

### 4.4 Accuracy plateaus alongside calibration

Test accuracy plateaus at width≥16 (96.3%→97.4%), and width 128 vs. 512 shows no significant accuracy difference (t=1.22, p=0.24). Unlike Guo et al.'s large-model finding — where accuracy plateaus but ECE keeps *worsening* as capacity keeps growing — we see no evidence of re-worsening calibration up to width 512 in this small-data, small-model regime; ECE also plateaus (0.016–0.019 for width≥64, no clear trend). This is consistent with the mechanism Guo et al. propose (overfitting to NLL after accuracy saturates) simply not having taken hold yet at these capacities/epoch counts — we did not test far larger widths or longer training where that mechanism might eventually emerge.

### 4.5 Temperature scaling: point-estimate gains, no significant effect at n=10

Temperature scaling lowers mean ECE at 7 of 9 widths (all except width 2's collapse-diluted average, and width 4, where it slightly *worsens* ECE: 0.048→0.053, plausibly because T* is being fit on a validation set where the width-4 model's poorly-separated logits make the NLL-minimizing T a poor match for the test-set ECE objective). But paired t-tests (same 10 seeds, pre- vs. post-scaling) show no significant effect at any individual width: p-values range from 0.06 (width 2, marginal) to 0.90 (width 512), none surviving even an uncorrected 0.05 threshold. We report this as a genuine null result at this sample size rather than treating the point-estimate reduction as established.

## 5. Discussion and Limitations

The central, well-supported finding is: **within the small-network regime, calibration is not monotonic in width** — there is a specific underparameterized band (roughly 4–8 hidden units on this 64-dim, 10-class, ~1,800-sample problem) that is significantly worse calibrated than either extreme we tested, and once width exceeds a problem-dependent threshold (~16 units here), further capacity increase up to 512 units does not measurably help or hurt calibration or accuracy. We explicitly do not claim this "sufficient capacity" threshold is a universal number — it will depend on the dataset's intrinsic dimensionality and class count, and we tested exactly one dataset (digits) and one architecture family (single-hidden-layer MLP).

We speculate, without directly testing it, that the width 4–8 peak coincides with the region where the model has enough capacity to fit a non-trivial fraction of the training data confidently but not enough to do so uniformly across classes/examples — consistent with the train−test accuracy gap also peaking in that same band (+0.047 to +0.062, vs. +0.017 at width 2 and +0.026 to +0.037 for width≥16). We label this connection speculative: we did not run an experiment that isolates overfitting from capacity as the driver of the ECE peak, and a different capacity axis (depth, or width on a different dataset) could show a differently shaped or absent peak.

The training-collapse artifact at width=2 is a useful cautionary example: without checking per-seed accuracy, one could report "smaller is better calibrated all the way down," which the aggregate numbers on their face suggest but which is false once collapsed runs are excluded. We flag this as the kind of alternative explanation that should always be checked directly for any headline calibration finding at extreme capacities, rather than a general claim that underparameterization always causes such collapses (we saw it only at width=2, on this dataset).

**Scope.** All results are from one dataset (sklearn digits, 1,797 8×8 images), one architecture (1-hidden-layer MLP, ReLU), one optimizer configuration, and one capacity axis (width, not depth). We did not test larger real-world image datasets, convolutional architectures, or the regime where Guo et al.'s "over-capacity worsens calibration" trend might reappear at larger widths than we tested. Temperature scaling's null result is specific to our validation set size (337 examples) and grid search; larger validation sets could plausibly detect a real but small effect.

## 6. Conclusion

In a controlled sweep of MLP width from 2 to 512 hidden units on a small classification benchmark, with 10 seeds per width and formal statistical tests, we find that calibration error is not monotonic across the small-capacity regime: there is a statistically robust peak of miscalibration at width 4–8 (t=16.0, p=3×10⁻¹⁵ vs. the width≥16 plateau), and the apparent exception — very good calibration at the most extreme width=2 setting — is shown directly to be a training-collapse artifact rather than genuine calibration, once collapsed and non-collapsed seeds are separated. Temperature scaling shows a consistent point-estimate ECE reduction but no statistically significant effect at our sample size. These findings are demonstrated on one dataset and one architecture family; we do not claim the specific width thresholds generalize, only the qualitative pattern (a non-monotonic small-width calibration curve, and the collapse-artifact risk) as something future studies of small-network calibration should check for directly rather than assume away.
