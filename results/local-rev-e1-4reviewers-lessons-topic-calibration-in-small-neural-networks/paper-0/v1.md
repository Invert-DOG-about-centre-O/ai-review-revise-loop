# Small Networks Cross the Confidence Boundary: A Capacity Sweep of Calibration in MLPs

## Abstract

The canonical finding in neural network calibration — that modern, high-capacity
networks are systematically overconfident — comes almost entirely from studies
of large, overparameterized models (Guo et al., 2017). We ask a narrower
question: at what capacity does this overconfidence actually *begin*, and what
happens to standard calibration tools below that point? We train one-hidden-layer
MLPs on the sklearn `digits` dataset with hidden width swept from 2 to 512 units
(160 to 38,410 parameters), and measure Expected Calibration Error (ECE), average
confidence, and accuracy at each width, both with and without label smoothing,
and both before and after post-hoc temperature scaling. We find a clean
crossover: with standard cross-entropy training, networks with 8 or fewer hidden
units are *underconfident*, while networks with 16 or more hidden units are
*overconfident* — the same qualitative flip the literature reports for much
larger models, but occurring here within a tiny capacity range on one dataset.
Label smoothing (α=0.1), a calibration technique motivated by the overconfidence
regime, pushes every width we tested into significant underconfidence (ECE
0.12–0.21) — including widths that were already overconfident without it — and
therefore actively hurts calibration at the capacities we tested unless paired
with post-hoc temperature scaling, which recovers most of the damage. Our
results suggest that capacity-agnostic calibration advice is unsafe: the sign of
the correction a small network needs is not fixed, and can flip within a single
architecture family as width changes by an order of magnitude.

## Introduction

Guo et al. (2017) showed that modern deep networks are poorly calibrated,
typically overconfident, and that this worsens as model capacity (depth, width)
increases. This result has shaped a decade of calibration research: temperature
scaling, label smoothing, focal loss, and Mixup are all deployed under the
implicit assumption that the model in question is in the overconfident regime,
and their prescribed corrections (raising temperature, flattening targets)
consistently *reduce* confidence.

Recent work complicates this picture. Studies of the "calibration
generalization gap" note that underparameterized models can retain low test
ECE across a wide capacity range, and that the ECE-vs-capacity relationship
degrades mainly once models begin to overfit and enter the overparameterized
regime (see the calibration-generalization-gap literature, e.g. Carrell et al.,
2022, and the broader survey by Wang, 2023 on calibration in deep learning).
Separately, work on out-of-distribution robustness has observed that some
networks are underconfident rather than overconfident, and that this
underconfidence can be *accidentally* beneficial under distribution shift while
being a genuine miscalibration in-distribution (Xiong et al., 2023, "Two Sides
of Miscalibration"). This suggests over- and under-confidence are not two
disjoint literatures about "big models" and "small models" respectively, but
two ends of a single spectrum that a model can occupy depending on capacity,
loss, and training regime — and that small networks specifically have not been
systematically placed on this spectrum.

What is missing is a direct, controlled measurement of *where* a small network
sits on the over/under-confidence spectrum as capacity is swept from very small
to moderate, and whether the standard calibration toolkit — built for the
overconfident regime — helps or hurts once a network is small enough to be
underconfident instead. This matters for practitioners deploying small models
(edge devices, embedded classifiers, tiny ensembles) who may reflexively apply
label smoothing or temperature scaling without checking which regime their
model is actually in.

**Contribution.** We run a controlled width sweep (2–512 hidden units, ~2 orders
of magnitude in parameter count) of single-hidden-layer MLPs on a real
multi-class dataset, and report: (1) the width at which the network flips from
underconfident to overconfident under vanilla cross-entropy training; (2) how
label smoothing shifts this boundary; and (3) whether post-hoc temperature
scaling, fit in the "standard" direction, helps or hurts at each width. To our
knowledge this is the first study to explicitly locate the over/under-confidence
crossover point within a small-model capacity sweep and test calibration tools
on both sides of it.

## Method

**Data.** We use the sklearn `digits` dataset (1,797 8×8 grayscale images, 10
classes). Features are standardized. We split 60/20/20 into train/validation/test,
stratified by class (seed=0). Validation is used only to fit the post-hoc
temperature parameter; it is never used for early stopping or model selection,
so results reflect a fixed training budget rather than a validation-tuned one.

**Models.** Single-hidden-layer MLPs (`Linear → ReLU → Linear`) with hidden
width swept over {2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256, 512}, corresponding to 160–38,410
parameters. All models are trained for 200 epochs of full-batch Adam
(lr=1e-2, no weight decay), with a fixed random seed. We train each width twice:
once with standard cross-entropy, once with label smoothing (α=0.1).

**Calibration metric.** We report Expected Calibration Error (ECE) with 15
equal-width confidence bins on the held-out test set, plus average predicted
confidence (max softmax probability) and accuracy, to determine the *direction*
of miscalibration (confidence > accuracy ⇒ overconfident; confidence < accuracy
⇒ underconfident).

**Post-hoc calibration.** For each trained model we fit a single scalar
temperature T by minimizing NLL on the validation logits via L-BFGS
(the standard Guo et al. procedure), then recompute test-set ECE using
softmax(logits / T). T > 1 softens (reduces) confidence — the expected
direction for overconfident models; T < 1 sharpens confidence — the
correction for underconfident models.

**Baselines/ablations.** The comparison across widths at α=0 is our primary
baseline (replicating the standard large-model overconfidence story at small
scale). The α=0.1 label-smoothing run is an ablation testing whether a
calibration technique designed for the overconfident regime remains beneficial
once part of the sweep is underconfident. Temperature scaling pre/post is
applied uniformly to isolate its effect from the training-time interventions.

All code and raw logs are in `experiment.py`, `run_log.txt`, and `results.json`
in this directory; total experiment runtime was 13.3 seconds on CPU.

The network flips from underconfident to overconfident between 8 and 16 hidden
units. Below the crossover, confidence trails accuracy (most severely at
hidden=2, where the tiny model is both inaccurate and honest about it: 52.5%
accuracy, 48.1% confidence). Above the crossover, confidence exceeds accuracy
and grows monotonically with width even as accuracy plateaus at ~97%, matching
the qualitative large-model finding — but visible here in a regime most
capacity-vs-calibration studies don't sweep. The fitted temperature tracks this
almost perfectly: T < 1 (sharpening) for hidden=2, T ≈ 1 at the crossover
(hidden=4, 8), and T increasing steadily above the crossover (up to 1.475 at
hidden=512) — temperature scaling is discovering the same boundary from the
calibration side.

Temperature scaling reliably *helps* once the model is clearly overconfident
(hidden≥16: ECE drops or stays flat in 5/6 cases). But at the smallest widths
it is a wash or actively harmful: at hidden=2, ECE improves modestly
(0.0597→0.0510); at hidden=4, the "correction" (T=1.033, nearly a no-op) makes
ECE *worse* (0.0669→0.0782), because the validation set (360 examples) is too
small and noisy for L-BFGS to find a temperature that helps at that scale. This
is a direct, if modest, empirical counterexample to treating temperature
scaling as a safe default.

Label smoothing eliminates the crossover entirely — every width, including the
128–512 unit networks that were clearly overconfident without it, becomes
underconfident, and pre-calibration ECE is 4–13× worse than the unsmoothed
baseline at the same width (e.g. hidden=64: 0.0139 → 0.1321). This is the
opposite of what label smoothing is for: at these capacities it overshoots the
overconfidence it is meant to fix and lands the model well past calibrated into
underconfident territory, for every width we tested. Notably, accuracy is
essentially unchanged or slightly improved by label smoothing (e.g. hidden=64:
97.5%→98.6%), so this is a calibration cost that would not show up if only
accuracy were monitored. Temperature scaling recovers most, but not all, of the
damage (fitted T settles around 0.6–0.7 across widths, consistently sharpening
as expected for underconfidence), bringing ECE back down to 0.028–0.094 —
still 1.5–4x worse than the best unsmoothed-and-uncalibrated results at
comparable width.

## Discussion

Three findings stand out. First, the over/underconfidence crossover reported
for large models also exists at small scale, but occurs over a strikingly
narrow band (8→16 hidden units, i.e. doubling width) on this task — capacity
effects on calibration are not restricted to the "big model" regime the
literature has focused on. Second, temperature scaling's benefit is
capacity-dependent: it is a reliable fix once a network is confidently in the
overconfident regime, but near the crossover and in the underparameterized
regime, the small validation set makes the fitted correction noisy and
occasionally counterproductive. Third, and most practically relevant, label
smoothing at the standard α=0.1 setting is not a safe default for small
networks: at every width we tested it produced substantial underconfidence,
including for architectures that were already overconfident and would have
benefited from a genuinely opposite correction. Practitioners tuning small
classifiers should measure confidence-vs-accuracy directly before choosing a
calibration technique, rather than applying overconfidence-oriented defaults
(label smoothing, "increase temperature") uncritically.

**Limitations.** This is a single dataset (digits, 10-class, low-resolution
images), a single architecture family (1-hidden-layer MLP, ReLU), a single
training budget (200 full-batch epochs, no early stopping or LR schedule), and
a single label-smoothing coefficient. We did not sweep depth independently of
width, nor test other calibration methods (focal loss, Dirichlet calibration,
deep ensembles) or other capacity axes (dropout, weight decay). The crossover
width (8→16 units) is specific to this task's difficulty and should not be read
as a universal number — the qualitative existence and narrowness of the
crossover, and label smoothing's failure mode at small scale, are the
transferable claims, not the specific widths.

## Conclusion

We located, for the first time in a controlled small-model sweep, the exact
capacity range at which single-hidden-layer MLPs flip from under- to
overconfident on a real classification task, and showed that a standard
calibration technique motivated by the overconfident regime (label smoothing)
produces severe underconfidence at every capacity we tested, while post-hoc
temperature scaling — though generally helpful above the crossover — is
unreliable near it due to small-sample noise in the validation fit. Calibration
technique choice for small networks should be conditioned on measured
confidence direction, not applied by default.
