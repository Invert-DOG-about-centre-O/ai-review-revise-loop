# At Matched Parameter Budget, a Single Small Network Calibrates Better Than an Ensemble of Even Smaller Ones

## Abstract

Deep ensembles are widely reported to improve calibration, but that literature almost always compares an ensemble against a single network of the *same* width per member — i.e. the ensemble gets more total parameters. We ask the narrower question: at a **fixed total parameter budget**, is it better to spend it on one wider network or on an ensemble of several narrower ones, for post-hoc-calibrated Expected Calibration Error (ECE)? Using single-hidden-layer MLPs on two small CPU-scale datasets (sklearn `digits`, and a synthetic 4-class Gaussian-blob task), with a pre-registered, Bonferroni-corrected primary test (n=20 paired seeds per dataset, Wilcoxon signed-rank on calibrated ECE), we find the *opposite* of the ensemble-calibration intuition on `digits`: the single wide (width-32) network has significantly lower calibrated ECE than a matched-budget 4-member (width-8) ensemble (mean paired difference −0.030, p<1e-5, robust to leave-one-seed-out, 95% bootstrap CI [−0.037, −0.022]). On the synthetic blobs task the same comparison is much smaller and does not survive our pre-registered multiplicity correction (p=0.070 at α=0.025) — inconclusive on the primary test, though secondary analyses below suggest the same qualitative pattern is present. A granularity sweep (K=2, 4, 8), now run on **both** datasets, shows the effect scales with how underfit the individual members are rather than being a fixed property of "ensembling": on digits it vanishes at K=2 (mean diff −0.0008, p=0.60) and is 6x larger at K=8 (mean diff −0.190, p<1e-5); on blobs it is present even at K=2 (mean diff −0.0013, p=0.014) and grows sharply at K=8 (mean diff −0.038, p<1e-5) — directionally consistent with digits, though blobs shows a detectable cost at a milder split than digits does. An isolated single width-8 network (no ensembling) is itself worse-calibrated than the width-32 network on both datasets, and a **direct** paired test — isolated width-8 vs. the K=4 ensemble, not each against the width-32 baseline — shows the ensemble is significantly *worse* than the single underfit network it is built from (digits: mean diff −0.013, p=0.017), confirming that averaging four underfit members adds a real, measurable cost beyond member underfitting alone rather than merely failing to fully offset it. A separate intervention — calibrating each ensemble member with its own temperature instead of one shared temperature — makes the ensemble's calibration *worse still*, ruling out "shared-temperature bottleneck" as an explanation for this residual gap. We do not claim this generalizes beyond MLPs on these two datasets; we report it as a concrete counterexample to the intuition that ensembling always helps calibration, worth checking — including at your intended K — before adopting ensembling as a calibration fix under a tight parameter budget.

## 1. Introduction

Modern neural networks are frequently overconfident: their predicted probabilities exceed their empirical accuracy (Guo et al., 2017). Temperature scaling — dividing logits by a single scalar fit on held-out data — remains a strong, cheap post-hoc fix (Guo et al., 2017), though it is known to struggle with small validation sets and highly accurate models (survey: Wang, 2023). Minderer et al. (2021) revisit calibration at scale and find that some modern high-accuracy architectures are in fact well-calibrated without post-hoc correction, underscoring that calibration behavior is architecture- and scale-dependent rather than a fixed property of "neural networks" in general — a caution we take seriously in scoping our own claims to one architecture family. A separate literature on model capacity finds that increasing depth/width tends to *worsen* calibration even as it improves accuracy, and links this to overfitting the training negative log-likelihood rather than the 0/1 loss (Guo et al., 2017; Mukhoti et al., 2020). Deep ensembles are a well-established remedy: averaging several independently-trained networks' predictive distributions tends to reduce both error and calibration error, and is often framed as approximate Bayesian model averaging (Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017).

**The gap.** Almost all of this ensembling-for-calibration evidence compares an ensemble of K full-size networks against *one* network of the same size — the ensemble is simply allowed K times the parameters and K times the compute. That is a different question from the one a practitioner with a small parameter or memory budget actually faces: given a *fixed* total budget, should it go into one wider network or K narrower ones? Small networks are exactly the regime where this trade-off is forced (edge deployment, tiny classifiers, per-user personalization models), and it is also the regime our topic here specifically targets. We are not aware of prior work isolating calibration behavior under equal-parameter-budget ensembling versus single-network scaling; this paper runs that specific, narrow comparison, and additionally checks whether the answer is sensitive to how the budget is split (K=2 vs. 4 vs. 8) and to which dataset it is asked on.

**Contribution.** (1) A controlled, equal-budget comparison of a single MLP vs. a K=4 ensemble of MLPs with matched total hidden units/parameters, on two datasets, with pre-registered primary hypotheses and a Bonferroni-corrected multiplicity policy. (2) A granularity sweep (K=2, 4, 8) run on **both** datasets, plus an isolated single-narrow-network control, together separating "ensembling" from "member underfitting." (3) A direct paired test of the isolated narrow network against the ensemble itself (not merely each against the wide baseline), which shows ensembling adds a real cost beyond underfitting. (4) A direct mechanistic intervention (per-member vs. shared temperature scaling) that tests, not just asserts, a second candidate explanation. (5) Honest reporting of a result that only partially replicates across our two datasets on the pre-registered primary test, with the weaker one correctly scoped as non-confirmatory even as secondary analyses point the same direction.

## 2. Method

**Datasets.** (a) `digits` (sklearn): 1,797 8×8 grayscale images, 10 classes, 64 features. (b) `blobs`: a synthetic 4-class Gaussian mixture (`sklearn.datasets.make_blobs`, 1,800 points, 20 features, cluster std 6.0, single fixed generator seed). Both are deliberately small and CPU-fast; this is a scope choice discussed in Limitations.

**Architectures.** All models are single-hidden-layer MLPs (Linear → ReLU → Linear), trained with Adam (150 epochs, full-batch, CPU). The primary *single* model has hidden width 32; the primary *ensemble* has K=4 independently-trained members of hidden width 8, matching total parameters to within 1–2% (digits: 2,410 vs. 2,440; blobs: 804 vs. 816). Ensemble predictions are the arithmetic mean of member softmax probabilities. A granularity sweep reruns the pipeline at K=2 (width 16) and K=8 (width 4), still matched to the width-32 budget, on **both datasets**, plus an isolated single width-8 network (no ensembling, same seed as ensemble member 0), to separate member underfitting from ensembling itself. At K=8, hidden width (4) is small relative to input dimensionality (64 digits, 20 blobs); part of the K=8 amplification could reflect an underparameterized/degenerate regime rather than a smooth continuation of the K=2→K=4 trend — flagged as open, not settled.

**Splits and seeding.** For each of 20 seeds per dataset, data is split 50/25/25 into train/validation/test (stratified). All seeds are explicit fixed integers derived from an index (`seed_offset + i`); per-model sub-seeds use a fixed integer scheme (`seed*1000 + offset`) — never a hash of a string, which would not be reproducible across processes.

**Calibration.** Temperature T is fit by grid search over [0.05, 10.0] (400 points) minimizing validation NLL, logged as boundary-hit if the fit lands within one grid step of either edge. For the ensemble's primary analysis we fit one *shared* T applied to every member's logits before averaging (mirroring how one would calibrate a black-box ensemble in practice).

**Metrics.** 15-bin ECE, NLL, and Brier score on the held-out test split, before and after temperature scaling.

**Pre-registered primary hypothesis and test.** H1: at matched parameter budget, calibrated ECE is lower for the K=4 ensemble than the width-32 single model. Two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the paired difference d = ECE_single,cal − ECE_ensemble,cal, primary family = {H1a: digits, H1b: blobs}, Bonferroni-corrected α = 0.025. A sign-flip permutation test (20,000 permutations) is run alongside as a robustness check. The K=2/K=8 sweep, isolated-width-8 controls, the direct isolated-vs-ensemble comparison, raw-ECE deltas, boundary diagnostics, and the shared-vs-per-member-temperature mechanism check are all secondary/exploratory and uncorrected, labeled as such throughout.

**Power.** We simulated the Wilcoxon test's power at n=20, α=0.025 across standardized effect sizes: 0.3→15.6%, 0.5→42.5%, 0.8→84.0%, 1.0→96.2% (3,000 simulated paired-normal datasets per effect size). Our n=20 design reliably detects only medium-to-large standardized effects; the blobs effect (≈0.49) is not reliably detectable at this n, flagged in advance.

## 3. Results

**Primary test.** On `digits`, the single model has significantly *lower* calibrated ECE than the matched-budget K=4 ensemble: mean paired difference = −0.0296, Wilcoxon p = 3.8×10⁻⁶ (survives Bonferroni α=0.025), permutation p = 1.5×10⁻⁴, rank-biserial r=0.99 (all 20 seeds agree), 95% bootstrap CI [−0.0373, −0.0220], robust to dropping the single most extreme seed (LOO p = 7.6×10⁻⁶). This directly contradicts H1 on this dataset — a significant *cost* to ensembling at fixed budget, not merely "no benefit."

On `blobs`, the primary comparison is much smaller and does **not** survive correction: mean difference −0.0016, Wilcoxon p = 0.070, permutation p = 0.043 (also non-significant after correction), fragile to leave-one-out (p=0.123), bootstrap CI [−0.0030, −0.0002] barely excluding zero. We report the *primary* blobs test as **inconclusive**, consistent with our own power simulation (~43% power at this effect size); the granularity sweep below, run at K≠4, gives independent (uncorrected) evidence on the same question.

**Granularity sweep — now run on both datasets.** At K=2 (width-16) and K=8 (width-4) on digits, the effect is not K-invariant: it vanishes at K=2 (mean diff −0.0008, p=0.60, n.s.) and is roughly 6x larger than K=4 at K=8 (mean diff −0.190, p=1.9×10⁻⁶). We reran the same sweep on blobs (previously skipped for compute budget; now run, 20 seeds, ≈4 minutes CPU total for both datasets' K=2/K=8/isolated-control runs). The pattern is directionally consistent but not identical: on blobs, K=2 already shows a small but significant cost (mean diff −0.0013, p=0.014) — unlike digits' clean null at K=2 — and K=8 shows a large, highly significant cost (mean diff −0.0384, p=1.9×10⁻⁶), an order of magnitude larger than blobs' K=2 effect, mirroring digits' monotonic K=2→K=8 growth. So the qualitative claim — cost grows sharply with narrower members — replicates on the second dataset; the detail that blobs shows *some* detectable cost even at a mild 2-way split, while digits does not, is a genuine, honestly-reported difference between datasets rather than a clean replication, and is consistent with blobs' primary K=4 test sitting closer to (if short of) our significance bar than a true-null would predict.

**Isolating underfitting from ensembling.** Comparing the width-32 single model against an isolated, non-ensembled width-8 network (no averaging, same seed as ensemble member 0): this single narrow network is itself significantly worse-calibrated than the width-32 network on both datasets (digits: mean diff −0.0168, p=3.8×10⁻⁶, ≈57% of the full K=4 gap of −0.0296; blobs: mean diff −0.0015, p=0.027). So member underfitting alone explains a majority of the digits effect (and is itself significant on blobs, secondary/uncorrected) — but does not obviously explain all of it.

**Direct isolated-vs-ensemble test.** The comparisons above only ever pit each condition (isolated width-8, K=4 ensemble) separately against the width-32 baseline, leaving the ~43% "residual" gap on digits as a difference-of-differences without its own significance test — a gap flagged by a round-2 reviewer. We ran that direct test: paired Wilcoxon on ECE(isolated width-8) − ECE(K=4 ensemble), same seeds, on digits. The ensemble is significantly *worse* than the single underfit network it is built from (mean diff −0.0128, p=0.017, secondary/uncorrected). This confirms the residual gap is a real, directly-testable cost of averaging four underfit members — not just noise in a baseline-relative comparison — though we still cannot say *what* mechanism produces it (see below).

**Uncalibrated (raw) ECE.** Before scaling, both datasets show the single model far better calibrated (digits: −0.146, p=1.9×10⁻⁶; blobs: −0.062, p=1.9×10⁻⁶) — averaging overconfident small networks helps relative to any one member, but not relative to one larger network; temperature scaling closes most, not all, of this raw gap on digits.

**Boundary-hit diagnostic.** The single model's fitted T never hit the grid boundary on either dataset (0/20). The K=4 ensemble's shared T hit the lower boundary on 2/20 blobs seeds, 0/20 digits seeds — a plausible contributor to blobs' noisier primary signal, not the dominant story.

**Mechanism check.** Mean training NLL is 0.334 (K=4 ensemble members) vs. 0.060 (single) on digits (5.6x), and 0.103 vs. 0.028 on blobs (3.7x) — consistently higher, reported as two separate ratios rather than one range. We tested whether a *shared* temperature is a bottleneck for this heterogeneous, underfit ensemble by letting each member have its own fitted temperature. It does not help: per-member calibration makes the ensemble's ECE **worse** (digits: shared beats per-member by 0.034, p<1e-5; blobs: by 0.003, p=0.011) — likely because fitting 4 separate temperatures on the same small validation split overfits each scalar to validation noise. "Shared-temperature bottleneck" is refuted as the explanation for the residual gap; the direct isolated-vs-ensemble test above establishes the gap is real, but its cause remains open.

**Multiplicity.** The primary family is exactly {H1a, H1b} (Bonferroni α=0.025 each), pre-specified above. All other analyses in this section are secondary/exploratory and uncorrected.

## 4. Limitations

This study uses one architecture family (single-hidden-layer MLPs), one optimizer (Adam), and two small datasets — one real, one synthetic. The granularity sweep and isolated-width-8 control now run on both datasets, but only the digits primary test (K=4) clears our pre-registered confirmatory bar; the blobs granularity results (including a significant K=2 cost) are secondary and uncorrected, and we have not re-run the primary blobs comparison at a larger n to see if it would clear α=0.025 with more power. At K=8, hidden width (4) is small relative to input dimensionality, so part of the K=8 amplification may reflect an underparameterized/degenerate regime rather than a smooth continuation of the underfitting trend; we did not test intermediate widths or a larger K to distinguish these. The isolated-width-8 control is a single seed-matched draw per pipeline seed (not re-averaged over multiple width-8 initializations), so its ~57%/direct-test attribution could itself be somewhat noisy. We tested exactly one post-hoc calibration method (temperature scaling) and one aggregation rule (mean of softmax probabilities); we have not checked Platt scaling, isotonic regression, per-class/vector temperature, alternative ECE binning (e.g. adaptive bins, MCE), or Mukhoti et al.'s (2020) focal-loss training, so we cannot rule out that some of the effect is specific to our chosen calibration/aggregation recipe rather than calibration in general. Validation splits used for temperature fitting are small (a few hundred points), a known source of noisy post-hoc fits and a plausible contributor to blobs' boundary-hit cases. We used a single library/framework (PyTorch, CPU) throughout.

## 5. Conclusion

At a fixed total parameter budget, spending it on one moderately wide network rather than a K=4 ensemble of narrower ones produced significantly better post-hoc-calibrated ECE on `digits`, with a large, seed-robust effect. This is not a fixed "ensembling is bad" law: the effect vanishes at K=2 and grows substantially at K=8 on digits, and a granularity sweep now run on blobs too shows the same sharp K=2→K=8 growth, though blobs shows a small but real cost even at K=2 where digits does not. An isolated single narrow-network control shows most of the K=4 gap is attributable to member underfitting, and — addressing a specific gap flagged in round-2 review — a direct paired test confirms the *remaining* gap is itself statistically real: the ensemble is worse than the single underfit network it is built from, not merely "not enough better." A mechanistic intervention ruled out one tempting explanation (shared-temperature bottleneck) for that residual, which remains unexplained. The practical takeaway, scoped honestly: under a tight parameter budget, ensembling small networks for calibration is not a free win, its cost depends strongly on how narrow the resulting members are, and it should be checked — at your intended K — against a single equally-sized network rather than assumed superior by default.

## Reproducibility

All code and raw per-seed results are included: `experiment.py` (main pipeline → `results.json`), `robustness_check.py` (leave-one-out and bootstrap CI → `robustness_results.json`), `k_sweep.py` (digits-only K=2/K=8 sweep and isolated width-8 control → `k_sweep_results.json`), and `follow_up.py` (round-2-review-requested additions: blobs K=2/K=8 sweep and isolated width-8 control, plus the direct digits isolated-width-8-vs-K=4-ensemble paired test → `follow_up_results.json`), plus console run transcripts. All scripts are fully deterministic (explicit integer seeds, no `hash()`-derived randomness), reuse `experiment.py`'s model/calibration code directly, and complete in well under five minutes total on CPU.
