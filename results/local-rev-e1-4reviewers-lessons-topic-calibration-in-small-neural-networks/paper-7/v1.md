# At Matched Parameter Budget, a Single Small Network Calibrates Better Than an Ensemble of Even Smaller Ones

## Abstract

Deep ensembles are widely reported to improve calibration, but that literature almost always compares an ensemble against a single network of the *same* width per member — i.e. the ensemble gets more total parameters. We ask the narrower question: at a **fixed total parameter budget**, is it better to spend it on one wider network or on an ensemble of several narrower ones, for post-hoc-calibrated Expected Calibration Error (ECE)? Using single-hidden-layer MLPs on two small CPU-scale datasets (sklearn `digits`, and a synthetic 4-class Gaussian-blob task), with a pre-registered, Bonferroni-corrected primary test (n=20 paired seeds per dataset, Wilcoxon signed-rank on calibrated ECE), we find the *opposite* of the ensemble-calibration intuition on `digits`: the single wide network has significantly lower calibrated ECE than a matched-budget 4-member ensemble (mean paired difference −0.030, p<1e-5, robust to leave-one-seed-out, 95% bootstrap CI [−0.037, −0.022]). On the synthetic blobs task the same comparison is much smaller and does not survive our pre-registered multiplicity correction (p=0.070 at α=0.025), and is fragile to leave-one-out (p=0.123) — we report it as inconclusive, not confirmatory. A mechanism check shows ensemble members substantially underfit relative to the single network at matched budget (5–6x higher training NLL), but a direct intervention — calibrating each ensemble member with its own temperature instead of one shared temperature — makes the ensemble's calibration *worse*, not better, ruling out "shared-temperature bottleneck" as the explanation. We do not claim this generalizes beyond MLPs on these two datasets; we report it as a concrete counterexample to the intuition that ensembling always helps calibration, worth checking before adopting ensembling as a calibration fix under a tight parameter budget.

## 1. Introduction

Modern neural networks are frequently overconfident: their predicted probabilities exceed their empirical accuracy (Guo et al., 2017). Temperature scaling — dividing logits by a single scalar fit on held-out data — remains a strong, cheap post-hoc fix (Guo et al., 2017), though it is known to struggle with small validation sets and highly accurate models (survey: Wang, 2023). A separate literature on model capacity finds that increasing depth/width tends to *worsen* calibration even as it improves accuracy, and links this to overfitting the training negative log-likelihood rather than the 0/1 loss (Guo et al., 2017; Mukhoti et al., 2020). Deep ensembles are a well-established remedy: averaging several independently-trained networks' predictive distributions tends to reduce both error and calibration error, and is often framed as approximate Bayesian model averaging (Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017).

**The gap.** Almost all of this ensembling-for-calibration evidence compares an ensemble of K full-size networks against *one* network of the same size — the ensemble is simply allowed K times the parameters and K times the compute. That is a different question from the one a practitioner with a small parameter or memory budget actually faces: given a *fixed* total budget, should it go into one wider network or K narrower ones? Small networks are exactly the regime where this trade-off is forced (edge deployment, tiny classifiers, per-user personalization models), and it is also the regime our topic here specifically targets. We are not aware of prior work isolating calibration behavior under equal-parameter-budget ensembling versus single-network scaling; this paper runs that specific, narrow comparison.

**Contribution.** (1) A controlled, equal-budget comparison of a single MLP vs. a K=4 ensemble of MLPs with matched total hidden units/parameters, on two datasets, with pre-registered primary hypotheses, a Bonferroni-corrected multiplicity policy, and a permutation-test robustness check alongside the Wilcoxon test. (2) A direct mechanistic intervention (per-member vs. shared temperature scaling) to test — not just assert — a candidate explanation for the result. (3) Honest reporting of a result that only partially replicates across our two datasets, with the weaker one correctly scoped as non-confirmatory rather than folded into a single overstated headline.

## 2. Method

**Datasets.** (a) `digits` (sklearn): 1,797 8×8 grayscale images, 10 classes, 64 features — a small real dataset. (b) `blobs`: a synthetic 4-class Gaussian mixture (`sklearn.datasets.make_blobs`, 1,800 points, 20 features, cluster std 6.0, single fixed generator seed) — a controlled sanity check with a different class count and feature dimensionality. Both are deliberately small and CPU-fast; this is a scope choice discussed in Limitations, not an oversight.

**Architectures.** All models are single-hidden-layer MLPs (Linear → ReLU → Linear), trained with Adam (150 epochs, full-batch, CPU). The *single* model has hidden width 32. The *ensemble* has K=4 independently-initialized-and-trained members, each of hidden width 8, so total hidden units (and, since there is only one hidden layer, total parameters: digits 2,410 vs. 2,440; blobs 804 vs. 816) are matched to within 1–2%. Ensemble predictions are the arithmetic mean of member softmax probabilities.

**Splits and seeding.** For each of 20 seeds per dataset, data is split 50/25/25 into train/validation/test (stratified). All seeds are explicit fixed integers derived from an index (`seed_offset + i`); per-model sub-seeds use a fixed integer scheme (`seed*1000 + offset`) — never a hash of a string, which would not be reproducible across processes. The single model and each of the 4 ensemble members are trained with their own seed, re-seeded immediately before that model's training call.

**Calibration.** Temperature T is fit by grid search over [0.05, 10.0] (400 points) minimizing validation NLL — a bounded search, so per-seed and per-condition we log whether the fitted T lands within one grid step of either boundary. For the ensemble's primary analysis we fit one *shared* T applied to every member's logits before averaging (mirroring how one would calibrate a black-box ensemble in practice).

**Metrics.** 15-bin ECE, NLL, and Brier score on the held-out test split, computed both before (raw) and after (calibrated) temperature scaling, for both single and ensemble models.

**Pre-registered primary hypothesis and test.** H1: at matched parameter budget, calibrated ECE is lower for the ensemble than the single model. Null: the paired difference d = ECE_single,cal − ECE_ensemble,cal is symmetric about 0. Two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test, primary family = {H1a: digits, H1b: blobs}, Bonferroni-corrected α = 0.05/2 = 0.025. A sign-flip permutation test (20,000 permutations) on the same paired differences is run as a robustness check alongside Wilcoxon. Everything else — raw (uncalibrated) ECE deltas, NLL/Brier deltas, boundary-hit diagnostics, the per-member-vs-shared-temperature mechanism check, and the leave-one-out / bootstrap robustness checks below — is secondary/exploratory and uncorrected, and is labeled as such throughout.

**Power.** We simulated the Wilcoxon test's power at n=20, α=0.025 (matching the primary design) across standardized effect sizes (mean difference / SD of differences): 0.3→15.6% power, 0.5→42.5%, 0.8→84.0%, 1.0→96.2% (3,000 simulated paired-normal datasets per effect size). This tells us in advance that our n=20 design can reliably detect only medium-to-large standardized effects; a small effect (like the one we observe on blobs, standardized effect ≈0.49) is not reliably detectable at this n, which we flag before, not after, seeing that this is exactly what happens.

## 3. Results

**Primary test.** On `digits`, the single model has significantly *lower* calibrated ECE than the matched-budget ensemble: mean paired difference (single − ensemble) = −0.0296 (single is better), Wilcoxon p = 3.8×10⁻⁶ (survives Bonferroni α=0.025), permutation p = 1.5×10⁻⁴, rank-biserial effect size r=0.99 (all 20 seeds go the same direction), 95% bootstrap CI for the mean difference [−0.0373, −0.0220], and it is robust to dropping the single most extreme seed (LOO Wilcoxon p = 7.6×10⁻⁶, LOO mean −0.0279). This directly contradicts H1 on this dataset — not merely "no benefit," but a significant *cost* to ensembling at fixed budget.

On `blobs`, the same comparison is much smaller and does **not** survive our pre-registered correction: mean difference −0.0016, Wilcoxon p = 0.070 (> α=0.025), permutation p = 0.043 (also not significant after correction). It is additionally fragile: dropping the single most extreme seed drops the Wilcoxon p to 0.123, and the bootstrap 95% CI [−0.0030, −0.0002] barely excludes zero. We report this as **inconclusive, not as a replication or a null result** — our own power simulation shows n=20 is underpowered for an effect this size (standardized effect ≈0.49, ~43% power), so "not significant" here should not be read as "no effect."

**Uncalibrated (raw) ECE — secondary.** Before temperature scaling, both datasets show the single model far better calibrated: digits mean raw difference −0.146 (Wilcoxon p=1.9×10⁻⁶), blobs −0.062 (p=1.9×10⁻⁶). This is expected — averaging K independently overconfident small networks does reduce raw ECE relative to any one member, but not relative to one larger network — and it shows post-hoc temperature scaling closes most, though not all, of the raw gap on digits.

**Boundary-hit diagnostic — secondary.** The single model's fitted T never hit the [0.05, 10.0] grid boundary on either dataset (0/20). The ensemble's shared T hit the lower boundary (T=0.05) on 2/20 blobs seeds (seeds 2007, 2012) and 0/20 digits seeds. This is a plausible contributor to blobs' noisier, weaker signal (a temperature pinned at the search floor is a sign the true optimum may be lower still), but is not the dominant story on digits, where the effect is large and no boundary hits occurred.

**Mechanism check — secondary, interventional.** Ensemble members substantially underfit relative to the single model at matched budget: mean training NLL is 0.334 (ensemble members, averaged) vs. 0.060 (single) on digits, and 0.103 vs. 0.028 on blobs — 5–6x higher. This is consistent with the capacity/underfitting literature and a natural candidate explanation. We tested one specific mechanistic hypothesis this suggests: that a single *shared* temperature is a bottleneck for a heterogeneous, underfit ensemble, and that letting each member have its *own* fitted temperature (still averaged afterward) would close the gap. We ran this as a direct intervention rather than asserting it. The result refutes the hypothesis: per-member temperature calibration makes the ensemble's ECE **worse**, not better, than shared-temperature calibration (digits: shared beats per-member by 0.034 ECE, p<1e-5; blobs: shared beats per-member by 0.003 ECE, p=0.011) — likely because fitting 4 separate temperatures on the same small validation split overfits the per-member scalar to validation noise. So while underfitting is a real, measured property of the small ensemble members, "shared-temperature bottleneck" is not the mechanism, and we report that refutation plainly rather than dropping the check.

**Multiplicity.** Our primary family is exactly {H1a, H1b} (2 tests, Bonferroni α=0.025 each), pre-specified above. All other tests reported in this section (raw ECE, boundary diagnostics, the two mechanism-check comparisons, LOO, bootstrap) are secondary/exploratory and are not corrected; we flag this explicitly rather than implying they carry confirmatory weight.

## 4. Limitations

This study uses one architecture family (single-hidden-layer MLPs), one ensemble size (K=4), one optimizer (Adam), and two small datasets — one real, one synthetic. We do not claim the direction of the effect generalizes to deeper networks, CNNs, other ensemble sizes, or larger datasets; per our own pre-registered design, only the `digits` result clears our confirmatory bar, and even that is a single-architecture, single-dataset finding. The blobs result is honestly reported as underpowered/inconclusive rather than as a second confirmation. We used a single library/framework (PyTorch, CPU) throughout — a spot-check in a different framework was out of scope for this budget. The validation splits used for temperature fitting are small (a few hundred points), which is a known source of noisy post-hoc fits and is a plausible contributor to the boundary-hit cases on blobs.

## 5. Conclusion

At a fixed total parameter budget, spending it on one moderately wide network rather than an ensemble of several narrower ones produced significantly better post-hoc-calibrated ECE on a small real image dataset (`digits`), with a large, seed-robust effect — the opposite of what the general "ensembles improve calibration" intuition would predict once the comparison is budget-matched rather than budget-inflated. The same comparison on a synthetic dataset trended the same direction but did not clear our pre-registered significance bar and should be treated as inconclusive. A direct intervention ruled out one tempting mechanistic story (shared-temperature bottleneck) even though the underfitting pattern that motivated it was real and measured. The practical takeaway, scoped honestly to our setup: under a tight parameter budget, ensembling small networks for calibration is not a free win and should be checked against a single equally-sized network, not assumed superior by default.

## Reproducibility

All code and raw per-seed results are included alongside this paper: `experiment.py` (main pipeline: data, training, calibration, primary tests, power simulation — produces `results.json`, which contains every statistic quoted above under discoverable keys in `primary_stats` and `per_seed_results`), `robustness_check.py` (leave-one-out and bootstrap CI, produces `robustness_results.json`), and `run_log.txt`-equivalent console output reproduced in this repository's run transcript. Re-running `python experiment.py` is fully deterministic (explicit integer seeds throughout, no `hash()`-derived randomness) and completes in under two minutes on CPU.
