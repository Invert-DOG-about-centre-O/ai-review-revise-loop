# At Matched Parameter Budget, a Single Small Network Calibrates Better Than an Ensemble of Even Smaller Ones

## Abstract

Deep ensembles are widely reported to improve calibration, but that literature almost always compares an ensemble against a single network of the *same* width per member — i.e. the ensemble gets more total parameters. We ask the narrower question: at a **fixed total parameter budget**, is it better to spend it on one wider network or on an ensemble of several narrower ones, for post-hoc-calibrated Expected Calibration Error (ECE)? Using single-hidden-layer MLPs on two small CPU-scale datasets (sklearn `digits`, and a synthetic 4-class Gaussian-blob task), with a pre-registered, Bonferroni-corrected primary test (n=20 paired seeds per dataset, Wilcoxon signed-rank on calibrated ECE), we find the *opposite* of the ensemble-calibration intuition on `digits`: the single wide (width-32) network has significantly lower calibrated ECE than a matched-budget 4-member (width-8) ensemble (mean paired difference −0.030, p<1e-5, robust to leave-one-seed-out, 95% bootstrap CI [−0.037, −0.022]). On the synthetic blobs task the same comparison is much smaller and does not survive our pre-registered multiplicity correction (p=0.070 at α=0.025) — inconclusive, not confirmatory. A follow-up sweep over ensemble granularity on digits shows this is *not* a fixed property of "ensembling": at K=2 (width-16 members, mildly narrower) the effect vanishes (mean diff −0.0008, p=0.60), while at K=8 (width-4, severely narrower) it is far larger (mean diff −0.190, p<1e-5) — the single-beats-ensemble effect scales with how underfit the individual members are, not with ensembling per se. Consistent with this, a single *non-ensembled* width-8 network is itself significantly worse-calibrated than the width-32 network (mean diff −0.017, p<1e-5), showing much — but not all — of the K=4 gap is attributable to member underfitting rather than an ensembling-specific mechanism. A separate intervention — calibrating each K=4 ensemble member with its own temperature instead of one shared temperature — makes the ensemble's calibration *worse*, ruling out "shared-temperature bottleneck" as an explanation for the residual gap. We do not claim this generalizes beyond MLPs on these two datasets; we report it as a concrete counterexample to the intuition that ensembling always helps calibration, worth checking — including at your intended K — before adopting ensembling as a calibration fix under a tight parameter budget.

## 1. Introduction

Modern neural networks are frequently overconfident: their predicted probabilities exceed their empirical accuracy (Guo et al., 2017). Temperature scaling — dividing logits by a single scalar fit on held-out data — remains a strong, cheap post-hoc fix (Guo et al., 2017), though it is known to struggle with small validation sets and highly accurate models (survey: Wang, 2023). Minderer et al. (2021) revisit calibration at scale and find that some modern high-accuracy architectures are in fact well-calibrated without post-hoc correction, underscoring that calibration behavior is architecture- and scale-dependent rather than a fixed property of "neural networks" in general — a caution we take seriously in scoping our own claims to one architecture family. A separate literature on model capacity finds that increasing depth/width tends to *worsen* calibration even as it improves accuracy, and links this to overfitting the training negative log-likelihood rather than the 0/1 loss (Guo et al., 2017; Mukhoti et al., 2020). Deep ensembles are a well-established remedy: averaging several independently-trained networks' predictive distributions tends to reduce both error and calibration error, and is often framed as approximate Bayesian model averaging (Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017).

**The gap.** Almost all of this ensembling-for-calibration evidence compares an ensemble of K full-size networks against *one* network of the same size — the ensemble is simply allowed K times the parameters and K times the compute. That is a different question from the one a practitioner with a small parameter or memory budget actually faces: given a *fixed* total budget, should it go into one wider network or K narrower ones? Small networks are exactly the regime where this trade-off is forced (edge deployment, tiny classifiers, per-user personalization models), and it is also the regime our topic here specifically targets. We are not aware of prior work isolating calibration behavior under equal-parameter-budget ensembling versus single-network scaling; this paper runs that specific, narrow comparison, and additionally checks whether the answer is sensitive to how the budget is split (K=2 vs. 4 vs. 8).

**Contribution.** (1) A controlled, equal-budget comparison of a single MLP vs. a K=4 ensemble of MLPs with matched total hidden units/parameters, on two datasets, with pre-registered primary hypotheses and a Bonferroni-corrected multiplicity policy. (2) A granularity sweep (K=2, 4, 8) and an isolated single-narrow-network control that together separate "ensembling" from "member underfitting" as candidate explanations. (3) A direct mechanistic intervention (per-member vs. shared temperature scaling) to test — not just assert — a second candidate explanation. (4) Honest reporting of a result that only partially replicates across our two datasets, with the weaker one correctly scoped as non-confirmatory.

## 2. Method

**Datasets.** (a) `digits` (sklearn): 1,797 8×8 grayscale images, 10 classes, 64 features. (b) `blobs`: a synthetic 4-class Gaussian mixture (`sklearn.datasets.make_blobs`, 1,800 points, 20 features, cluster std 6.0, single fixed generator seed). Both are deliberately small and CPU-fast; this is a scope choice discussed in Limitations.

**Architectures.** All models are single-hidden-layer MLPs (Linear → ReLU → Linear), trained with Adam (150 epochs, full-batch, CPU). The primary *single* model has hidden width 32. The primary *ensemble* has K=4 independently-initialized-and-trained members, each of hidden width 8, so total parameters are matched to within 1–2% (digits: 2,410 vs. 2,440; blobs: 804 vs. 816). Ensemble predictions are the arithmetic mean of member softmax probabilities. A follow-up sweep (Section 3) reruns the same pipeline at K=2 (width 16) and K=8 (width 4), still matched to the width-32 budget, plus an isolated single width-8 network (no ensembling) trained with the same seed as ensemble member 0, to separate the contribution of member underfitting from ensembling itself.

**Splits and seeding.** For each of 20 seeds per dataset, data is split 50/25/25 into train/validation/test (stratified). All seeds are explicit fixed integers derived from an index (`seed_offset + i`); per-model sub-seeds use a fixed integer scheme (`seed*1000 + offset`) — never a hash of a string, which would not be reproducible across processes.

**Calibration.** Temperature T is fit by grid search over [0.05, 10.0] (400 points) minimizing validation NLL, logged as boundary-hit if the fit lands within one grid step of either edge. For the ensemble's primary analysis we fit one *shared* T applied to every member's logits before averaging (mirroring how one would calibrate a black-box ensemble in practice).

**Metrics.** 15-bin ECE, NLL, and Brier score on the held-out test split, before and after temperature scaling.

**Pre-registered primary hypothesis and test.** H1: at matched parameter budget, calibrated ECE is lower for the K=4 ensemble than the width-32 single model. Two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the paired difference d = ECE_single,cal − ECE_ensemble,cal, primary family = {H1a: digits, H1b: blobs}, Bonferroni-corrected α = 0.025. A sign-flip permutation test (20,000 permutations) is run alongside as a robustness check. The K=2/K=8 sweep, the isolated width-8 control, raw-ECE deltas, boundary diagnostics, and the per-member-vs-shared-temperature mechanism check are secondary/exploratory and uncorrected, labeled as such throughout.

**Power.** We simulated the Wilcoxon test's power at n=20, α=0.025 across standardized effect sizes: 0.3→15.6%, 0.5→42.5%, 0.8→84.0%, 1.0→96.2% (3,000 simulated paired-normal datasets per effect size). Our n=20 design reliably detects only medium-to-large standardized effects; the blobs effect (≈0.49) is not reliably detectable at this n, flagged in advance.

## 3. Results

**Primary test.** On `digits`, the single model has significantly *lower* calibrated ECE than the matched-budget K=4 ensemble: mean paired difference = −0.0296, Wilcoxon p = 3.8×10⁻⁶ (survives Bonferroni α=0.025), permutation p = 1.5×10⁻⁴, rank-biserial r=0.99 (all 20 seeds agree), 95% bootstrap CI [−0.0373, −0.0220], robust to dropping the single most extreme seed (LOO p = 7.6×10⁻⁶). This directly contradicts H1 on this dataset — a significant *cost* to ensembling at fixed budget, not merely "no benefit."

On `blobs`, the comparison is much smaller and does **not** survive correction: mean difference −0.0016, Wilcoxon p = 0.070, permutation p = 0.043 (also non-significant after correction), fragile to leave-one-out (p=0.123), bootstrap CI [−0.0030, −0.0002] barely excluding zero. We report this as **inconclusive**, consistent with our own power simulation (~43% power at this effect size).

**Granularity sweep — secondary, addresses whether K=4 is a special case.** Reviewers of an earlier draft asked whether the digits effect depends on the specific K=4 split rather than reflecting a general budget-matching phenomenon. We reran the digits pipeline (same 20 seeds, same splits) at K=2 (width-16 members) and K=8 (width-4 members), matched to the same width-32 budget. The result is *not* K-invariant: at K=2, the single-vs-ensemble gap effectively disappears (mean diff −0.0008, Wilcoxon p=0.60, n.s.); at K=8, it is roughly 6x larger than at K=4 (mean diff −0.190, p=1.9×10⁻⁶). So the effect is not "ensembling is bad" in general — it scales strongly with how narrow (underfit) the individual members are at a given K, and a mild 2-way split (width 16 vs. 32) shows no detectable cost at our sample size.

**Isolating underfitting from ensembling — secondary, interventional.** To test whether the K=4 gap is really about *ensembling* or simply about training a width-8 network at all, we compared the width-32 single model against one *isolated, non-ensembled* width-8 network (no averaging, same seed as ensemble member 0). This single narrow network is itself significantly worse-calibrated than the width-32 network (mean diff −0.0168, p=3.8×10⁻⁶) — about 57% of the full K=4 ensemble gap (−0.0296). So member underfitting alone accounts for a majority of the effect, but averaging four such underfit members does not recover the rest of the gap and, if anything, the remaining ~43% suggests averaging adds a further small cost beyond what underfitting alone predicts, on this dataset.

**Uncalibrated (raw) ECE — secondary.** Before scaling, both datasets show the single model far better calibrated (digits: −0.146, p=1.9×10⁻⁶; blobs: −0.062, p=1.9×10⁻⁶) — averaging overconfident small networks helps relative to any one member, but not relative to one larger network; temperature scaling closes most, not all, of this raw gap on digits.

**Boundary-hit diagnostic — secondary.** The single model's fitted T never hit the grid boundary on either dataset (0/20). The K=4 ensemble's shared T hit the lower boundary on 2/20 blobs seeds, 0/20 digits seeds — a plausible contributor to blobs' noisier signal, not the dominant story on digits.

**Mechanism check — secondary, interventional.** Mean training NLL is 0.334 (K=4 ensemble members) vs. 0.060 (single) on digits (5.6x), and 0.103 vs. 0.028 on blobs (3.7x) — consistently higher, though the "5–6x" range in an earlier draft understated how much smaller the blobs ratio is; we now report the two ratios separately rather than as a single range. We tested whether a single *shared* temperature is a bottleneck for this heterogeneous, underfit ensemble, and whether letting each member have its *own* fitted temperature would close the gap. It does not: per-member calibration makes the ensemble's ECE **worse** (digits: shared beats per-member by 0.034, p<1e-5; blobs: by 0.003, p=0.011) — likely because fitting 4 separate temperatures on the same small validation split overfits each scalar to validation noise. So "shared-temperature bottleneck" is refuted as the explanation for the residual (post-underfitting) gap; the granularity sweep and isolated-width-8 control above are the more informative decomposition of where the effect comes from.

**Multiplicity.** The primary family is exactly {H1a, H1b} (Bonferroni α=0.025 each), pre-specified above. All other analyses in this section (granularity sweep, isolated control, raw ECE, boundary diagnostics, mechanism check, LOO, bootstrap) are secondary/exploratory and uncorrected.

## 4. Limitations

This study uses one architecture family (single-hidden-layer MLPs), one optimizer (Adam), and two small datasets — one real, one synthetic. Beyond K=4, we checked K=2 and K=8 only on digits (not blobs, for compute budget); we do not know whether the K=2 null result or the K=8 amplification replicate on blobs or on other datasets. We tested exactly one post-hoc calibration method (temperature scaling) and one aggregation rule (mean of softmax probabilities); we have not checked Platt scaling, isotonic regression, per-class/vector temperature, or Mukhoti et al.'s (2020) focal-loss training, so we cannot rule out that some of the digits effect is specific to shared-temperature-scaling-on-averaged-probabilities rather than calibration in general. Per our pre-registered design, only the `digits` result clears our confirmatory bar; blobs is honestly reported as underpowered/inconclusive. Validation splits used for temperature fitting are small (a few hundred points), a known source of noisy post-hoc fits and a plausible contributor to blobs' boundary-hit cases. We used a single library/framework (PyTorch, CPU) throughout.

## 5. Conclusion

At a fixed total parameter budget, spending it on one moderately wide network rather than a K=4 ensemble of narrower ones produced significantly better post-hoc-calibrated ECE on `digits`, with a large, seed-robust effect. This is not a fixed "ensembling is bad" law: a granularity sweep shows the effect vanishes at K=2 and grows substantially at K=8, and an isolated single narrow-network control shows most (not all) of the K=4 gap is attributable to member underfitting rather than ensembling per se. The synthetic blobs comparison trends the same direction but did not clear our pre-registered significance bar and should be treated as inconclusive. A direct intervention ruled out one tempting mechanistic story (shared-temperature bottleneck) for the residual gap. The practical takeaway, scoped honestly: under a tight parameter budget, ensembling small networks for calibration is not a free win, its cost depends strongly on how narrow the resulting members are, and it should be checked — at your intended K — against a single equally-sized network rather than assumed superior by default.

## Reproducibility

All code and raw per-seed results are included: `experiment.py` (main pipeline: data, training, calibration, primary tests, power simulation → `results.json`), `robustness_check.py` (leave-one-out and bootstrap CI → `robustness_results.json`), and `k_sweep.py` (the K=2/K=8 granularity sweep and isolated width-8 control described in Section 3 → `k_sweep_results.json`), plus console run transcripts. All three scripts are fully deterministic (explicit integer seeds, no `hash()`-derived randomness); `k_sweep.py` reuses `experiment.py`'s model/calibration code directly and completes in under two minutes on CPU.
