# Capacity-Dependent Miscalibration: Small Neural Networks Are Underconfident, Not Overconfident

## Abstract

The literature on neural network calibration is dominated by a single narrative: modern, high-capacity networks are overconfident, and post-hoc temperature scaling (T > 1) fixes it. We ask whether this holds at the small end of the capacity spectrum. Using a synthetic 3-class Gaussian-discriminant task with an analytically known Bayes posterior, we train 2-hidden-layer MLPs with widths from 2 to 256 units and fit the optimal post-hoc temperature T* for each, with 10 seeds per width. We find a capacity-dependent crossover: the smallest networks (width 2) have T* = 0.44 ± 0.18 (underconfident), while the largest (width ≥ 64) plateau at T* ≈ 2.40 (overconfident), with the crossing (T*=1) between widths 8 and 16 on this task instance — Welch's t-tests confirm a sharp, significant rise there (p=1.9e-4 and p=2.0e-6) bracketed by flat plateaus on both sides, and this jump is unchanged when we double the seed count (10→20; p=1.9e-9). A second, independently-drawn Gaussian instance replicates the qualitative sign-flip pattern but shifts the crossover to below width 4 and the plateau magnitude to ≈4.2 vs 2.4 — so we now state explicitly that *only the sign-flip-with-capacity phenomenon is shown to generalize; the specific crossover width and its "pinning" are properties of one task instance, not a portable number.* Two further checks probe whether small-network underconfidence is a true capacity effect: training width-2 networks 5x longer (400 vs. 80 epochs) does not significantly change T* (0.44 vs. 0.59, p=0.29), and directly checking training-set fit shows width-2 networks already reach 99.2% train accuracy by epoch 80 (99.3% at epoch 400, loss 0.036→0.023) — they are not grossly undertrained, so underconfidence is not simply a fitting artifact. Extending the label-smoothing ablation to all seven widths, including the previously-omitted crossover-region widths 8 and 32, label smoothing pushes T* below 1 everywhere (width 8: 0.65→0.28; width 32: 2.19→0.33) and inflates raw ECE by roughly 6–15x, with the two largest fold-changes (width 4: 16x, width 8: 15x) both explained by unusually small no-LS baseline ECE rather than a distinct effect. A single free (sign-unconstrained) temperature fit on a small held-out set corrects all cases to near-Bayes calibration. Code, logs, and raw results are included for reproducibility.

## Introduction

Guo et al. (2017) established that modern deep networks are typically overconfident and that temperature scaling (T > 1) is a simple, effective fix — close to folklore: "big networks are overconfident, use T > 1." But the models studied there — ResNets, DenseNets with millions of parameters — sit at one extreme of the capacity spectrum. Small networks (few hundred to a few thousand parameters), used on-device, in tabular pipelines, or as calibration-sensitive components of larger systems, are much less studied. There are reasons to expect qualitatively different behavior: a small network trained with cross-entropy is capacity- and optimization-limited, and cross-entropy pushes predictions toward one-hot targets; a model that *cannot* fit the training data perfectly will often produce blunter (lower-confidence) outputs than one that overfits — the opposite miscalibration sign. A natural objection is that this is really an *optimization* effect (small nets undertrained) rather than a *capacity* effect; we test this directly below, both via longer training and by directly checking training-set fit.

### Related work

Guo et al. (2017) introduced ECE and showed temperature scaling corrects overconfidence in large image classifiers. Follow-up work on adaptive/attended temperature scaling (2018; 2022) refines *how* T is chosen but keeps T > 1 as the assumption. Minderer et al. (2021) found some newer architecture families are much better calibrated than classic CNNs even at large scale, suggesting capacity alone doesn't fully determine calibration direction. Recent work on foundation models (2025) found some large *pretrained* models are actually **underconfident** in-distribution, attributed to architecture/training rather than capacity alone. Wang et al. (2023), "Two Sides of Miscalibration," argue underconfidence is understudied relative to overconfidence. On the small/low-data side, prior work notes temperature scaling degrades when the calibration set is small, motivating lower-parameter alternatives, but no prior study directly sweeps model *width* on a controlled task with a known Bayes posterior, across seeds and data instances, with a formal significance test for *where* the crossover occurs. That is the gap we address.

## Method

**Task.** A synthetic 3-class Gaussian discriminant analysis (QDA) problem in D=8 dimensions: each class k has mean μ_k and diagonal class-specific covariance Σ_k, giving a quadratic Bayes-optimal boundary while the true posterior P(y|x) is available in closed form, enabling a **Bayes-ECE**: mean absolute difference between predicted and true posterior confidence — free of binning/finite-sample noise.

**Data.** 2,000 train / 800 val / 2,000 test points, i.i.d. per data instance.

**Models.** 2-hidden-layer ReLU MLPs, widths {2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 256}, Adam (lr=1e-2, weight decay 1e-5), 80 epochs, batch size 256, plain cross-entropy.

**Seeds and robustness.** 10 training seeds per width on the main data instance, plus 10 additional seeds (10–19) at widths 8 and 16 specifically to test whether the sharp jump there is an artifact of the original seed set; a second, independently-drawn data-generating instance (different class means/variances, same regime) with 5 seeds per width, to separate task-instance artifacts from the general pattern.

**Convergence checks.** To test whether width-2 underconfidence reflects true capacity limits or merely undertraining, we (a) retrain width-2 networks for 400 epochs (5x) with 10 seeds and compare T*, and (b) directly measure training-set accuracy and loss at epoch 80 vs. 400 to check whether the model was still improving on its own training objective at the point T* is measured.

**Calibration metrics.** For each model: accuracy, 15-bin ECE, Bayes-ECE, and T* found by grid search over T ∈ [0.05, 10] (200-point geometric grid) minimizing validation NLL, unconstrained (T can be < 1).

**Significance testing.** t-based 95% CIs (df=9) and Welch's two-sample t-test between adjacent widths, and, for the width 8 vs 16 jump specifically, a pooled test at n=20 per width using the extra seed batch.

**Label-smoothing ablation.** ε=0.1 at all seven widths, including 8 and 32 (added this revision to cover the crossover region), 10 seeds each (main instance).

## Results

Table 1: capacity sweep, mean ± SD over 10 seeds (main data instance), with t-distribution (df=9) 95% CI half-widths.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2   | 0.990 | 0.012 | 0.018 | **0.44 ± 0.18** | ±0.13 | 0.007 |
| 4   | 0.991 | 0.004 | 0.008 | **0.63 ± 0.12** | ±0.09 | 0.007 |
| 8   | 0.990 | 0.005 | 0.007 | **0.65 ± 0.23** | ±0.16 | 0.007 |
| 16  | 0.988 | 0.008 | 0.010 | **1.26 ± 0.33** | ±0.24 | 0.007 |
| 32  | 0.987 | 0.011 | 0.011 | **2.19 ± 0.26** | ±0.18 | 0.007 |
| 64  | 0.987 | 0.011 | 0.011 | **2.41 ± 0.55** | ±0.40 | 0.008 |
| 256 | 0.988 | 0.011 | 0.011 | **2.40 ± 0.19** | ±0.14 | 0.008 |

T* is essentially flat from width 2–8 (0.44 to 0.65) and from width 32–256 (2.19 to 2.41), with the T*=1 crossing occurring **between width 8 and width 16**. Welch's t-tests between adjacent widths: width 2 vs 4 (t=−2.70, p=0.016), width 4 vs 8 (t=−0.28, p=0.78, a genuine low-end plateau), a sharp rise at width 8 vs 16 (t=−4.83, p=1.9e-4) and width 16 vs 32 (t=−7.05, p=2.0e-6), then a flat high-end plateau (32 vs 64: p=0.30; 64 vs 256: p=0.98). Accuracy stays flat (98.7–99.1%) throughout, so the T* trend is not confounded by task difficulty.

**Is the width 8→16 jump a seed-set artifact? (new).** A reviewer asked whether the sharp jump reflects the specific seeds 0–9 rather than a real discontinuity. We trained 10 additional seeds (10–19) at both widths: width 8 gives T* = 0.685 ± 0.234 and width 16 gives T* = 1.307 ± 0.180, closely matching the original batch. Pooling both batches (n=20 per width), the difference remains highly significant (t=−7.88, p=1.9e-9), essentially unchanged from the n=10 result. The jump is not a small-sample seed artifact.

**Is this capacity or just undertraining?** We retrained width-2 networks for 400 epochs (10 seeds): T* = 0.59 ± 0.37 vs. 0.44 ± 0.18 at 80 epochs — not a significant difference (p=0.29), and still clearly below 1 in both cases. We also directly checked whether width-2 networks were still fitting their training data at epoch 80: train accuracy was already 99.24% ± 0.14% at epoch 80 versus 99.30% ± 0.08% at epoch 400 (train loss 0.036 ± 0.016 → 0.023 ± 0.003) — a small further reduction in loss but an accuracy plateau, indicating the network is close to converged on the training objective well before epoch 80, not grossly underfit. Together these are evidence (not proof, since only one width/budget pair was tested) against pure undertraining as the explanation for width-2 underconfidence, consistent with the capacity-based account in the title.

Table 2: label smoothing (ε=0.1), full width sweep including the crossover region, mean over 10 seeds (main data instance).

| Width | T* (no LS) | T* (LS) | ECE (no LS) | ECE (LS) | ECE fold-change |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2   | 0.44 | **0.14** | 0.012 | 0.093 | 7.7x |
| 4   | 0.63 | **0.19** | 0.004 | 0.071 | 16.2x |
| 8   | 0.65 | **0.28** | 0.005 | 0.069 | 14.7x |
| 16  | 1.26 | **0.27** | 0.008 | 0.067 | 8.1x |
| 32  | 2.19 | **0.33** | 0.011 | 0.065 | 5.9x |
| 64  | 2.41 | **0.37** | 0.011 | 0.065 | 6.0x |
| 256 | 2.40 | **0.41** | 0.011 | 0.067 | 6.0x |

With widths 8 and 32 added, label smoothing drives T* below 1 at *every* width, including width 32/64/256, the most strongly overconfident settings without it — the interaction does not behave differently in the crossover region than at the plateaus. Two reviewers independently asked why the crossover-region widths were missing from this ablation; the completed table shows the same qualitative story holds there. As before, the ECE fold-change is not uniform: widths 4 and 8 (16.2x, 14.7x) are outliers relative to the other five widths (≈6–8x), and in both cases this traces to an unusually small no-LS baseline ECE (0.0044 and 0.0047, the two smallest in Table 1) rather than a qualitatively different LS effect — the same absolute LS-induced ECE lands as a much larger ratio. This raw-ECE inflation is largely a mechanical consequence of label smoothing raising prediction entropy uniformly (regardless of correctness) rather than a new failure mode; the width-dependent trend in T*(LS) itself (0.14→0.41) shows the underlying capacity effect persists underneath the entropy shift. A single fitted temperature still restores near-Bayes calibration in every case.

**Robustness of the crossover width.** Repeating the no-LS sweep on an independently-drawn second Gaussian instance (5 seeds/width) gives T* = 0.90, 1.14, 2.15, 4.18, 4.29 at widths 2, 4, 16, 64, 256 respectively (accuracy 97.5–98.1%). The direction replicates exactly, but the crossover point shifts from ≈8–16 (instance 1) to **below width 4**, and the plateau value nearly doubles (4.2 vs 2.4). Given this, and as a reviewer suggested, we soften our earlier framing: the contribution of the denser sampling and t-tests is *localizing the crossover on this specific task instance with statistical rigor*, not identifying a crossover width that transfers across tasks. Only the sign-flip-with-capacity pattern is shown to generalize across the two instances tested.

## Discussion and limitations

This remains a small, synthetic, low-dimensional (D=8, ~99% Bayes accuracy) study using only feedforward MLPs, one optimizer/learning-rate/depth configuration, and n=2 independently-drawn task instances — enough to reject "the crossover width is fixed" but not to characterize *what* task property (separability, dimensionality, condition number of Σ_k) predicts it; that requires a systematic sweep over task difficulty, left to future work along with real-data and convolutional/transformer validation. One plausible (untested) mechanistic hypothesis for why the transition concentrates in a narrow width range is that it reflects a shift in which regularization pressure dominates: at very small widths the network is representationally constrained (blunting confidence), while beyond some width the effective capacity to fit training labels sharply increases and cross-entropy's pull toward one-hot outputs starts to dominate — but we have not tested this directly. On undertraining, we now have two checks at width 2 (5x more epochs; direct train-accuracy/loss measurement) that both argue against a pure optimization confound, but only at one width and one training budget; we did not vary learning rate, optimizer, or depth, and did not test whether large-width overconfidence is itself sensitive to the fixed 80-epoch recipe. What we believe generalizes is the qualitative claim — capacity determines the *sign*, not just magnitude, of miscalibration on this task family — since it replicated under a different Gaussian draw, denser width sampling, a doubled seed count at the crossover, and formal significance testing; the specific crossover location does not generalize and should not be read as a portable number.

## Conclusion

On a controlled synthetic task with a known Bayes posterior, small neural networks are systematically underconfident (T*<1) while wide networks are overconfident (T*>1). Denser width sampling and Welch's t-tests localize the crossover to between width 8 and 16 on this task instance, and this localization is robust to doubling the seed count (p=1.9e-9 at n=20). Two convergence checks — 5x longer training, and direct training-set fit measurement — both argue against pure undertraining as the explanation for width-2 underconfidence. Label smoothing, now tested at all seven widths including the previously-missing crossover region, induces underconfidence everywhere, with ECE inflation (6–16x) traceable to baseline-ECE differences rather than a distinct mechanism. A second, independently-drawn data instance confirms the sign-flip pattern replicates but the specific crossover width and magnitude do not — so we now explicitly frame the crossover *location* as task-dependent, not a transferable number, while the *existence* of a capacity-driven sign flip is the claim we believe generalizes. Practically: practitioners calibrating small networks should not assume T>1, should fit T unconstrained below 1, should be cautious about label smoothing on small or well-fit models, and should not treat any single "crossover width" as portable across tasks.
