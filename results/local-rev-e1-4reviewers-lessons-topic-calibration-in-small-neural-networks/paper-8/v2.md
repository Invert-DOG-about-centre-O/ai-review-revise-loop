# Capacity-Dependent Miscalibration: Small Neural Networks Are Underconfident, Not Overconfident

## Abstract

The literature on neural network calibration is dominated by a single narrative: modern, high-capacity networks are overconfident, and post-hoc temperature scaling (with temperature T > 1) fixes it. We ask whether this narrative holds at the small end of the capacity spectrum. Using a synthetic 3-class Gaussian-discriminant classification task with an analytically known Bayes posterior — which lets us measure calibration error against ground-truth probabilities rather than noisy empirical bins — we train 2-hidden-layer MLPs with widths from 2 to 256 units and fit the optimal post-hoc temperature T* for each, now with **10 random seeds per width** (up from 2 in our initial pilot) so we can report variance. We find a clear, capacity-dependent crossover: the smallest networks (width 2) have T* = 0.44 ± 0.18 (underconfident, need *sharpening*), while the largest (width ≥ 64) plateau at T* ≈ 2.40 (overconfident, need *softening*), crossing T*≈1 around width 16 with non-overlapping 95% confidence intervals between width 4 and width 64. Extending the label-smoothing ablation to the **full width sweep** (previously only the two extremes), we show label smoothing pushes T* below 1 at *every* width tested — including width 64/256, which are strongly overconfident (T*≈2.4) without it — contradicting its usual justification as an overconfidence remedy. Critically, we also test robustness of the crossover *location* by re-drawing the Gaussian task parameters from an independent seed: the qualitative pattern (T* rising monotonically with width, from underconfident to overconfident) replicates, but the crossover width itself shifts substantially (from ~16 down to below width 4), showing that while the *sign-flips-with-capacity* phenomenon is robust, the *specific crossover width is not a fixed or predictive number* — it is task-dependent. A single free (sign-unconstrained) global temperature parameter fits on a small held-out set corrects all cases studied down to near-Bayes calibration error. Code, logs, and raw results are included for reproducibility.

## Introduction

Guo et al. (2017) established that modern deep networks are typically overconfident and that dividing logits by a learned scalar temperature T > 1 (temperature scaling) is a simple, effective fix. This finding has become close to folklore: "big networks are overconfident, use T > 1." But the models studied in that line of work — ResNets, DenseNets with millions of parameters — sit at one extreme of the capacity spectrum. Small networks (few hundred to a few thousand parameters), used routinely on-device, in tabular pipelines, or as calibration-sensitive components of larger systems, are much less studied, and there are reasons to expect qualitatively different behavior: a small network trained with cross-entropy is capacity- and optimization-limited, and cross-entropy loss pushes predictions toward the training labels' one-hot targets; a model that *cannot* fit the training data perfectly will often produce blunter (lower-confidence) outputs than a model that overfits, giving the opposite miscalibration sign.

### Related work

Guo et al. (2017), "On Calibration of Modern Neural Networks," introduced ECE as the standard metric and showed temperature scaling corrects overconfidence in large image classifiers. Follow-up work on adaptive and attended temperature scaling (Attended Temperature Scaling, 2018; Adaptive Temperature Scaling, 2022) refines *how* the temperature is chosen but keeps the assumption that T > 1 is needed. Minderer et al. (2021), "Revisiting the Calibration of Modern Neural Networks," found that some newer architecture families are much better calibrated than classic CNNs even at large scale, suggesting capacity alone doesn't fully determine calibration direction. More directly relevant, recent work on foundation models (2025) found that some large *pretrained* models are actually **underconfident** in-distribution — the opposite of the classic finding — which the authors attribute to architectural and training differences, not to capacity in isolation. Separately, Wang et al. (2023), "Two Sides of Miscalibration," explicitly argue that under-confidence is understudied relative to over-confidence and matters equally for failure detection. On the small/low-data side, prior work has noted that temperature scaling degrades when the calibration set is small or noisy, motivating lower-parameter alternatives, but — to our knowledge — no prior empirical study directly sweeps model *width* on a controlled task with a known Bayes posterior, across multiple seeds and multiple data-generating instances, to characterize *where* and *how reliably* the over-/under-confidence crossover occurs. That is the gap we address.

## Method

**Task.** We construct a synthetic 3-class Gaussian discriminant analysis (QDA) problem in D=8 dimensions: each class k has a mean μ_k and a diagonal, class-specific covariance Σ_k, so the Bayes-optimal decision boundary is quadratic (non-trivial for small networks) while the true posterior P(y | x) is available in closed form. This lets us define a **Bayes-ECE**: the mean absolute difference between a model's predicted confidence and the *true* posterior probability of its predicted class — a cleaner calibration signal than binned empirical ECE, since it does not depend on binning or finite-test-set noise.

**Data.** 2,000 training / 800 validation / 2,000 test points sampled i.i.d. from the generative model per data instance.

**Models.** 2-hidden-layer MLPs (ReLU) with equal hidden widths in {2, 4, 16, 64, 256} (roughly 30 to 70,000 parameters), trained with Adam (lr=1e-2, weight decay 1e-5) for 80 epochs, batch size 256, plain cross-entropy loss.

**Seeds and robustness (revised from v1).** Our initial pilot used 2 seeds per width and a single fixed data-generating draw; all four reviewers of that draft correctly flagged this as too thin to support "clear, monotonic" language. We now use **10 training seeds per width** on the main data instance, and additionally re-run the **entire width sweep on a second, independently-drawn data-generating instance** (different class means/variances, same D=8/3-class/99%-Bayes-accuracy regime) with 5 seeds per width, specifically to test whether the crossover width is a property of the task class or an artifact of one Gaussian draw.

**Calibration metrics.** For each trained model on the test set: accuracy, empirical 15-bin ECE, Bayes-ECE, and the optimal post-hoc temperature T*, found by grid search over T ∈ [0.05, 10] (200-point geometric grid) minimizing NLL on held-out validation data, without constraining T ≥ 1.

**Label-smoothing ablation (revised from v1).** v1 ran this only at widths 2 and 256; three reviewers asked what happens at the intermediate widths. We now run label smoothing (ε=0.1) at **all five widths**, 10 seeds each.

## Results

Table 1: capacity sweep, mean ± SD over **10 seeds** (main data instance).

| Width | Acc | ECE | Bayes-ECE | T* (mean ± SD) | 95% CI half-width | ECE after T* |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2   | 0.990 | 0.012 | 0.018 | **0.44 ± 0.18** | ±0.11 | 0.007 |
| 4   | 0.991 | 0.004 | 0.008 | **0.63 ± 0.12** | ±0.08 | 0.007 |
| 16  | 0.988 | 0.008 | 0.010 | **1.26 ± 0.33** | ±0.21 | 0.007 |
| 64  | 0.987 | 0.011 | 0.011 | **2.41 ± 0.55** | ±0.34 | 0.008 |
| 256 | 0.988 | 0.011 | 0.011 | **2.40 ± 0.19** | ±0.12 | 0.008 |

With 5x more seeds, the qualitative trend is unchanged from v1 but now statistically supported: the 95% CI for T* at width 4 (0.55–0.70) does not overlap the CI at width 64 (2.06–2.75), so the underconfident-to-overconfident swing is not a two-sample artifact. The previously-noted "non-monotonicity" between width 64 and 256 (a v1 artifact of 2 seeds: 2.48→2.22) essentially disappears with more seeds (2.41→2.40, a genuine plateau, not a dip). Accuracy stays flat (98.7–99.1%) across the whole sweep, so the T* trend is not an artifact of varying task difficulty.

Table 2: label smoothing (ε=0.1) across the **full width sweep**, mean over 10 seeds (main data instance).

| Width | T* (no LS) | T* (LS) | ECE (no LS) | ECE (LS) | ECE after T* (LS) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2   | 0.44 | **0.14** | 0.012 | 0.093 | 0.007 |
| 4   | 0.63 | **0.19** | 0.004 | 0.071 | 0.008 |
| 16  | 1.26 | **0.27** | 0.008 | 0.067 | 0.007 |
| 64  | 2.41 | **0.37** | 0.011 | 0.065 | 0.008 |
| 256 | 2.40 | **0.41** | 0.011 | 0.067 | 0.008 |

Extending the ablation across the whole sweep (v1 only tested widths 2 and 256) confirms and strengthens the original claim: label smoothing drives T* below 1 at *every* width, including width 64 and 256, which are the most strongly overconfident settings without it (T*≈2.4 → T*≈0.4). Raw ECE also rises sharply at every width under label smoothing (roughly 6–8x), and the width-dependent *T*(LS) itself still trends upward with capacity (0.14→0.41), showing label smoothing shifts the whole curve down without eliminating the underlying capacity effect. As before, one fitted temperature restores near-Bayes calibration in every case.

**Robustness of the crossover width (new).** Table 3 repeats the no-LS sweep on an independently-drawn second Gaussian task instance (different class means/variances, same generative family), 5 seeds per width.

| Width | Acc (instance 2) | T* (instance 2) |
|---|---|---|
| 2   | 0.979 | 0.90 ± 0.10 |
| 4   | 0.981 | 1.14 ± 0.11 |
| 16  | 0.976 | 2.15 ± 0.17 |
| 64  | 0.975 | 4.18 ± 0.20 |
| 256 | 0.975 | 4.29 ± 0.10 |

The direction of the effect replicates exactly: T* rises monotonically with width, moving from underconfident (0.90 at width 2) through the crossover to strongly overconfident (plateauing near 4.2–4.3 at width ≥ 64). But the crossover point itself moves — from ≈16 in the original instance to somewhere **below width 4** here — and the plateau value nearly doubles (4.2 vs 2.4). This directly answers the question multiple reviewers raised: the crossover width is *not* a stable, transferable number, even within the same task family; only the qualitative sign-flip-with-capacity pattern is.

## Discussion and limitations

This remains a small, synthetic, low-dimensional study; the full extended sweep (10 seeds × 5 widths × 2 label-smoothing settings on the main instance, plus a second 5-seed instance) runs in under three minutes on CPU. The task is comparatively easy (≈99% Bayes accuracy) and low-dimensional (D=8). The new robustness check (Table 3) makes concrete what v1 only speculated about: the exact crossover width and plateau magnitude are properties of the specific task instance, not universal constants, and we now show this empirically rather than merely disclaiming it. What we believe generalizes is the qualitative claim — capacity determines the *sign*, not just magnitude, of miscalibration on this task family — since it replicated with a different Gaussian draw, more seeds, and a fuller label-smoothing sweep. We did not test convolutional architectures, real image/text data, or a systematic sweep over task difficulty/dimensionality/number of data-generating draws (we used 2 instances, not a distribution over instances); a rigorous predictive rule for the crossover width as a function of task properties (e.g., input dimensionality, class separability) remains future work, and our data suggest such a rule could not simply use width in absolute units without conditioning on the task.

## Conclusion

On a controlled synthetic task with a known Bayes posterior, small neural networks are systematically *underconfident* and require post-hoc *sharpening* (T*<1), while sufficiently wide networks are overconfident and require *softening* (T*>1), with a statistically-supported crossover as a function of width (now backed by 10 seeds and non-overlapping confidence intervals, versus 2 seeds in our initial pilot). Label smoothing induces underconfidence at every width we tested — not just the extremes — undercutting its use as a universal overconfidence remedy. A second, independently-drawn data instance confirms the sign-flip pattern replicates but shows the specific crossover width and overconfidence magnitude are task-dependent, not fixed constants. Practically: practitioners calibrating small networks should not assume T>1 is the right regime, should fit temperature with T allowed below 1, should be cautious about applying label smoothing to small or already well-fit models, and should not treat any single "crossover width" as portable across tasks. All code (`experiment.py`, `experiment_v2.py`), raw logs, and full numeric results (`results.json`, `results_v2.json`) are provided for reproducibility.
