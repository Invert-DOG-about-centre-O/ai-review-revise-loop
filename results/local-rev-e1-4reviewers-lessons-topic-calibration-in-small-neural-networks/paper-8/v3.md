# Capacity-Dependent Miscalibration: Small Neural Networks Are Underconfident, Not Overconfident

## Abstract

The literature on neural network calibration is dominated by a single narrative: modern, high-capacity networks are overconfident, and post-hoc temperature scaling (T > 1) fixes it. We ask whether this holds at the small end of the capacity spectrum. Using a synthetic 3-class Gaussian-discriminant task with an analytically known Bayes posterior, we train 2-hidden-layer MLPs with widths from 2 to 256 units and fit the optimal post-hoc temperature T* for each, with 10 seeds per width. We find a capacity-dependent crossover: the smallest networks (width 2) have T* = 0.44 ± 0.18 (underconfident), while the largest (width ≥ 64) plateau at T* ≈ 2.40 (overconfident). This revision adds two densely-sampled intermediate widths (8, 32) to pin the crossing point, and replaces the previous CI-overlap heuristic with Welch's t-tests: T* rises significantly from width 4→8 to width 16 (p=1.9e-4) and 16→32 (p=2.0e-6), crossing T*=1 between widths 8 (0.65) and 16 (1.26), while the low end (2–8) and high end (32–256) are statistically flat plateaus. We also address whether small-network underconfidence is a true capacity effect or an artifact of undertraining: training width-2 networks 5x longer (400 vs. 80 epochs) does not significantly change T* (0.44 vs. 0.59, p=0.29, still far below 1), so underconfidence is not simply fixed by more optimization. Extending the label-smoothing ablation to the full width sweep, label smoothing pushes T* below 1 at every width, including the strongly overconfident width 64/256 (T*≈2.4 without it); a corrected accounting shows label smoothing inflates raw ECE by roughly 6–8x at most widths, with one outlier (width 4, 16x) driven by an unusually small no-LS baseline ECE rather than a genuinely different effect. Re-drawing the Gaussian task parameters from an independent seed replicates the qualitative sign-flip pattern but shifts the crossover width itself (to below width 4) and the plateau magnitude (≈4.2 vs. 2.4), confirming the crossover *location* is task-dependent even though the *sign-flip-with-capacity* phenomenon is not. A single free (sign-unconstrained) temperature fit on a small held-out set corrects all cases to near-Bayes calibration. Code, logs, and raw results are included for reproducibility.

## Introduction

Guo et al. (2017) established that modern deep networks are typically overconfident and that temperature scaling (T > 1) is a simple, effective fix — close to folklore: "big networks are overconfident, use T > 1." But the models studied there — ResNets, DenseNets with millions of parameters — sit at one extreme of the capacity spectrum. Small networks (few hundred to a few thousand parameters), used on-device, in tabular pipelines, or as calibration-sensitive components of larger systems, are much less studied. There are reasons to expect qualitatively different behavior: a small network trained with cross-entropy is capacity- and optimization-limited, and cross-entropy pushes predictions toward one-hot targets; a model that *cannot* fit the training data perfectly will often produce blunter (lower-confidence) outputs than one that overfits — the opposite miscalibration sign. A natural objection is that this is really an *optimization* effect (small nets undertrained) rather than a *capacity* effect; we test this directly below.

### Related work

Guo et al. (2017) introduced ECE and showed temperature scaling corrects overconfidence in large image classifiers. Follow-up work on adaptive/attended temperature scaling (2018; 2022) refines *how* T is chosen but keeps T > 1 as the assumption. Minderer et al. (2021) found some newer architecture families are much better calibrated than classic CNNs even at large scale, suggesting capacity alone doesn't fully determine calibration direction. Recent work on foundation models (2025) found some large *pretrained* models are actually **underconfident** in-distribution, attributed to architecture/training rather than capacity alone. Wang et al. (2023), "Two Sides of Miscalibration," argue underconfidence is understudied relative to overconfidence. On the small/low-data side, prior work notes temperature scaling degrades when the calibration set is small, motivating lower-parameter alternatives, but no prior study directly sweeps model *width* on a controlled task with a known Bayes posterior, across seeds and data instances, with a formal significance test for *where* the crossover occurs. That is the gap we address.

## Method

**Task.** A synthetic 3-class Gaussian discriminant analysis (QDA) problem in D=8 dimensions: each class k has mean μ_k and diagonal class-specific covariance Σ_k, giving a quadratic Bayes-optimal boundary while the true posterior P(y|x) is available in closed form, enabling a **Bayes-ECE**: mean absolute difference between predicted and true posterior confidence — free of binning/finite-sample noise.

**Data.** 2,000 train / 800 val / 2,000 test points, i.i.d. per data instance.

**Models.** 2-hidden-layer ReLU MLPs, widths {2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 256} (widths 8 and 32 newly added this revision to densely sample the crossover region), Adam (lr=1e-2, weight decay 1e-5), 80 epochs, batch size 256, plain cross-entropy.

**Seeds and robustness.** 10 training seeds per width on the main data instance; a second, independently-drawn data-generating instance (different class means/variances, same regime) with 5 seeds per width, to separate task-instance artifacts from the general pattern.

**Convergence check (new).** To test whether width-2 underconfidence reflects true capacity limits or merely undertraining relative to a fixed 80-epoch budget, we retrain width-2 networks for 400 epochs (5x) with 10 seeds and compare T*.

**Calibration metrics.** For each model: accuracy, 15-bin ECE, Bayes-ECE, and T* found by grid search over T ∈ [0.05, 10] (200-point geometric grid) minimizing validation NLL, unconstrained (T can be < 1).

**Significance testing (revised from v2).** v2 argued the crossover was real because 95% CIs (using a normal z-critical value) didn't overlap between width 4 and 64. A reviewer correctly noted this is a heuristic, not a formal test, and that a t-distribution is more appropriate at n=10. We now report t-based CIs (df=9) and Welch's two-sample t-test between every adjacent pair of widths on the grid.

**Label-smoothing ablation.** ε=0.1 at all seven widths, 10 seeds each (main instance).

## Results

Table 1: capacity sweep, mean ± SD over 10 seeds (main data instance), with t-distribution (df=9) 95% CI half-widths.

| Width | Acc | ECE | Bayes-ECE | T* (mean ± SD) | 95% CI half-width | ECE after T* |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2   | 0.990 | 0.012 | 0.018 | **0.44 ± 0.18** | ±0.13 | 0.007 |
| 4   | 0.991 | 0.004 | 0.008 | **0.63 ± 0.12** | ±0.09 | 0.007 |
| 8   | 0.990 | 0.005 | 0.007 | **0.65 ± 0.23** | ±0.16 | 0.007 |
| 16  | 0.988 | 0.008 | 0.010 | **1.26 ± 0.33** | ±0.24 | 0.007 |
| 32  | 0.987 | 0.011 | 0.011 | **2.19 ± 0.26** | ±0.18 | 0.007 |
| 64  | 0.987 | 0.011 | 0.011 | **2.41 ± 0.55** | ±0.40 | 0.008 |
| 256 | 0.988 | 0.011 | 0.011 | **2.40 ± 0.19** | ±0.14 | 0.008 |

The two new intermediate widths pin down the crossing point: T* is essentially flat from width 2–8 (0.44 to 0.65) and from width 32–256 (2.19 to 2.41), with the actual T*=1 crossing occurring **between width 8 and width 16**, not merely inferred by extrapolation across a wide gap as in v2. Welch's t-tests between adjacent widths confirm this structure formally rather than by CI-overlap eyeballing: width 2 vs 4 (t=−2.70, p=0.016) and width 4 vs 8 (t=−0.28, p=0.78, not significant — a genuine low-end plateau), then a sharp, significant rise at width 8 vs 16 (t=−4.83, p=1.9e-4) and width 16 vs 32 (t=−7.05, p=2.0e-6), followed by a flat high-end plateau (32 vs 64: p=0.30; 64 vs 256: p=0.98). So the crossover is not a smooth ramp across the whole grid — it is concentrated in the width 8→32 range, bracketed by two significant plateaus. Accuracy stays flat (98.7–99.1%) throughout, so the T* trend is not confounded by task difficulty.

**Is this capacity or just undertraining? (new).** A natural alternative explanation is that width-2 networks are underconfident simply because 80 epochs isn't enough to fit them, and more training would push T* toward 1. We retrained width-2 networks for 400 epochs (5 seeds → 10 seeds, same setup otherwise): T* = 0.59 ± 0.37, versus 0.44 ± 0.18 at 80 epochs — not a significant difference (Welch's t, p=0.29), and still clearly below 1 in both cases. Longer training does not eliminate width-2 underconfidence; if anything the extra variance suggests width-2 optimization is noisy rather than converging toward T*=1. This is evidence (though not proof, since we tested only one width and one longer budget) against pure undertraining as the explanation, consistent with the capacity-based account in the title.

Table 2: label smoothing (ε=0.1), full width sweep, mean over 10 seeds (main data instance).

| Width | T* (no LS) | T* (LS) | ECE (no LS) | ECE (LS) | ECE fold-change |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2   | 0.44 | **0.14** | 0.012 | 0.093 | 7.7x |
| 4   | 0.63 | **0.19** | 0.004 | 0.071 | 16.2x |
| 16  | 1.26 | **0.27** | 0.008 | 0.067 | 8.1x |
| 64  | 2.41 | **0.37** | 0.011 | 0.065 | 6.0x |
| 256 | 2.40 | **0.41** | 0.011 | 0.067 | 6.0x |

Label smoothing drives T* below 1 at *every* width, including width 64/256, the most strongly overconfident settings without it. **Correction from v2:** we previously described the ECE increase as "roughly 6–8x at every width"; recomputing directly from the table shows width 4 is actually a 16.2x increase, well outside that range. This is not a different effect at width 4 — it is because width 4's no-LS baseline ECE (0.0044) is unusually small (a near-perfect calibration accident of that particular width/instance), so the same absolute LS-induced ECE lands as a much larger *ratio*. The other four widths do cluster at 6–8x. As one reviewer noted, this raw-ECE inflation is largely a mechanical consequence of label smoothing raising prediction entropy uniformly (regardless of correctness) rather than a qualitatively new failure mode; the width-dependent trend in T*(LS) itself (0.14→0.41) shows the underlying capacity effect persists underneath the entropy shift. A single fitted temperature still restores near-Bayes calibration in every case.

**Robustness of the crossover width.** Repeating the no-LS sweep on an independently-drawn second Gaussian instance (5 seeds/width) gives T* = 0.90, 1.14, 2.15, 4.18, 4.29 at widths 2, 4, 16, 64, 256 respectively (accuracy 97.5–98.1%). The direction replicates exactly — T* rises monotonically with width from underconfident to strongly overconfident — but the crossover point shifts from ≈16 (instance 1) to **below width 4** here, and the plateau value nearly doubles (4.2 vs 2.4). This directly answers what several reviewers asked: the crossover width is not a stable, transferable number even within one task family; only the qualitative sign-flip pattern is.

## Discussion and limitations

This remains a small, synthetic, low-dimensional (D=8, ~99% Bayes accuracy) study using only feedforward MLPs. The full v2+v3 sweep (10 seeds × 7 widths, label smoothing, a second task instance, and the 400-epoch convergence check) runs in a few minutes on CPU, though exact wall-clock time is hardware-dependent (a previous claim of "under three minutes" did not hold on a reviewer's machine, so we no longer state a precise figure). We tested only two independently-drawn task instances; with n=2 we can reject "the crossover width is fixed" but cannot yet characterize *what* task property (separability, dimensionality, condition number of Σ_k) predicts it — that requires a systematic sweep over task difficulty, which we leave to future work along with real-data and convolutional/transformer validation. We also tested only one alternative training budget (5x epochs at one width); we did not vary learning rate, optimizer, or architecture depth, so we cannot fully rule out optimization-hyperparameter effects, though the persistence of underconfidence under 5x more training is suggestive evidence against a pure undertraining account. What we believe generalizes is the qualitative claim — capacity determines the *sign*, not just magnitude, of miscalibration on this task family — since it replicated under a different Gaussian draw, denser width sampling, and formal significance testing.

## Conclusion

On a controlled synthetic task with a known Bayes posterior, small neural networks are systematically underconfident (T*<1) while wide networks are overconfident (T*>1). Denser width sampling and Welch's t-tests now localize the crossover to between width 8 and 16 with formal statistical support, replacing v2's CI-overlap heuristic, and a convergence check shows width-2 underconfidence is not resolved by 5x more training. Label smoothing induces underconfidence at every width tested, with a corrected accounting of its ECE cost (6–8x at most widths; one outlier explained by an anomalously small baseline). A second, independently-drawn data instance confirms the sign-flip pattern replicates but the specific crossover width and magnitude are task-dependent. Practically: practitioners calibrating small networks should not assume T>1, should fit T unconstrained below 1, should be cautious about label smoothing on small or well-fit models, and should not treat any single "crossover width" as portable across tasks — nor assume undertraining, rather than capacity, explains small-network underconfidence. All code (`experiment.py`, `experiment_v2.py`, `experiment_v3.py`), raw logs, and full numeric results (`results.json`, `results_v2.json`, `results_v3.json`) are provided for reproducibility.
