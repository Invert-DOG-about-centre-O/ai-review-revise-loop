# Capacity-Dependent Miscalibration: Small Neural Networks Are Underconfident, Not Overconfident

## Abstract

The literature on neural network calibration is dominated by a single narrative: modern, high-capacity networks are overconfident, and post-hoc temperature scaling (with temperature T > 1) fixes it. We ask whether this narrative holds at the small end of the capacity spectrum. Using a synthetic 3-class Gaussian-discriminant classification task with an analytically known Bayes posterior — which lets us measure calibration error against ground-truth probabilities rather than noisy empirical bins — we train 2-hidden-layer MLPs with widths from 2 to 256 units and fit the optimal post-hoc temperature T* for each. We find a clear, monotonic, capacity-dependent crossover: the smallest networks (width 2) have T* ≈ 0.37 (they are underconfident and need *sharpening*), while the largest (width ≥ 64) have T* ≈ 2.2–2.5 (they are overconfident and need *softening*), with the crossover near T*≈1 occurring around width 16. We further show that label smoothing, a common calibration heuristic, pushes T* well below 1 at *every* width tested, i.e. it induces underconfidence regardless of model capacity, contradicting its usual justification as an overconfidence remedy. A single free (sign-unconstrained) global temperature parameter fits on a small held-out set corrects all cases studied down to near-Bayes calibration error. Code, logs, and raw results are included for reproducibility.

## Introduction

Guo et al. (2017) established that modern deep networks are typically overconfident and that dividing logits by a learned scalar temperature T > 1 (temperature scaling) is a simple, effective fix. This finding has become close to folklore: "big networks are overconfident, use T > 1." But the models studied in that line of work — ResNets, DenseNets with millions of parameters — sit at one extreme of the capacity spectrum. Small networks (few hundred to a few thousand parameters), used routinely on-device, in tabular pipelines, or as calibration-sensitive components of larger systems, are much less studied, and there are reasons to expect qualitatively different behavior: a small network trained with cross-entropy is capacity- and optimization-limited, and cross-entropy loss pushes predictions toward the training labels' one-hot targets; a model that *cannot* fit the training data perfectly will often produce blunter (lower-confidence) outputs than a model that overfits, giving the opposite miscalibration sign.

### Related work

Guo et al. (2017), "On Calibration of Modern Neural Networks," introduced ECE as the standard metric and showed temperature scaling corrects overconfidence in large image classifiers. Follow-up work on adaptive and attended temperature scaling (Attended Temperature Scaling, 2018; Adaptive Temperature Scaling, 2022) refines *how* the temperature is chosen but keeps the assumption that T > 1 is needed. Minderer et al. (2021), "Revisiting the Calibration of Modern Neural Networks," found that some newer architecture families are much better calibrated than classic CNNs even at large scale, suggesting capacity alone doesn't fully determine calibration direction. More directly relevant, recent work on foundation models (2025) found that some large *pretrained* models are actually **underconfident** in-distribution — the opposite of the classic finding — which the authors attribute to architectural and training differences, not to capacity in isolation. Separately, Wang et al. (2023), "Two Sides of Miscalibration," explicitly argue that under-confidence is understudied relative to over-confidence and matters equally for failure detection. On the small/low-data side, prior work has noted that temperature scaling degrades when the calibration set is small or noisy, motivating lower-parameter alternatives, but — to our knowledge — no prior empirical study directly sweeps model *width* on a controlled task with a known Bayes posterior to characterize *where* the over-/under-confidence crossover occurs. That is the gap we address.

## Method

**Task.** We construct a synthetic 3-class Gaussian discriminant analysis (QDA) problem in D=8 dimensions: each class k has a mean μ_k (drawn once, fixed across all runs) and a diagonal, class-specific covariance Σ_k, so the Bayes-optimal decision boundary is quadratic (non-trivial for small networks) while the true posterior P(y | x) is available in closed form via the Gaussian likelihood and Bayes' rule. This lets us define, in addition to standard metrics, a **Bayes-ECE**: the mean absolute difference between a model's predicted confidence and the *true* posterior probability of its predicted class — a much cleaner calibration signal than binned empirical ECE, since it does not depend on binning or on finite-test-set accuracy noise. Bayes accuracy on this task is 99.2%.

**Data.** 2,000 training / 800 validation / 2,000 test points sampled i.i.d. from the generative model, fixed seed for the data-generating parameters.

**Models.** 2-hidden-layer MLPs (ReLU) with equal hidden widths in {2, 4, 16, 64, 256}, trained with Adam (lr=1e-2, weight decay 1e-5) for 80 epochs, batch size 256, plain cross-entropy loss, 2 random seeds per width. This width range spans roughly 30 to 70,000 parameters.

**Calibration metrics.** For each trained model on the test set we compute: accuracy, NLL, 15-bin empirical ECE, Bayes-ECE (defined above), and the optimal post-hoc temperature T* — found by grid search over T ∈ [0.05, 10] (geometric grid, 200 points) minimizing NLL on the held-out validation set, following standard temperature scaling but *without* constraining T ≥ 1, so both sharpening and softening are possible. We report ECE after applying T* to the test set (ece_ts) to check that the fitted temperature actually improves calibration out of sample.

**Ablation.** We repeat the smallest (width 2) and largest (width 256) settings with label smoothing (ε=0.1) instead of plain cross-entropy, to test the common belief that label smoothing is primarily a remedy for overconfidence.

## Results

Table 1: capacity sweep (mean over 2 seeds), plain cross-entropy.

| Width | Params (approx) | Acc | ECE | Bayes-ECE | T* | ECE after T* |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2   | ~30    | 0.989 | 0.028 | 0.033 | **0.37** | 0.006 |
| 4   | ~70    | 0.991 | 0.007 | 0.012 | **0.65** | 0.007 |
| 16  | ~450   | 0.990 | 0.008 | 0.009 | **1.35** | 0.007 |
| 64  | ~5,000 | 0.987 | 0.011 | 0.011 | **2.48** | 0.008 |
| 256 | ~70,000| 0.988 | 0.011 | 0.010 | **2.22** | 0.008 |

The central result is the monotonic trend in T* across widths 2 → 4 → 16, moving from clearly below 1 (underconfident: the model must be *sharpened* to match its accuracy) to clearly above 1 (overconfident: it must be *softened*), crossing T*≈1 near width 16, and plateauing around T*≈2.2–2.5 for width ≥ 64 — the classic large-model overconfidence regime reported in prior work. Accuracy is roughly flat (98.7–99.1%) across the whole sweep, close to the 99.2% Bayes rate, so the T* trend is not an artifact of varying task difficulty or underfitting-on-accuracy; the networks all learn to classify about equally well, but their *confidence calibration* differs systematically with capacity. Bayes-ECE (measured against the true posterior, not just binned empirical accuracy) shows the same pattern, giving an independent confirmation that this is a real calibration effect, not a binning artifact. In all cases, fitting a single free-sign temperature on 800 validation points brings ECE down to 0.006–0.008, close to the empirical noise floor for a 2,000-point test set — i.e., regardless of whether the raw model was under- or over-confident, one-parameter temperature scaling (letting T range below 1) is sufficient to fix it.

Table 2: label smoothing ablation (ε=0.1), mean over 2 seeds.

| Width | ECE (no LS) | ECE (LS) | T* (no LS) | T* (LS) | ECE after T* (LS) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2   | 0.028 | **0.145** | 0.37 | **0.16** | 0.009 |
| 256 | 0.011 | **0.073** | 2.22 | **0.40** | 0.008 |

Label smoothing sharply increases raw ECE at *both* the smallest and largest widths (0.028→0.145 at width 2; 0.011→0.073 at width 256) and drives T* well below 1 in both cases — including for width 256, which without label smoothing was clearly overconfident (T*=2.22) but with it becomes underconfident (T*=0.40). This directly contradicts using label smoothing as a general-purpose overconfidence fix: on this task, where the Bayes-optimal confidence is close to 1 for most points (99.2% Bayes accuracy), label smoothing's soft-label floor (capping the effective target confidence at 1−ε) systematically depresses predicted confidence below the true posterior, at any capacity. As with the capacity sweep, a single fitted temperature (here T*≪1) restores near-Bayes calibration in every case.

## Discussion and limitations

This is a small, synthetic, low-dimensional study, run under a strict compute budget (all 14 training runs complete in under 25 seconds total on CPU); we deliberately traded scale for the ability to compute an exact, noise-free ground-truth posterior, which is what makes the width-vs-T* trend interpretable rather than confounded by binning noise. The task is comparatively easy (99.2% Bayes accuracy) and low-dimensional (D=8); we would not expect the exact crossover width (≈16 here) to transfer to other tasks — what we expect to generalize is the *qualitative* finding that capacity determines the *sign*, not just the magnitude, of miscalibration, consistent with scattered observations in the literature (small/low-capacity models trending underconfident; classic large CNNs trending overconfident; some large foundation models also trending underconfident for architectural reasons). We did not test convolutional architectures, real image/text data, or regularization sweeps (dropout, weight decay strength) as separate capacity-like axes; those are natural next steps. We also did not test whether the crossover width itself moves with task difficulty or input dimensionality, which would be needed to turn this into a predictive rule ("networks below width w need T<1").

## Conclusion

We show, on a controlled synthetic task with a known Bayes posterior, that small neural networks are systematically *underconfident* and require post-hoc *sharpening* (T*<1), while sufficiently wide networks on the same task are overconfident and require *softening* (T*>1, replicating the classic finding), with a clear monotonic crossover as a function of width. We further show label smoothing induces underconfidence regardless of capacity on this task, undercutting its use as a universal overconfidence remedy. Practically, the takeaway is narrow but actionable: practitioners calibrating small networks should not assume T>1 is the right regime and should fit temperature with T allowed below 1, and should be cautious about applying label smoothing to small or already well-fit models.
