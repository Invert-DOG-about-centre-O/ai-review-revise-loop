# Width and Calibration in Small MLPs: The Direction of the Effect Is Dataset-Dependent, Not Universal

## Abstract

We study how expected calibration error (ECE) and the post-hoc temperature-scaling correction change as a function of hidden-layer width in small multilayer perceptrons (MLPs), holding architecture family and training budget fixed. Using two classification datasets — the real 10-class `digits` dataset and a synthetic 4-class `make_classification` dataset — we sweep width from 2 to 256 hidden units, run 20 independently-seeded train/val/test splits per width, and pre-register three hypotheses about width vs. raw ECE, width vs. optimal temperature $T^*$, and whether temperature scaling's raw benefit is genuinely associated with pre-scaling ECE or merely an artifact of the arithmetic identity `reduction = ece_pre − ece_post`. On `digits`, wider networks are *better* calibrated before scaling (Spearman $\rho=-0.87$, permutation $p<10^{-4}$); on the synthetic dataset, wider networks are *worse* calibrated (Spearman $\rho=+0.59$, permutation $p<10^{-4}$). Both trends survive dropping either extreme width from the grid. We do not claim a universal width–calibration relationship; instead we report an honest, dataset-dependent finding and a worked example of how easily a spurious "does TS help more when the model started worse" claim can arise: a naive Pearson correlation between pre-scaling ECE and TS's raw improvement is large and looks highly significant ($r=0.84$, $p<10^{-40}$ on the synthetic set), but a permutation null that shuffles post-scaling ECE independently of pre-scaling ECE — which we separately validate has a nominal false-positive rate under a known true null — shows this correlation is not distinguishable from what the pre/post arithmetic coupling produces by chance alone ($p=1.0$ in both datasets). Temperature scaling reduces mean ECE in the majority of runs but makes calibration measurably worse in a non-trivial minority (11–23% of runs, concentrated at small width, where held-out validation splits are noisiest).

## 1. Introduction

Modern neural networks, particularly large over-parameterized ones, are frequently miscalibrated — confident probabilities that don't match empirical accuracy — and temperature scaling (Guo et al., 2017) is the standard cheap post-hoc fix. Most calibration literature targets large vision/NLP models. Practitioners deploying *small* networks (embedded devices, tabular/low-data settings, cost-constrained services) need to know: does capacity itself drive calibration error at the small end of the spectrum, and does the standard post-hoc fix behave predictably as capacity changes?

### Related work

Guo et al. (2017) established that modern (typically large, deep) networks are overconfident and that a single scalar temperature fit on a held-out set is a simple, effective corrective. Follow-up work has proposed more expressive parameterizations of temperature scaling for further gains (e.g. parameterized/adaptive temperature scaling, ensemble temperature scaling), generally in the large-model regime. A separate thread studies the *calibration generalization gap*: in over-parameterized models, test ECE can keep rising during training even as train/test error and train ECE fall, because the model becomes increasingly confident on the test points it gets wrong. Theoretical work has pushed back on a simple "over-parameterization causes overconfidence" story, showing in binary-classification settings that the relationship is more nuanced and depends on other factors (regularization, architecture, data separability). Empirically, capacity's effect on calibration is reported as inconsistent across studies. This inconsistency is exactly our starting point: we test whether a *single, controlled* width sweep — same architecture family, same training budget, two datasets — produces a consistent sign for the width–ECE relationship, or whether the sign itself is dataset-dependent even under otherwise-identical experimental control.

### Contribution

(1) A controlled width sweep (2–256 hidden units, one hidden layer, fixed solver and iteration budget) with 20 seeds per cell on two datasets, with pre-registered hypotheses and a Bonferroni-corrected multiple-testing budget. (2) A demonstration, using a permutation-null design validated against a known ground truth, that a large and apparently highly significant "TS helps more when the model started more miscalibrated" correlation is a statistical artifact of the pre/post arithmetic identity, not a real relationship, in this data. (3) An honest report that the width–ECE relationship's *direction* is not universal across our two datasets, avoiding a stronger claim than the data supports.

## 2. Method

**Datasets.** (a) `digits` (sklearn): 1797 real 8×8 grayscale handwritten-digit images, 10 classes, standardized features. (b) `synthetic`: an `sklearn.datasets.make_classification` set, 1800 samples, 20 features (10 informative, 5 redundant), 4 classes, 2 clusters/class, 3% label noise, standardized features, fixed generator seed (42) so the underlying data-generating process is identical across all width/seed cells.

**Architecture and training.** Single-hidden-layer MLP (ReLU), `sklearn.neural_network.MLPClassifier`, solver `lbfgs`, `max_iter=1500` (fixed across all widths — the training-length budget is not confounded with width). Width grid: {2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256} hidden units. For each (dataset, width) cell we run 20 independent seeds. Seeds are explicit integers from a fixed table (`base_seed + width_index*1000 + seed_index`; never derived from `hash()`), and each run reseeds immediately before its own split and training, so no run's model depends on RNG state left over from a previous run in the loop — each reported (dataset, width, seed) triple corresponds to one specific, independently reproducible trained model.

**Splits and evaluation.** Each seed gets its own stratified 50% train / 25% validation / 25% test split. Logits (pre-softmax scores) are recovered by manually replaying the trained model's forward pass (`coefs_`, `intercepts_`, ReLU) since `MLPClassifier` only exposes post-softmax probabilities. We report accuracy, 15-bin ECE, multiclass Brier score, and NLL on the test set, both before and after temperature scaling. $T^*$ is fit by minimizing NLL on the validation split (coarse grid over [0.1, 20] then local refinement), and the post-scaling metrics are then computed on the held-out test split (never the validation split used to fit $T^*$).

**Pre-registered hypotheses (decided before running the analysis, tested with a fixed Bonferroni budget of $\alpha=0.05/6=0.0083$ across 3 hypotheses × 2 datasets):**
- **H1**: raw (pre-scaling) ECE is monotonically related to $\log_2(\text{width})$ — Spearman correlation across all (width, seed) pairs within a dataset, tested against a label-permutation null (10,000 permutations), per dataset.
- **H2**: optimal temperature $T^*$ is monotonically related to $\log_2(\text{width})$ — same design.
- **H3**: TS's raw benefit (`reduction = ece_pre − ece_post`) is associated with `ece_pre` beyond what the arithmetic identity alone produces. Because `reduction` is a deterministic function of `ece_pre` and `ece_post`, a plain Pearson correlation between `ece_pre` and `reduction` is guaranteed to inherit bias from `Var(ece_pre)` even under a true null. We instead use a permutation null that shuffles `ece_post` independently of `ece_pre`, recomputes `reduction`, and asks whether the observed `corr(ece_pre, reduction)` exceeds this null's distribution.

We separately validate the H3 permutation procedure's own false-positive rate: we simulate 600 independent "true-null" datasets (n=160, matching our per-dataset sample size, `pre` and `post` drawn independently from Gamma(2,1) so there is by construction zero relationship of any kind between them) and run the identical permutation test on each. The observed false-positive rate at nominal $\alpha=0.05$ is 0.047 (within 2 SE of nominal; see `sanity_check_permnull_results.json`), so the test is not itself anti-conservative or liberal.

**Secondary/exploratory (not multiplicity-corrected, explicitly labeled):** leave-one-width-out robustness (drop width=2, drop width=256, recheck H1/H2 sign and significance); the fraction of runs where TS made ECE worse; a cross-tabulation of solver convergence warnings against width.

**Power.** With $n=160$ per dataset (8 widths × 20 seeds) and Bonferroni $\alpha=0.0083$, the minimum detectable Spearman $\rho$ at 80% power (Fisher-z approximation) is $\approx 0.27$. All primary effects we report as significant have $|\rho|$ or $|r|$ well above this threshold ($\geq 0.57$), so the significant findings are not marginal detections at the edge of test sensitivity — but this also means the design cannot resolve smaller true effects, a limitation we return to below.

## 3. Results

All numbers below are computed by `analysis.py` from `raw_results.json` (320 total runs = 2 datasets × 8 widths × 20 seeds) and persisted in `analysis_results.json`; nothing is hand-derived.

**Convergence audit.** 11/320 runs (3.4%) emitted an `lbfgs` convergence warning at `max_iter=1500`; all are concentrated at the smallest widths (`digits` width 2: 6/20, width 4: 2/20; `synthetic` width 16: 3/20). We flag this proactively: width=16 on `synthetic` is exactly where ECE and $T^*$ show their sharpest jump (see below), so non-convergence is a plausible partial contributor there, though the elevated-ECE plateau persists across widths 32–256 where convergence was clean, so non-convergence alone does not explain the broader trend.

**H1 (width vs. raw ECE).** `digits`: $\rho=-0.874$, permutation $p<10^{-4}$ — wider networks are better calibrated before any correction (95% CI on ece_pre shrinks from [0.070, 0.086] at width 2 to [0.019, 0.024] at width 256). `synthetic`: $\rho=+0.595$, permutation $p<10^{-4}$ — the opposite sign; wider networks are worse calibrated (ece_pre roughly triples from width 8 to width 16 and stays elevated through width 256). Both are significant well past the Bonferroni threshold.

**H2 (width vs. $T^*$).** `digits`: $\rho=-0.570$, $p<10^{-4}$ (less correction needed at larger width, consistent with H1). `synthetic`: $\rho=+0.577$, $p<10^{-4}$ (more correction needed at larger width, also consistent with H1). The sign of H2 tracks the sign of H1 within each dataset, which is the internally-consistent pattern we would expect if $T^*$ is responding to genuine overconfidence rather than noise.

**H3 (is TS's benefit related to ece_pre beyond the arithmetic identity?)** The naive Pearson correlation between `ece_pre` and `reduction` is $r=0.144$ ($p=0.069$, not significant) on `digits` and $r=0.840$ ($p<10^{-43}$, apparently overwhelming) on `synthetic`. Under our validated permutation null, neither survives: `digits` $p=1.0$, `synthetic` $p=1.0$. The large naive correlation on `synthetic` is exactly the pattern the pre/delta-trap lesson warns about — it is statistically indistinguishable from what shuffling `ece_post` independently of `ece_pre` produces by chance, because `reduction` is arithmetically tied to `ece_pre`. We do not claim TS's benefit is unrelated to model miscalibration in any deeper sense — only that this particular correlation, taken at face value, does not provide evidence for it.

**Robustness (leave-one-width-out).** Dropping width=2: `digits` H1 $\rho=-0.886$, H2 $\rho=-0.944$ (both still $p<10^{-4}$); `synthetic` H1 $\rho=0.404$, H2 $\rho=0.375$ (still $p<10^{-4}$, smaller magnitude, driven partly by width=2's low ECE anchoring the low end). Dropping width=256: `digits` H1 $\rho=-0.857$, H2 $\rho=-0.475$; `synthetic` H1 $\rho=0.687$, H2 $\rho=0.706$. In all four leave-one-out checks the sign of both effects is preserved within each dataset, so neither trend is an artifact of a single extreme width.

**Does TS ever hurt?** Yes: on `digits`, TS increases ECE (post > pre) in 37/160 runs (23.1%), concentrated at small width (width=2 alone: mean ece_pre 0.078 → mean ece_post 0.130, i.e., TS is harmful *on average* at that width, not just in isolated runs — plausibly because the val split at width 2 is small and the fitted low-accuracy decision surface is unstable). On `synthetic`, TS increases ECE in 18/160 runs (11.3%). We do not treat TS as a free, always-beneficial correction; at the smallest width tested here it was net-harmful on the real dataset.

## 4. Discussion and limitations

The central finding is a non-finding in the way that matters most: we do **not** find a universal sign for the width–calibration relationship. On real digit data, added capacity improves raw calibration; on our synthetic multiclass generator, added capacity worsens it, consistent with the "overparameterized models become overconfident" literature but only on one of our two datasets. Both directions are individually well-powered, significant past a Bonferroni correction, and robust to dropping either end of the width grid — this is not a case of an underpowered or fragile trend, it is a case of two real, opposite, dataset-specific trends. We deliberately do not attempt to force these into a single story (e.g., by post-hoc appeal to "task difficulty" or "separability") since we did not design an experiment that could isolate which property of the datasets drives the sign flip; that would require an orthogonal manipulation (e.g., sweeping class separability or label noise at fixed width, on a fixed dataset family) that we did not run here and flag explicitly as future work rather than speculate about post-hoc.

**Scope.** This is a two-dataset, one-architecture-family (single-hidden-layer MLP), one-solver (`lbfgs`) study. We do not claim these findings generalize to deeper networks, convolutional architectures, other solvers, or other data regimes; we picked `lbfgs` specifically because it converged reliably at our fixed iteration budget across widths (an earlier `adam` run at the same nominal budget left 240/320 runs non-converged, which we discarded as a design rather than report on top of an uncontrolled confound — see `experiment.py` history). A single spot-check with a different solver family (e.g. SGD/Adam at a matched, empirically-verified-converged budget) or a third dataset would be needed before treating either directional finding as more than dataset-specific evidence.

**Small held-out splits.** Validation and test splits here are small (≈450 and ≈450 points for `digits`; ≈450/450 for `synthetic`), and $T^*$ is fit on the validation split alone — the per-cell 95% CIs reported above and the 11–23% rate of TS making things worse both reflect this: point estimates of ECE and $T^*$ from a few hundred held-out points are noisy enough to occasionally flip the direction of the correction, not just its magnitude.

**Multiplicity.** We pre-registered 3 hypotheses × 2 datasets = 6 primary tests and applied Bonferroni correction; all four hypothesis directions we report as significant clear $p<10^{-4}$, far below the $\alpha=0.0083$ threshold. The leave-one-out and TS-harm checks are explicitly secondary/exploratory and not multiplicity-corrected against the primary family.

## 5. Conclusion

In a controlled, pre-registered width sweep across two datasets with a fixed architecture family and training budget, the sign of the raw ECE–width relationship is significant, robust to dropping extreme widths, but *opposite* between datasets — we report this as a genuine finding of dataset-dependence rather than force a single narrative. Separately, we show concretely how a naive "TS helps more where miscalibration was worse" correlation can look highly significant purely from the arithmetic coupling between pre- and post-scaling ECE, and that a permutation null (validated to have a nominal false-positive rate under a known true null) is needed to tell the two apart — in our data, no such genuine relationship survives that check. Practically: temperature scaling should not be assumed free or always beneficial, particularly at the smallest widths and with small held-out sets, where it measurably hurt calibration in a sizeable minority of runs.

## Reproducibility

All code, raw per-run results, and analysis outputs are in this directory: `experiment.py` (data generation and training, produces `raw_results.json`), `analysis.py` (all statistical tests, produces `analysis_results.json`), `sanity_check_permnull.py` (permutation-null false-positive-rate validation, produces `sanity_check_permnull_results.json`). Every statistic quoted above is read directly from these JSON files, not hand-recomputed. Total wall-clock time for the full pipeline (both datasets, 320 model fits, all statistical tests) was under 5 minutes on a single CPU core.
