# Parameter-Matched Small-Network Ensembles Do Not Reliably Improve Calibration Over a Single Network of Equal Size

## Abstract

Deep ensembling is widely credited with improving calibration, but almost all evidence for this comes from ensembles that multiply the *total* parameter count relative to a single reference model. We ask a narrower, budget-honest question: at a **fixed total hidden-unit budget**, is it better to spend that budget on one network or split it across several smaller, independently-initialized networks whose softmax outputs are averaged? We compare a single 64-hidden-unit MLP ("Single-Large") against an ensemble of four 16-unit MLPs ("Ensemble-4", same total width) and a single 16-unit MLP ("Single-Small", an unensembled reference), on two classification problems: a synthetic 4-class Gaussian mixture with an analytically known Bayes posterior, and the real `sklearn` `digits` dataset. Across 15 independent seeds per condition, before temperature scaling Ensemble-4 has *significantly higher* expected calibration error (ECE) than Single-Large on both datasets (digits: paired Wilcoxon p=6×10⁻⁵; synthetic: p=2×10⁻⁴), driven by ensembling shrinking confidence *below* accuracy (mean confidence 0.941 vs. accuracy 0.964 on digits) — the opposite of the textbook overconfidence story. After per-model temperature scaling, the gap shrinks but does not close on digits (post-TS ECE 0.0244 vs. 0.0201, p=0.011) while becoming statistically indistinguishable on the near-saturated synthetic task (p=0.72). A follow-up ablation isolating bootstrap resampling from pure-init diversity finds no significant difference between them (p=0.80), so we cannot confidently attribute the residual post-TS gap to either mechanism specifically — we report this as inconclusive rather than resolved. We conclude that "ensembling improves calibration" does not transfer to the fixed-budget regime relevant to small/embedded models, and that a single appropriately-sized network with temperature scaling is a competitive, cheaper alternative.

## 1. Introduction

Modern neural networks are frequently miscalibrated — their softmax confidences do not match empirical accuracy — and post-hoc fixes such as temperature scaling (Guo et al., 2017) are the standard remedy. A second, complementary story in the literature is that *deep ensembles* improve calibration by averaging out individual overconfident members (Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017). This claim, however, is almost always tested by comparing an ensemble of M full-size networks (M× the parameters and M× the compute of a single model) against a single full-size network — an apples-to-oranges comparison from a capacity-budget perspective. For small/embedded neural networks, where a total parameter or FLOP budget is often the binding constraint, the operative question is different: given a fixed total budget, is it better spent on one wider network or several narrower ensembled ones?

**Related work.** On calibration mechanisms, Guo et al. (2017) established that modern (larger, less regularized) networks are typically overconfident and that a single scalar temperature fit on a validation set is a cheap, effective fix; follow-up work has proposed adaptive/instance-aware and ensemble variants of temperature scaling (e.g., GETS for graph networks, ICLR 2025; sample-margin-aware recalibration, 2025) but these largely retain the single-network-vs-scaled-ensemble asymmetry. Label smoothing (Müller et al., 2019) and self-distillation (Zhang et al., 2020) are alternative train-time calibration tools, with known interactions (label-smoothed teachers hurt subsequent distillation). On ensembling, Lakshminarayanan et al. (2017) showed deep ensembles of full-size networks are both more accurate and better calibrated than a single member, but did not control for total parameter budget. To our knowledge the budget-matched question — many small vs. one equally-large — has not been directly isolated for calibration specifically, and it is exactly the comparison that matters when capacity itself (not just compute for training M models) is constrained, e.g. on-device inference.

We test three width-budget allocations at matched total hidden units, on two datasets chosen to triangulate the finding: a synthetic Gaussian-mixture task with an *analytically known* Bayes-optimal posterior (letting us sanity-check that the qualitative story is not an artifact of using empirical accuracy as a calibration proxy — reported in the results but accuracy there is too saturated to be very informative), and the real `digits` dataset, which carries the main empirical weight of our conclusions.

## 2. Method

**Datasets.**
- *Synthetic*: K=4 classes in R¹⁰, class-conditional N(μ_k, I) with fixed random means, equal priors (4000 train / 1000 val / 2000 test). Because the covariance is identity and priors are equal, the true posterior is exactly softmax(x·μ_k − ½‖μ_k‖²), computable in closed form — used only as an auxiliary sanity metric (L1 distance to Bayes posterior), not the headline result, since accuracy on this task is ≈99.8% for all conditions and hence a poor discriminator.
- *Real*: `sklearn.datasets.load_digits` (1797 8×8 images, 10 classes), standardized features, stratified 60/20/20 train/val/test split.

**Models.** All models are single-hidden-layer MLPs (`sklearn.neural_network.MLPClassifier`, ReLU, Adam, weight decay 1e-4, max 400 iterations), trained from an RNG seed derived independently for every (dataset, condition, seed) combination — never from global or carried-over RNG state — so that no two reported numbers share leaked randomness across conditions.
- **Single-Large**: one MLP, hidden width 64.
- **Ensemble-4**: four independently-initialized MLPs, hidden width 16 each (same *total* hidden units as Single-Large), each trained on an independent bootstrap resample of the training set; predictions averaged in probability space.
- **Single-Small**: one MLP, hidden width 16 (unensembled reference, same width as one ensemble member).

**Calibration.** For each condition we fit a single scalar temperature T (bounded scalar minimization of validation NLL, operating on log-probabilities so it applies uniformly to single models and probability-averaged ensembles) and report metrics both pre- and post-scaling.

**Metrics** (test set): accuracy, NLL, multiclass Brier score, 15-bin ECE (|confidence − accuracy| weighted by bin mass), signed ECE (confidence − accuracy, to reveal over- vs under-confidence direction), mean max-probability (confidence), and, for the synthetic task only, mean L1 distance between predicted and true Bayes posteriors.

**Statistics.** 15 independent seeds per (dataset, condition). We pre-registered the comparison Ensemble-4 vs. Single-Large ECE (post-TS) as the primary test, using a one-sided paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test (H1: Ensemble-4 ECE is larger), matching the direction we report; we also report the pre-TS version and an NLL version for completeness, and flag that these are not independently pre-registered so treat them as exploratory/secondary.

**Follow-up mechanism check.** To test *why* Ensemble-4 is worse-calibrated (rather than assert a cause), we ran a second experiment (`mechanism_check.py`) adding a third ensemble variant, **Ensemble-4-NoBoot**, whose four members are trained on the *full*, non-bootstrapped training set and differ only in random initialization — isolating "bootstrap resampling shrinks effective per-member data" from "probability-averaging itself shifts confidence," and directly computing signed calibration error rather than assuming a direction.

## 3. Results

All raw numbers are in `results.json` and `mechanism_results.json`; both experiment scripts are included and reproduce every number below (`experiment.py`, `mechanism_check.py`; total combined runtime ≈145s on CPU).

**Synthetic (near-saturated, accuracy ≈0.998 for all conditions).** Pre-TS ECE: Single-Large 0.0025, Ensemble-4 0.0036, Single-Small 0.0034 (Ensemble-4 significantly worse than Single-Large, p=1.5×10⁻⁴). Post-TS ECE: 0.0016 / 0.0015 / 0.0017 respectively — statistically indistinguishable (p=0.72). Post-TS NLL also not significantly different (p=0.58). Given the tiny absolute magnitudes here (ECE std ≈0.0005–0.0012 across seeds) and near-ceiling accuracy, we treat this dataset as a directional check, not a strong result on its own: it shows the same *pre-TS* ordering as digits but the effect is largely absorbed by temperature scaling once the task is this easy.

**Digits (real data, accuracy ≈0.96–0.97).** This is the paper's main evidence.

| Condition | Pre-TS ECE | Post-TS ECE | Post-TS NLL | Accuracy | Mean confidence (pre-TS) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Single-Large (H=64) | 0.0188 | 0.0201 | 0.1047 | 0.970 | 0.970 |
| Ensemble-4 (4×H=16) | 0.0347 | 0.0244 | 0.1270 | 0.964 | 0.941 |
| Single-Small (H=16) | 0.0196 | 0.0222 | 0.1483 | 0.963 | 0.966 |

Ensemble-4 has significantly higher ECE than Single-Large both pre-TS (p=6.1×10⁻⁵) and post-TS (p=0.011, mean difference +0.0043 ECE), and significantly worse post-TS NLL (p=5.8×10⁻⁴). Single-Small — despite having a quarter of Ensemble-4's parameters and no ensembling at all — matches or beats Ensemble-4 on ECE, though it has the worst NLL of the three, indicating it makes occasional confidently-wrong predictions that ECE's binned/coarse metric under-penalizes relative to NLL.

**Direction of the effect (signed ECE, mechanism check, digits, pre-TS):** Single-Large −0.0013 (essentially calibrated, marginally underconfident), Single-Small −0.0004 (essentially calibrated), Ensemble-4-Bootstrap −0.0229 (clearly **underconfident**), Ensemble-4-NoBoot −0.0142 (also underconfident, smaller magnitude). This directly falsifies the assumption that ensembling helps *because* it corrects overconfidence in this setting: our base small networks are not meaningfully overconfident to begin with (early-stopped, weight-decayed, high-accuracy MLPs), so the additional confidence-shrinkage from probability-averaging pushes them past calibrated into underconfident, which is what inflates ECE.

**Isolating the mechanism.** Post-TS ECE for Ensemble-4-NoBoot (0.0210) is not significantly different from either Ensemble-4-Bootstrap (0.0218, p=0.80) or Single-Large (0.0201, p=0.93). We had hoped this ablation would cleanly attribute the residual post-TS gap to bootstrap resampling vs. pure init-diversity; instead, with 15 seeds, neither pairwise comparison reaches significance in this follow-up. **We report this as inconclusive** rather than picking the more convenient interpretation — the direct experiment.py comparison (Ensemble-4-Bootstrap vs. Single-Large) does show a significant post-TS gap, but our attempt to explain *why* via the bootstrap/init decomposition did not resolve at this sample size. A larger seed count (50–100) would be needed to adjudicate between the two candidate mechanisms.

**Limitations of scale.** Both datasets are small (2000 and ~360 test examples respectively) and both architectures are single-hidden-layer MLPs; absolute ECE differences are on the order of 0.001–0.004, which is a real, statistically supported effect at our sample size but a small one in practical terms. We did not test convolutional architectures, deeper ensembles (M>4), or non-classification calibration (regression/quantile calibration). These are single-dataset-class findings (one synthetic, one small real tabular/image task) and should not be read as a general claim about ensembling and calibration beyond the fixed-total-width regime we tested.

## 4. Conclusion

At a fixed total parameter/width budget, splitting capacity across an ensemble of small networks does not reliably improve calibration over a single network of the same total size — on our real-data benchmark it measurably *hurts* calibration, both before and (to a smaller, but still statistically significant, degree) after temperature scaling. The mechanism is not the textbook "ensembling corrects overconfidence" story: our small, regularized base networks were already close to calibrated, and probability-averaging pushed them into mild underconfidence instead. We could not conclusively attribute the residual post-scaling gap to bootstrap resampling versus pure initialization diversity within our sample size, and we report that honestly as an open question rather than resolving it with an underpowered test. Practically, for small/embedded models under a hard capacity budget, a single appropriately-sized network plus temperature scaling is a simpler and at least equally well-calibrated alternative to a parameter-matched ensemble — the accuracy and compute cost of training/serving four models bought no calibration benefit here. Future work: repeat with 50+ seeds to adjudicate the bootstrap-vs-init-diversity mechanism question, extend to CNNs on image benchmarks (CIFAR-10/100) and to larger ensemble counts, and test whether the effect reverses once base networks are made deliberately overconfident (e.g., by removing weight decay/early stopping), which would more closely match the regime in which the original deep-ensemble calibration claims were made.
