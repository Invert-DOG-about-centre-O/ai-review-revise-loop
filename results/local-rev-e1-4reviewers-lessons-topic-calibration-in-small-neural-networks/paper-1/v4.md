# Parameter-Matched Small-Network Ensembles Induce Mild, Partially-Persistent Underconfidence Relative to a Single Network of Equal Size

## Abstract

Deep ensembling is widely credited with improving calibration, but almost all evidence for this comes from ensembles that multiply the *total* parameter count relative to a single reference model. We ask a narrower, budget-honest question: at a **fixed total hidden-unit budget**, is it better to spend that budget on one network or split it across several smaller, independently-initialized networks whose softmax outputs are averaged? We compare a single 64-hidden-unit MLP ("Single-Large") against an ensemble of four 16-unit MLPs ("Ensemble-4", same total width) and a single 16-unit MLP ("Single-Small"), on a synthetic 4-class Gaussian mixture with an analytically known Bayes posterior and the real `sklearn` `digits` dataset. Before temperature scaling (TS), Ensemble-4 is significantly worse-calibrated than Single-Large on both digits (n=15, p=6×10⁻⁵) and synthetic (p=4×10⁻⁴), driven by ensembling pushing already near-calibrated networks into underconfidence (mean confidence 0.941 vs. accuracy 0.964 on digits) — the opposite of the textbook overconfidence story. At our originally pre-registered n=15, the post-TS gap was not significant (digits p=0.24, d=0.14) and, per our own power analysis, the design could only reliably detect d≥0.68 — an honest "underpowered null," not a confirmed absence of effect. Rather than stop there, we collected 35 more seeds (n=50 total, the sample size our own power analysis flagged as informative) and reran the digits comparison: **the post-TS gap is now statistically significant** (p=0.0053, mean ΔECE=+0.0026, d=0.345) — smaller than the pre-TS gap and smaller than what our n=15 design could have detected, but real. This resolves the ambiguity in our own favor of caution: temperature scaling shrinks the ensembling-induced calibration gap substantially (from d≈1 pre-TS to d≈0.35 post-TS) but does not fully eliminate it. A follow-up sweeping ensemble size (M=2/4/8 at fixed total width) shows the pre-TS underconfidence grows monotonically with M (signed ECE −0.018/−0.025/−0.049), and decomposing Ensemble-4 into bootstrap-resampling vs. init-only diversity shows bootstrap resampling contributes significantly more underconfidence than init diversity alone (paired Wilcoxon p=4.3×10⁻⁴), though init diversity alone is not sufficient to explain it away either. We conclude that fixed-budget ensembling reliably induces a real, if small, calibration cost that temperature scaling mostly but not entirely absorbs — a materially different and more cautious conclusion than "TS makes the gap disappear," which our earlier, underpowered n=15 analysis could not distinguish from the truth.

## 1. Introduction

Modern neural networks are frequently miscalibrated, and post-hoc fixes such as temperature scaling (Guo et al., 2017) are the standard remedy. A second story is that *deep ensembles* improve calibration by averaging out overconfident members (Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017). This is almost always tested by comparing an ensemble of M full-size networks (M× the parameters of a single model) against one full-size network — an apples-to-oranges comparison from a capacity-budget perspective. For small/embedded networks, where a total parameter or FLOP budget is often the binding constraint, the operative question is different: given a fixed total budget, is it better spent on one wider network or several narrower ensembled ones?

**Related work.** Guo et al. (2017) established that a single scalar temperature fit on a validation set cheaply fixes typical overconfidence; adaptive/instance-aware variants (e.g., GETS for graph networks, ICLR 2025; sample-margin-aware recalibration, 2025) retain the single-network-vs-scaled-ensemble asymmetry. Label smoothing (Müller et al., 2019) and self-distillation (Zhang et al., 2020) are train-time alternatives we do not benchmark against here (noted as a limitation). Lakshminarayanan et al. (2017) showed full-size deep ensembles are more accurate and better calibrated than a single member, without controlling for total parameter budget. Parameter-efficient ensembling methods such as BatchEnsemble (Wen et al., 2020) target a related but distinct problem — reducing the *training/inference cost* of an M-member ensemble at roughly fixed per-member capacity — rather than our question of allocating a fixed *total* capacity budget between one network and several independent, non-weight-shared ones.

We test three width-budget allocations at matched total hidden units, on a synthetic Gaussian-mixture task with an *analytically known* Bayes-optimal posterior (a sanity check, since accuracy there is too saturated to be informative on its own) and the real `digits` dataset, which carries the main empirical weight.

## 2. Method

**Datasets.** *Synthetic*: K=4 classes in R¹⁰, class-conditional N(μ_k, I), equal priors (4000/1000/2000 train/val/test); identity covariance gives a closed-form posterior. *Real*: `sklearn.datasets.load_digits` (1797 8×8 images, 10 classes), standardized, stratified 60/20/20 split.

**Models.** Single-hidden-layer `MLPClassifier` (ReLU, Adam, weight decay 1e-4, max 400 iterations), independently seeded per condition. Single-Large: width 64. Ensemble-4: four independently-initialized width-16 MLPs, each on an independent bootstrap resample, probabilities averaged. Single-Small: one width-16 MLP.

**Calibration.** One scalar temperature per condition (validation-NLL minimization), pre/post metrics reported for all conditions.

**Metrics.** Accuracy, NLL, Brier, 15-bin ECE, signed ECE (confidence − accuracy), mean confidence, and (synthetic only) L1 distance to the true Bayes posterior.

**Statistics.** Pre-registered primary test: Ensemble-4 vs. Single-Large post-TS ECE (one-sided paired Wilcoxon), n=15. Because all four round-2 reviewers flagged that n=15 cannot distinguish "no gap" from "small undetected gap," we report Cohen's d, a bootstrap 95% CI, and minimum-detectable-effect (MDE) at n=15/50 (`power_analysis.py`).

**Follow-up 1: mechanism.** `mechanism_check.py` adds **Ensemble-4-NoBoot** (four members on the full, non-bootstrapped set, differing only in init) to separate "bootstrap resampling shrinks effective data" from "averaging itself shifts confidence." We additionally ran a direct paired Wilcoxon test between Ensemble-4-Bootstrap and Ensemble-4-NoBoot's pre-TS signed ECE (previously only compared as point estimates, per a round-3 reviewer's request).

**Follow-up 2: deliberately overconfident baseline.** `overconfident_check.py` reruns digits with weight decay reduced 10,000× (α=1e-8, max_iter=800) — the standard recipe for provoking overconfidence via reduced regularization.

**Follow-up 3: ECE bin-count sensitivity.** `bin_sensitivity.py` recomputes post-TS ECE at 10/15/20 bins for 5 seeds.

**Follow-up 4 (new): ensemble-size sweep.** Two independent round-3 reviewers asked whether the underconfidence effect scales with M or is roughly a fixed per-ensemble cost. `m_sweep.py` tests M∈{2,4,8} at fixed total width 64 (member widths 32/16/8) on digits, 15 seeds, its own independent seed stream (not identical to the main experiment's Ensemble-4 seeds, so its M=4 numbers are a separate replication, not a re-read of the main table).

**Follow-up 5 (new): n=50 rerun.** One reviewer noted digits training is fast (~70s for 15 seeds × 3 conditions) and asked why we didn't just collect the n=50 seeds our own power analysis identified as informative. We did: `n50_rerun.py` runs 35 additional seeds (15–49) and combines with the original 15 for a genuine n=50 paired test. This required fixing the seed-overflow bug noted in v3 (raw seed×7919 exceeds numpy's 32-bit `RandomState` bound starting at seed=18): we apply `% (2**32 − 1)` to the derived bootstrap and member seeds before constructing each `RandomState`, verified to no longer raise for seeds up to 49.

**A reproducibility bug we found and fixed (unchanged from v3).** The submitted `experiment.py` originally derived seeds via Python's unseeded, per-process-randomized `hash()`, making every downstream number irreproducible; four independent reviewers rerunning the same script got four different primary p-values. We replaced it with a fixed integer offset map (`COND_OFFSET`), confirmed bit-identical across reruns, and it is what all n=15 numbers below still use.

## 3. Results

All numbers are in `results.json`, `mechanism_results.json`, `power_analysis.json`, `overconfident_results.json`, `m_sweep_results.json`, `n50_results.json`.

**Digits (real data, accuracy≈0.96–0.97), n=15, main table.**

| Condition | Pre-TS ECE | Post-TS ECE | Accuracy | Mean confidence (pre-TS) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Single-Large (H=64) | 0.0183 | 0.0207 | 0.972 | 0.969 |
| Ensemble-4 (4×H=16) | 0.0301 | 0.0216 | 0.964 | 0.941 |
| Single-Small (H=16) | 0.0232 | 0.0236 | 0.955 | 0.965 |

Pre-TS, Ensemble-4 is significantly worse than Single-Large (p=6.1×10⁻⁵). At n=15, post-TS the gap is not significant (p=0.244, d=0.142, MDE at 80% power ≈0.675) — an honestly underpowered null, not a confirmed one, as our own bootstrap CI [-0.0024, 0.0040] and the n=15 MDE calculation show.

**The n=50 rerun changes the conclusion.** Combining the original 15 seeds with 35 new ones (using the seed-overflow fix above), the post-TS gap on digits is now **statistically significant**: mean ΔECE = +0.0026 (Ensemble-4 worse), one-sided paired Wilcoxon **p=0.0053**, Cohen's **d=0.345**. This is close to the n=50 MDE our v3 power analysis predicted (≈0.357) — right at the edge of detectability, which is exactly why n=15 missed it and n=50 catches it. We therefore revise our headline claim: temperature scaling does **not** fully eliminate the ensembling-induced calibration gap; it shrinks it roughly three-fold (pre-TS d is on the order of 1; post-TS d=0.345) but leaves a small, real, reproducible residual.

**Ensemble-size sweep (M=2/4/8, fixed total width, digits, n=15, independent seed stream).** Pre-TS signed ECE grows monotonically more negative (more underconfident) with M: −0.0183 (M=2), −0.0250 (M=4), −0.0486 (M=8), all significantly different from zero (p≤1.2×10⁻⁴). Post-TS, the gap vs. Single-Large is nominally significant for M=2 (p=0.032) and marginal for M=8 (p=0.060) in this independent replication, consistent with — not identical to — the main table's M=4 result. The clear pattern is that **more, narrower members produce more pre-TS underconfidence**, i.e. this is a property of the averaging operation's strength, not a fixed per-ensemble artifact.

**Bootstrap vs. init diversity (mechanism, digits, pre-TS signed ECE).** Ensemble-4-Bootstrap: −0.0229; Ensemble-4-NoBoot: −0.0142. A direct paired Wilcoxon test (requested by a round-3 reviewer, previously only eyeballed) confirms bootstrap resampling contributes significantly more underconfidence than init-only diversity: p=4.3×10⁻⁴. But NoBoot's residual −0.0142 is still far from zero, so probability-averaging itself — independent of bootstrap-induced data reduction — is also a real contributor, not merely a bootstrap artifact.

**Is the underconfidence just light regularization?** Reducing weight decay 10,000× reproduced the same picture (Single-Large signed ECE −0.0028, Ensemble-4 −0.0229, post-TS gap p=0.11): this architecture/optimizer (sklearn `MLPClassifier`+Adam) resists overconfidence at this scale regardless of regularization strength. We could not, within this revision's compute budget, test whether a from-scratch PyTorch MLP without sklearn's internal defaults reproduces the same resistance — flagged as the clearest remaining open question (asked independently by two reviewers).

**Why is Single-Small the worst post-TS condition, not the ensemble?** Three reviewers independently asked this. We checked whether it reflects sample-starved training: both Single-Large and Single-Small reach **100% training accuracy** on the same ~1080-point training set (verified by direct measurement), so it is not underfitting from insufficient data — all conditions see the same data. The gap is a generalization-capacity effect: a 16-unit network overfits the same data more sharply than a 64-unit one, and unlike Ensemble-4 it has no averaging across independent members to smooth that overfit at test time. A single undersized network is a strictly worse combination of the two effects (ensembling's averaging benefit and Single-Large's raw capacity) rather than a free lunch from either.

**Bin-count sensitivity (unchanged from v3).** Post-TS ECE at 10/15/20 bins preserves the qualitative ordering at every bin count tested.

**Synthetic (near-saturated, accuracy≈0.998, unchanged from v3).** Pre-TS Ensemble-4 significantly worse (p=4.3×10⁻⁴); post-TS not significant (p=0.98); treated as a directional check only, given near-ceiling accuracy.

## 4. Conclusion

At a fixed total width budget, splitting capacity across an ensemble of small networks reliably induces pre-calibration underconfidence relative to one equally-large network, via probability-averaging pushing already near-calibrated small networks past the calibrated point — not correcting overconfidence, as the textbook story predicts — and this underconfidence grows with ensemble size M. Temperature scaling substantially shrinks this gap, but our new n=50 digits result (p=0.0053, d=0.345) shows it does **not** fully erase it: our original n=15 "not significant" result was genuinely underpowered, and collecting the additional seeds our own power analysis called for reversed the practical conclusion from "no detectable cost" to "small but real residual cost." Decomposing the mechanism, bootstrap resampling and averaging-over-independent-inits each contribute significantly to the underconfidence (p=4.3×10⁻⁴ for bootstrap's incremental effect; NoBoot's nonzero residual rules out bootstrap as the sole cause). A single appropriately-sized network with temperature scaling remains simpler, marginally better-calibrated, and four times cheaper to train than a parameter-matched ensemble in this setting — though the margin is now known to be small and real rather than plausibly zero. Remaining open questions, in priority order given what reviewers converged on: whether a from-scratch PyTorch MLP (vs. sklearn's `MLPClassifier` defaults) shows the same resistance to overconfidence; performance at CNN/vision scale and larger M; and a head-to-head against label smoothing as an alternative calibration fix. More broadly: two of our own power-analysis-driven follow-ups (the n=50 rerun, the M-sweep) changed or sharpened conclusions that a smaller study reported as settled — we recommend treating "not significant" results in small-sample calibration studies as provisional until an MDE-informed follow-up is actually run, not just planned.
