# Parameter-Matched Small-Network Ensembles Do Not Reliably Improve Calibration Over a Single Network of Equal Size

## Abstract

Deep ensembling is widely credited with improving calibration, but almost all evidence for this comes from ensembles that multiply the *total* parameter count relative to a single reference model. We ask a narrower, budget-honest question: at a **fixed total hidden-unit budget**, is it better to spend that budget on one network or split it across several smaller, independently-initialized networks whose softmax outputs are averaged? We compare a single 64-hidden-unit MLP ("Single-Large") against an ensemble of four 16-unit MLPs ("Ensemble-4", same total width) and a single 16-unit MLP ("Single-Small"), on a synthetic 4-class Gaussian mixture with an analytically known Bayes posterior and the real `sklearn` `digits` dataset, across 15 independent seeds per condition. **Revision note:** an earlier version of this paper reported a statistically significant post-temperature-scaling calibration gap on digits (p=0.011); that number was generated by a script with an unseeded, per-process-random Python `hash()` call in its RNG-seed derivation, making every run non-reproducible — four independent reviewers each reran our code and got four different answers, one of them non-significant. We found and fixed the bug (replacing `hash()` with a fixed integer mapping) and reran everything; the code is now bit-identical across reruns. The corrected result changes our headline claim: before temperature scaling, Ensemble-4 is significantly worse-calibrated than Single-Large on both digits (p=6×10⁻⁵) and the synthetic task (p=4×10⁻⁴), driven by ensembling shrinking confidence *below* accuracy (mean confidence 0.941 vs. accuracy 0.964 on digits) — the opposite of the textbook overconfidence story. But after per-model temperature scaling, the gap is **no longer statistically significant on either dataset** (digits: post-TS ECE 0.0216 vs. 0.0207, p=0.24; synthetic: p=0.98). A follow-up ablation isolating bootstrap resampling from pure-init diversity likewise finds no significant difference (p=0.80). We conclude that fixed-budget ensembling reliably induces mild underconfidence before calibration, but temperature scaling — a near-free post-hoc fix — appears to absorb essentially all of the resulting calibration gap in our setting, undercutting the case for parameter-matched ensembling as a calibration tool for small/embedded models.

## 1. Introduction

Modern neural networks are frequently miscalibrated — their softmax confidences do not match empirical accuracy — and post-hoc fixes such as temperature scaling (Guo et al., 2017) are the standard remedy. A second, complementary story in the literature is that *deep ensembles* improve calibration by averaging out individual overconfident members (Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017). This claim, however, is almost always tested by comparing an ensemble of M full-size networks (M× the parameters and M× the compute of a single model) against a single full-size network — an apples-to-oranges comparison from a capacity-budget perspective. For small/embedded neural networks, where a total parameter or FLOP budget is often the binding constraint, the operative question is different: given a fixed total budget, is it better spent on one wider network or several narrower ensembled ones?

**Related work.** Guo et al. (2017) established that modern (larger, less regularized) networks are typically overconfident and that a single scalar temperature fit on a validation set is a cheap, effective fix; follow-up work has proposed adaptive/instance-aware and ensemble variants of temperature scaling (e.g., GETS for graph networks, ICLR 2025; sample-margin-aware recalibration, 2025) but these largely retain the single-network-vs-scaled-ensemble asymmetry. Label smoothing (Müller et al., 2019) and self-distillation (Zhang et al., 2020) are alternative train-time calibration tools. On ensembling, Lakshminarayanan et al. (2017) showed deep ensembles of full-size networks are both more accurate and better calibrated than a single member, but did not control for total parameter budget. To our knowledge the budget-matched question — many small vs. one equally-large — has not been directly isolated for calibration specifically, and it is exactly the comparison that matters when capacity itself (not just compute for training M models) is constrained, e.g. on-device inference.

We test three width-budget allocations at matched total hidden units, on two datasets: a synthetic Gaussian-mixture task with an *analytically known* Bayes-optimal posterior (sanity-checking that the qualitative story is not an artifact of empirical accuracy as a calibration proxy — accuracy there is too saturated to be very informative on its own), and the real `digits` dataset, which carries the main empirical weight of our conclusions.

## 2. Method

**Datasets.**
- *Synthetic*: K=4 classes in R¹⁰, class-conditional N(μ_k, I) with fixed random means, equal priors (4000 train / 1000 val / 2000 test). Because the covariance is identity and priors are equal, the true posterior is exactly softmax(x·μ_k − ½‖μ_k‖²), computable in closed form — used as an auxiliary sanity metric, not the headline result.
- *Real*: `sklearn.datasets.load_digits` (1797 8×8 images, 10 classes), standardized features, stratified 60/20/20 train/val/test split.

**Models.** All models are single-hidden-layer MLPs (`sklearn.neural_network.MLPClassifier`, ReLU, Adam, weight decay 1e-4, max 400 iterations), trained from an RNG seed derived independently for every (dataset, condition, seed) combination.
- **Single-Large**: one MLP, hidden width 64.
- **Ensemble-4**: four independently-initialized MLPs, hidden width 16 each (same *total* hidden units as Single-Large), each trained on an independent bootstrap resample of the training set; predictions averaged in probability space.
- **Single-Small**: one MLP, hidden width 16 (unensembled reference, same width as one ensemble member).

**Calibration.** For each condition we fit a single scalar temperature T (bounded scalar minimization of validation NLL, applied uniformly to single models and probability-averaged ensembles) and report metrics both pre- and post-scaling.

**Metrics** (test set): accuracy, NLL, multiclass Brier score, 15-bin ECE, signed ECE (confidence − accuracy), mean max-probability (confidence), and, for the synthetic task only, mean L1 distance between predicted and true Bayes posteriors.

**Statistics.** 15 independent seeds per (dataset, condition). We pre-registered Ensemble-4 vs. Single-Large ECE (post-TS) as the primary test, using a one-sided paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test (H1: Ensemble-4 ECE is larger); pre-TS and NLL versions are reported as exploratory/secondary.

**Follow-up mechanism check.** A second experiment (`mechanism_check.py`) adds a third ensemble variant, **Ensemble-4-NoBoot**, whose four members are trained on the *full*, non-bootstrapped training set and differ only in random initialization — isolating "bootstrap resampling shrinks effective per-member data" from "probability-averaging itself shifts confidence."

**A reproducibility bug we found and fixed.** In the version of `experiment.py` submitted for review, the per-condition RNG seed was derived as `seed * 31337 + hash(cname) % 1000`, where `cname` is a Python string (`'single_large'`, etc.) and `hash()` is Python's built-in string hash. Since Python 3.3, string hashing is randomized per-process by default (`PYTHONHASHSEED` is unset), so `hash(cname)` — and therefore every trained model's actual random seed and every downstream number in the paper — differed on every invocation of the script, despite the paper's claim that results were "regenerable by re-running these two scripts." All four independent reviewers of the first draft reran `experiment.py` unmodified and obtained different results for the primary pre-registered test, with reported p-values of 0.30, 0.032/0.009 (across two runs), 0.18/0.14, and the paper's originally-reported 0.011 — never converging. One reviewer additionally traced the exact cause by directly confirming `hash('ensemble4') % 1000` returned different values across separate `python -c` invocations. `mechanism_check.py` does not use `hash()` and reproduced correctly for all reviewers, which is why the bug was isolated to one script. We replaced the `hash()`-based offset with a fixed integer dictionary (`COND_OFFSET`) and confirmed two independent full reruns of the corrected script now produce bit-identical `results.json` output. All numbers below are from the corrected, verified-reproducible code.

## 3. Results

All raw numbers are in `results.json` and `mechanism_results.json`; both experiment scripts are included and reproduce every number below (`experiment.py`, `mechanism_check.py`; verified bit-identical across two independent reruns; combined runtime ≈100s on CPU).

**Synthetic (near-saturated, accuracy ≈0.998 for all conditions).** Pre-TS ECE: Single-Large 0.0025, Ensemble-4 0.0037, Single-Small 0.0033 (Ensemble-4 significantly worse than Single-Large, p=4.3×10⁻⁴). Post-TS ECE: 0.0019 / 0.0014 / 0.0014 respectively — statistically indistinguishable, and in fact Ensemble-4 is nominally *lower* here (p=0.98, one-sided test for Ensemble-4 > Single-Large). Post-TS NLL also not significantly different (p=0.96). Given the tiny absolute magnitudes (ECE std on the order of 0.0005–0.001 across seeds) and near-ceiling accuracy, we treat this dataset as a directional check on the pre-TS story, not a strong result on its own.

**Digits (real data, accuracy ≈0.96–0.97). This is the paper's main evidence.**

| Condition | Pre-TS ECE | Post-TS ECE | Post-TS NLL | Accuracy | Mean confidence (pre-TS) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Single-Large (H=64) | 0.0183 | 0.0207 | 0.1084 | 0.972 | 0.969 |
| Ensemble-4 (4×H=16) | 0.0301 | 0.0216 | 0.1194 | 0.964 | 0.941 |
| Single-Small (H=16) | 0.0232 | 0.0236 | 0.1460 | 0.955 | 0.965 |

Ensemble-4 has significantly higher ECE than Single-Large pre-TS (p=6.1×10⁻⁵, mean diff +0.0118), but **the post-TS gap is not statistically significant** (p=0.244, mean diff +0.0009 ECE, n=15) — a reversal of our first draft's headline claim, which reported p=0.011 for this exact comparison using the buggy, non-reproducible seeding. Post-TS NLL is only marginally different (p=0.060). Single-Small — a quarter of Ensemble-4's parameters and no ensembling — has the worst post-TS ECE and NLL of the three, suggesting undersized single networks are not automatically a free lunch either.

**Direction of the effect (signed ECE, mechanism check, digits, pre-TS):** Single-Large −0.0013 (essentially calibrated, marginally underconfident), Single-Small −0.0004 (essentially calibrated), Ensemble-4-Bootstrap −0.0229 (clearly **underconfident**), Ensemble-4-NoBoot −0.0142 (also underconfident, smaller magnitude). This is unchanged from the first draft and does not depend on the seeding bug (it uses `mechanism_check.py`, which was unaffected). It falsifies the assumption that ensembling helps *because* it corrects overconfidence here: our base small networks are not meaningfully overconfident to begin with, so probability-averaging pushes them past calibrated into mild underconfidence, which inflates pre-TS ECE — but this shift is largely correctable by a single scalar temperature.

**Isolating the mechanism.** Post-TS ECE for Ensemble-4-NoBoot (0.0210) is not significantly different from either Ensemble-4-Bootstrap (0.0218, p=0.80) or Single-Large (0.0205, p=0.93). This ablation remains inconclusive at 15 seeds: we cannot attribute what little post-TS gap remains to bootstrap resampling vs. pure init-diversity, and — per the corrected primary test above — there may be little real gap left to attribute once temperature scaling is applied.

**Limitations of scale.** Both datasets are small (2000 and ~360 test examples respectively) and both architectures are single-hidden-layer MLPs. We did not test convolutional architectures, deeper ensembles (M>4), or non-classification calibration. We also note the fixed-seed RNG scheme in `ensemble_run` overflows numpy's 32-bit seed bound for `seed ≳ 17` (`seed * 7919` exceeds 2³²−1), which we discovered while attempting a 50-seed robustness extension; this does not affect any number reported here (all use `N_SEEDS=15`) but would need a fix before extending seed count, and we flag it rather than silently working around it.

## 4. Conclusion

At a fixed total parameter/width budget, splitting capacity across an ensemble of small networks reliably induces measurable pre-calibration underconfidence relative to a single equally-large network — both on real data (digits, p=6×10⁻⁵) and a synthetic sanity check (p=4×10⁻⁴) — via probability-averaging pushing already near-calibrated small networks past the calibrated point rather than correcting overconfidence, the textbook story. However, after fixing a seeding bug that made our first-draft headline claim non-reproducible (confirmed independently by four reviewers rerunning our code and getting four different p-values), we now find this pre-TS gap is **not** statistically significant after standard per-model temperature scaling on either dataset (digits p=0.24, synthetic p=0.98). Our revised, corrected conclusion is narrower than our original one: fixed-budget ensembling is not a *reliable* route to better calibration than a single equally-sized network, but nor do we have solid evidence that it reliably *hurts* calibration once the standard, essentially free post-hoc fix (temperature scaling) is applied — that stronger claim depended on a bug. A single appropriately-sized network with temperature scaling remains a simpler, no-worse, and much cheaper alternative to a parameter-matched ensemble in our setting, since it needs one training run instead of four and post-TS calibration is statistically indistinguishable. We could not conclusively attribute the small residual post-TS gap to bootstrap resampling versus pure initialization diversity within our sample size. Future work: fix the 32-bit seed overflow and rerun with 50+ seeds to get a well-powered post-TS estimate and adjudicate the bootstrap-vs-init-diversity question; extend to CNNs on image benchmarks (CIFAR-10/100) and larger ensemble counts; and test whether a pre-TS ensembling advantage re-emerges once base networks are made deliberately overconfident (e.g., by removing weight decay/early stopping), closer to the regime in which the original deep-ensemble calibration claims were made. More broadly: given how easily an unseeded `hash()` call silently destroyed reproducibility here, we recommend that small-scale empirical ML papers verify bit-identical reruns of their own scripts *before* submission, not rely on reviewers to discover it — as happened in this case.
