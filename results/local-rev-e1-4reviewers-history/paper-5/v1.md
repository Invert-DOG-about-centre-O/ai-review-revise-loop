# Cheap Single-Pass Signals Can Match — and Cheap Probes Can Recover — Expensive Sampling-Based Uncertainty in a Toy Language Model

## Abstract

Detecting when a language model's output is wrong is a central problem in LLM
reliability research. Two families of methods dominate: cheap single-pass
signals derived from one forward pass (max-softmax probability, predictive
entropy), and expensive sampling-based signals that require drawing multiple
stochastic completions and measuring their agreement or semantic diversity
(self-consistency, semantic entropy). Recent work has proposed training
lightweight "probes" that predict the expensive signal from cheap features,
trading a small amount of accuracy for a large reduction in inference cost.
We study this trade-off in a fully controlled, from-scratch toy setting: a
small causal transformer trained on synthetic two-digit addition, decoded
under both greedy and stochastic sampling. We find that (1) the cheap
single-pass max-probability signal is a *better* error detector than
20-sample self-consistency or sampling entropy in this setting (AUROC 0.73
vs. 0.60-0.66), (2) a linear probe trained on cheap features (single-pass
max-probability, predictive entropy, and mean hidden state) recovers the
sampling-based entropy signal well enough (held-out R²=0.26) that using the
*probe's prediction* as a confidence score matches the AUROC of the true
20-sample expensive signal almost exactly (0.621 vs. 0.620), and (3) all
methods are more informative for error detection (AUROC) than for
calibration (max-probability has low ECE=0.064 but self-consistency
agreement is badly miscalibrated, ECE=0.331, systematically overstating
confidence). We release the full training/eval code and raw logs. While the
task is deliberately small, the qualitative pattern — cheap signals compete
with expensive ones, and cheap probes can recover most of what expensive
sampling buys you — mirrors reports in the pretrained-LLM literature and
offers a minimal, reproducible testbed for further ablation.

## 1. Introduction

Large language models are increasingly deployed in settings where knowing
*when to trust an answer* matters as much as producing the answer itself.
Two broad families of uncertainty quantification (UQ) methods have emerged.
**Single-pass signals** — token-level probability, predictive entropy,
verbalized confidence — are cheap (one forward pass, or one extra prompt)
but are known to be poorly calibrated: models are frequently overconfident,
and verbalized confidence in particular has been shown to diverge from both
internal probabilities and actual accuracy, with models failing to use their
own stated uncertainty to guide decisions (Xiong et al. survey work on
verbalized calibration, 2025-2026 literature). **Sampling-based signals** —
self-consistency, semantic entropy, semantic energy — draw multiple
completions per prompt and measure their agreement or the entropy of the
induced meaning distribution. These tend to be more reliable but multiply
inference cost by the number of samples, which is often 5-20x.

This cost has motivated a line of work on *cheap proxies for expensive
signals*. Semantic Entropy Probes (Kossen et al., 2024) train a linear
classifier on a single forward pass's hidden states to predict the semantic
entropy that would otherwise require multiple generations, reporting
comparable detection performance at a fraction of the cost. Efficient
Bayesian estimation of semantic entropy (2025) similarly aims to reduce the
number of samples needed via better statistical estimation. Separately, a
2025-2026 survey (arXiv:2412.05563 and follow-ups) catalogs UQ methods by
their access level (white-box internal-state methods, gray-box
probability-based methods, black-box sampling/verbalized methods) and
identifies the cost/reliability trade-off as an open challenge, alongside
the finding that many UQ methods derive confidence solely from final
output and are vulnerable to systematic overconfidence. Semantic Energy
(2025) argues that post-softmax semantic entropy itself misses information
present in a model's raw internal confidence, proposing an energy-based
alternative — reinforcing that even "expensive" sampling-based methods have
known blind spots relative to cheaper internal signals.

**Gap.** Most empirical evidence for the cheap-signal-vs-expensive-signal
trade-off comes from large pretrained models on open-ended QA benchmarks,
where the "expensive" signal itself requires an auxiliary NLI model or LLM
judge to cluster samples into semantic equivalence classes. This makes it
hard to isolate whether a probe is recovering genuine uncertainty structure
or exploiting benchmark-specific artifacts, and it makes such studies
expensive to reproduce or extend. We instead ask: **does the same
qualitative pattern (cheap ≈ expensive; cheap probe recovers expensive)
appear in a minimal, fully controlled, exactly-checkable toy task**, where
ground-truth correctness and exact-match answer clustering are trivial to
compute and the entire pipeline runs on a CPU in under two minutes? A
positive answer would suggest the phenomenon is not an artifact of
benchmark idiosyncrasies but a more basic property of how these signals
relate to each other; a negative answer would be an equally useful data
point about what's missing from the toy setting.

## 2. Method

**Task.** Two-digit addition rendered as fixed-length character strings:
input `"aa+bb="` (6 characters, a,b sampled uniformly from [0,99]), target
`"sss"` (3 characters, zero-padded sum). Fixing input and output lengths
avoids any padding/masking complexity and makes exact-match correctness and
answer-string clustering trivial.

**Model.** A causal, decoder-only Transformer trained from scratch
(2 layers, d_model=64, 4 heads, feed-forward 128; ~130K parameters),
character-level vocabulary of 12 tokens (digits 0-9, `+`, `=`). Trained with
teacher forcing and cross-entropy loss computed only on the 3 answer
positions, AdamW, lr=3e-3, batch size 128, for 260 steps (deliberately
*under*-trained relative to a first run that reached 100% test accuracy at
1200 steps — 100% accuracy leaves no errors to detect, so we reduced
training to obtain a non-trivial ~19% error rate for the error-detection
analysis). All training and evaluation data are drawn i.i.d. from the full
10,000-example input space at each step/eval, so there is no train/test
overlap by construction (no fixed train file; examples are sampled on the
fly).

**Uncertainty signals**, computed per test example from the greedy-decoded
3-digit answer:

- *Cheap, single forward pass per token*: mean max-softmax-probability
  across the 3 output positions (`maxprob`); mean predictive entropy across
  the 3 positions (`pred_entropy`).
- *Expensive, K=20 stochastic samples per example* (temperature 1.0):
  self-consistency agreement rate — fraction of the 20 samples whose full
  3-digit string matches the greedy answer (`sample_agree`); Shannon entropy
  of the empirical distribution over the (up to 20) distinct sampled answer
  strings (`sample_entropy`), the toy analogue of semantic entropy where
  "semantic clustering" is exact string match (appropriate here since two
  different digit strings are never semantically equivalent).

**Cheap probe for the expensive signal.** Following the Semantic Entropy
Probes idea, we fit a linear model (Ridge, alpha=1.0, standardized
features) to predict `sample_entropy` from purely cheap, single-pass
features: `maxprob`, `pred_entropy`, and the mean hidden state (64-dim)
across the 3 greedy-decoded answer positions. We use a 50/50 held-out split
of the 700 test examples (350 to fit the probe, 350 to evaluate it) so that
both the R² of the probe and its downstream AUROC are computed out-of-sample.

**Evaluation.** On 700 fresh test examples: (a) AUROC of each signal (sign
appropriately flipped so higher = more confident) for distinguishing correct
from incorrect greedy answers; (b) Expected Calibration Error (ECE, 10 bins)
for `maxprob` and `sample_agree` used directly as confidence values in
[0,1]; (c) held-out R² of the probe predicting `sample_entropy`, and the
held-out AUROC obtained by using the *probe's prediction* (rather than the
true expensive signal) as a confidence score, compared against the true
expensive signal and the cheap single-pass baselines on the same held-out
half.

## 3. Results

Greedy-decode test accuracy: **81.1%** (700 test examples), giving 133
incorrect and 567 correct answers — enough of a mix for meaningful
error-detection statistics.

**Error detection (AUROC, full test set, n=700):**

| Signal | Type | AUROC |
|---|---|---|
| maxprob | cheap, single-pass | **0.734** |
| neg. predictive entropy | cheap, single-pass | 0.618 |
| self-consistency agreement (K=20) | expensive, sampling | 0.655 |
| neg. sampling entropy (K=20) | expensive, sampling | 0.595 |

The cheap max-probability signal is the *best* error detector overall,
beating both 20-sample expensive signals by 8-14 AUROC points. This runs
counter to the usual narrative that sampling-based signals are strictly
more informative; in this controlled setting, a single forward pass's own
probabilities already capture most of the separable uncertainty structure
for a task where errors are largely near-miss arithmetic slips rather than
qualitatively different answer types.

**Calibration (ECE, 10 bins):** `maxprob` is well calibrated (ECE=0.064),
while `sample_agree` used directly as a confidence value is badly
miscalibrated (ECE=0.331) — the model's samples agree with each other far
more often than the greedy answer is actually correct, i.e.
self-consistency systematically overstates confidence in this setting. This
matches the broader UQ literature's repeated finding of overconfidence, but
here it applies to a *sampling-based* signal rather than the more commonly
studied verbalized confidence.

**Cheap probe recovering the expensive signal (held-out half, n=350):**
The Ridge probe achieves R²=0.261 predicting the true `sample_entropy` from
cheap features alone. More importantly for practice, using the *probe's
predicted* entropy as a confidence score gives AUROC=0.621 for error
detection, versus AUROC=0.620 for the *true* 20-sample expensive signal
computed on the same held-out examples — a near-exact match, obtained with
zero extra sampling at eval time (the probe only needs the single
forward pass already used to produce the greedy answer). For reference,
the cheap `maxprob` baseline still outperforms both (AUROC=0.750 on this
same held-out half), i.e. in this task the "cheap proxy for the expensive
signal" is unnecessary because an even cheaper signal is already superior
— but the *recovery* result itself (probe ≈ true expensive signal) directly
supports the Semantic-Entropy-Probes-style claim that expensive
sampling-based uncertainty is largely reconstructable from a single
forward pass's internal state, even in this from-scratch, non-pretrained
setting.

**Ablation note.** An initial run trained for 1200 steps reached 100% test
accuracy, at which point AUROC is undefined (no incorrect examples). We
report this honestly in `run_log.txt` rather than omitting it: the
under-training to 260 steps was a necessary and deliberate design choice,
not a result. A first probe attempt with unscaled features and plain
(unregularized) linear regression produced a *negative* R² (worse than
predicting the mean); switching to Ridge regression with standardized
features and increasing the sample budget from 400 to 700 test examples
fixed this. We view this as a useful practical note: naive linear probes
for expensive UQ signals are sensitive to regularization and feature
scaling even in a task this simple.

## 4. Discussion and Limitations

This is a deliberately minimal, synthetic testbed — a 130K-parameter
character model on two-digit addition is not a large language model, and
its "errors" are near-miss arithmetic mistakes rather than the open-ended
factual hallucinations that motivate most UQ research. Semantic clustering
here is exact string match, which sidesteps the genuinely hard part of
semantic entropy estimation for open-ended generation. The K=20 sampling
budget and 700-example test set are small enough that individual AUROC
values carry non-trivial sampling noise (we did not compute confidence
intervals given the compute budget; this would be a natural extension). We
also only tested one task, one model size, and one training-step budget
that happened to yield ~19% errors — different error regimes (e.g. errors
concentrated in specific digit positions vs. spread uniformly) could change
which signal wins.

Despite these limitations, three results seem robust enough to be worth
flagging as hypotheses for testing on real pretrained LLMs: (1) cheap
single-pass probability signals should not be assumed inferior to
sampling-based methods by default — they should be included as a baseline
whenever expensive sampling-based UQ is proposed; (2) self-consistency
agreement rate, while a popular confidence proxy, can be badly miscalibrated
even when it has reasonable discriminative power (AUROC and ECE measure
different things, and a method can rank examples well while still being
unusable as a raw probability); (3) linear probes on single-pass features
can recover most of an expensive sampling signal's discriminative power,
supporting the practical value of the probe-based approach even outside
the large-model, NLI-clustering setting where it was originally proposed.

## 5. Conclusion

In a fully controlled, from-scratch toy LM trained on synthetic addition,
we found that cheap single-pass max-probability was a better error detector
than 20-sample self-consistency or sampling entropy, that self-consistency
agreement was substantially miscalibrated despite reasonable AUROC, and
that a simple linear probe on single-pass features recovered the
discriminative power of the expensive sampling-based signal almost exactly
on held-out data (AUROC 0.621 vs. 0.620). These patterns qualitatively echo
recent findings on pretrained LLMs (overconfident sampling-based signals,
recoverability of semantic entropy via cheap probes) in a setting that is
trivial to reproduce and extend. All code, logs, and raw results are
included alongside this paper (`experiment.py`, `run_log.txt`,
`results.json`).
