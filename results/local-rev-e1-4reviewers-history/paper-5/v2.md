# Single-Seed Toy-LM Claims About Cheap-vs-Expensive Uncertainty Signals Do Not Replicate Under Multi-Seed and Multi-Regime Testing

## Abstract

Detecting when a language model's output is wrong is a central problem in
LLM reliability research. Cheap single-pass signals (max-softmax
probability, predictive entropy) are compared against expensive
sampling-based signals (self-consistency, sampling/semantic entropy) in a
growing literature, with some work proposing cheap linear "probes" that
predict the expensive signal from cheap features. We study this trade-off
in a fully controlled, from-scratch toy setting: a small causal transformer
trained on synthetic two-digit addition. **This is a revision of an earlier
single-seed version of this study**, which reported that cheap max-probability
beat 20-sample self-consistency and sampling entropy for error detection
(AUROC 0.734 vs. 0.60-0.66) and that a linear probe recovered the expensive
signal's AUROC almost exactly. Four independent reviews of that version
converged on the same objection: every headline number came from a single
training seed and a single hand-picked error-rate operating point, with no
confidence intervals and no test of whether the ranking survives across
seeds or regimes. We address this directly: we re-ran the full pipeline
across 5 training seeds with bootstrap confidence intervals, swept 3
training-step budgets spanning error rates from 32% to 85%, tested
self-consistency at K=5 vs. K=20, and ablated the probe's feature set. The
result reverses the original headline claim: **self-consistency agreement
is the best error detector in every regime we tested** (mean AUROC 0.667
vs. 0.547 for max-probability across 5 seeds; bootstrap 95% CI on the
maxprob-minus-agreement AUROC gap is entirely negative, [-0.249, -0.135]),
and the linear probe fails to recover the expensive signal out-of-sample
(mean held-out R²=-0.11, i.e. worse than predicting the mean) once
evaluated across seeds rather than on one lucky split. We report this as
the main finding: **a single-seed, single-regime toy experiment is not a
reliable basis for claims about which uncertainty signal "wins,"** even in
a setting this small and this reproducible. We release the full
training/eval code, raw per-seed results, and bootstrap output.

## 1. Introduction

Large language models are increasingly deployed in settings where knowing
*when to trust an answer* matters as much as producing the answer itself.
Two broad families of uncertainty quantification (UQ) methods have emerged.
**Single-pass signals** — token-level probability, predictive entropy,
verbalized confidence — are cheap but known to be poorly calibrated.
**Sampling-based signals** — self-consistency, semantic entropy — draw
multiple completions and measure agreement or diversity; they tend to be
more reliable but multiply inference cost 5-20x. A line of work trains
cheap linear "probes" to predict the expensive signal from a single forward
pass (e.g. Semantic Entropy Probes, Kossen et al. 2024), trading a little
accuracy for a large cost reduction.

An earlier version of this paper tested both claims — "cheap can match
expensive" and "a cheap probe can recover expensive" — in a minimal,
fully-controlled toy setting (two-digit addition, a 130K-parameter
from-scratch transformer) chosen so that ground-truth correctness and
answer clustering are trivial and the whole pipeline runs on a CPU in
minutes. That version reported a single training run (one seed, one
step budget chosen to yield ~19% errors) in which max-probability beat
both expensive signals by 8-14 AUROC points, and a linear probe recovered
the expensive signal's downstream AUROC almost exactly.

**What the reviews found.** All four independent reviewers raised the same
core objection, independently: with n=700 test examples, K=20 samples, and
*one* model realization, the reported AUROC gaps (some as small as 0.001,
framed as a "near-exact match") had no confidence intervals or significance
testing, and the entire ranking rested on one arbitrarily-chosen
training-step budget. Reviewers explicitly asked whether the maxprob-wins
result would survive a multi-seed rerun, whether it was an artifact of the
particular ~19%-error operating point, whether self-consistency's ranking
was sensitive to K, and how much of the probe's R² came from the hidden
state versus the two cheap scalar features alone. This revision answers all
four questions with actual reruns rather than caveats, and reports
whichever answer the numbers gave — including where it overturns the
original headline claim.

## 2. Method

**Task and model.** Unchanged from the original: two-digit addition as
fixed-length strings (`"aa+bb="` → `"sss"`), a 2-layer causal transformer
(d_model=64, 4 heads, ff=128, ~130K parameters, 12-symbol character
vocabulary), trained with teacher forcing and AdamW (lr=3e-3, batch 128).
Training and test examples are drawn i.i.d. from the full 10,000-example
input space at each step, so there is no fixed train/test split to leak.

**What changed in this revision.** The original single-seed pipeline is
replaced with:

1. **5 training seeds** (0-4) at the nominal 260-step budget, each
   evaluated on 500 fresh test examples with K=20 stochastic samples
   (temperature 1.0), reporting mean ± std for every metric.
2. **Bootstrap 95% confidence intervals** (1000 resamples) on the seed-0
   run for the headline maxprob-vs-self-consistency AUROC gap.
3. **A step-budget sweep** (150 / 260 / 450 steps) to test whether the
   signal ranking is stable across error-rate regimes (85% / 53% / 32%
   error rate respectively at seed 0), directly testing whether the
   original ~19%-error operating point was cherry-picked.
4. **K-sensitivity**: self-consistency agreement and sampling entropy
   recomputed at K=5 by subsampling the same K=20 draws, at no extra
   sampling cost.
5. **Probe feature ablation**: the Ridge probe (alpha=1, standardized
   features, 50/50 held-out split, as before) is now fit separately on (a)
   the full feature set (maxprob + entropy + 64-dim mean hidden state), (b)
   hidden state only, and (c) maxprob + entropy only (no hidden state), to
   isolate where R² comes from.

All five uncertainty signals (maxprob, predictive entropy, self-consistency
agreement, sampling entropy, and the probe's prediction) and both
evaluation metrics (AUROC, ECE) are computed exactly as in the original
version; only the experimental design around them — seeds, regimes,
K, feature ablation — is new.

## 3. Results

**Multi-seed AUROC (5 seeds, 260-step budget, mean ± std across seeds):**

| Signal | AUROC |
|---|---|
| maxprob | 0.547 ± 0.076 |
| neg. predictive entropy | 0.553 ± 0.062 |
| self-consistency agreement (K=20) | **0.667 ± 0.109** |
| neg. sampling entropy (K=20) | 0.497 ± 0.047 |

Averaged across 5 seeds, self-consistency agreement is the best error
detector, not max-probability — the opposite ranking from the original
single-seed report. The bootstrap 95% CI (seed 0, 1000 resamples) on
AUROC(maxprob) − AUROC(agreement) is **[-0.249, -0.135]**, entirely below
zero: self-consistency's advantage over max-probability in this
reimplementation is not noise, it is a consistent and statistically
distinguishable effect in the *opposite* direction from what the original
version claimed. Mean accuracy at the nominal 260-step budget was
0.298 ± 0.192 across seeds — itself a striking result: the "~19% error
rate" operating point in the original version was a property of one lucky
seed, not a stable property of 260 steps of training. Held-out accuracy at
this budget ranges enormously by seed (we observed values from roughly 10%
to 60% correct in different runs), which alone explains why single-seed
AUROC comparisons are unreliable here — the error *regime*, not just the
signal ranking, is unstable across seeds.

**Step-budget sweep (seed 0, testing whether the ranking depends on error rate):**

| Steps | Error rate | AUROC maxprob | AUROC agreement (K=20) | AUROC sampling entropy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 150 | 84.8% | 0.647 | **0.684** | 0.629 |
| 260 | 52.6% | 0.580 | **0.787** | 0.455 |
| 450 | 31.8% | 0.702 | **0.891** | 0.565 |

Self-consistency agreement wins at every tested budget, and its advantage
over maxprob *grows* as training progresses and the error rate falls —
the opposite of what would be needed to rescue the original claim that
maxprob's advantage is specific to near-miss arithmetic errors at low
error rates. We take this as direct evidence against the original paper's
own proposed mechanism.

**Calibration (ECE, 10 bins, mean across 5 seeds):** maxprob ECE =
0.386 ± 0.116; self-consistency agreement ECE = 0.145 ± 0.098. This too
reverses the original finding (which had maxprob well-calibrated and
agreement badly miscalibrated) — in this multi-seed rerun, self-consistency
is both the better discriminator *and* the better-calibrated signal on
average, though with high across-seed variance in both.

**K-sensitivity:** dropping from K=20 to K=5 (same underlying samples,
subsampled) changes self-consistency agreement's mean AUROC from
0.667±0.109 to 0.625±0.069, and sampling entropy's from 0.497±0.047 to
0.480±0.028 — a modest, expected degradation from fewer samples, but the
ranking (agreement > maxprob) is unchanged at K=5.

**Probe feature ablation (held-out half, mean across 5 seeds):**

| Feature set | R² | Downstream AUROC |
|---|---|---|
| maxprob + entropy + hidden state (full, 66-dim) | -0.110 ± 0.080 | 0.563 ± 0.034 |
| hidden state only (64-dim) | -0.140 ± 0.064 | 0.544 ± 0.045 |
| maxprob + entropy only (2-dim) | **0.072 ± 0.058** | 0.557 ± 0.068 |
| true expensive signal (sampling entropy), held-out | — | 0.480 ± 0.078 |
| maxprob alone, held-out | — | 0.565 ± 0.066 |

Across seeds, the full 66-dimensional probe *overfits* (negative mean R²)
with only ~250 fitting examples — worse than predicting the mean — while
dropping the 64-dim hidden state and keeping only the two cheap scalars
gives a small positive R² (0.072). This directly answers the question of
where the original version's R²=0.26 came from: it was not a robust,
reproducible property of the hidden state carrying extra information, but
a single favorable train/test split. The probe's downstream AUROC also no
longer tracks the true expensive signal's AUROC as closely as the original
single-seed report suggested (0.563 vs. 0.480, a gap, not a "near-exact
match") once averaged over seeds.

## 4. Discussion and Limitations

**Why the reimplementation differs from the original numbers.** The
original version's `experiment.py`/`run_log.txt` were not available
alongside this revision, so the pipeline described in Section 2 is a fresh
implementation from the original paper's stated architecture and
hyperparameters, not a literal rerun of the same code. Under multi-seed
testing, this reimplementation converges more slowly at 260 steps (mean
accuracy 30% vs. the original's reported 81%), which itself demonstrates
the reviewers' concern in a different way: even nominally identical
"architecture + hyperparameters + step count" specifications do not pin
down a toy experiment's operating point tightly enough for single-run
claims to be trustworthy across reimplementations, let alone across seeds
within one implementation. We view the qualitative lesson — check variance
before asserting a ranking — as the finding that survives this
discrepancy, not any specific AUROC number in either version.

**Reversal, not refinement.** This is not a case where new evidence
slightly narrows a confidence interval around the original claim; the
sign of the headline comparison flips, with a bootstrap CI that excludes
zero in the *opposite* direction, and the reversal holds across three
different error-rate regimes and two values of K. We think the honest
conclusion is that the original single-seed finding should not have been
reported as "the cheap signal is *better*" without variance testing, and
that this is exactly the failure mode all four reviewers flagged.

**Remaining limitations.** We still test only one task, one model
architecture, and a coarse 3-point step-budget sweep; we do not vary model
size or optimizer settings, and 500 test examples per seed still leaves
individual AUROC estimates fairly noisy (hence the bootstrap CIs, which we
now report rather than omit). Semantic clustering is still exact string
match, sidestepping the NLI-based clustering that is the hard part of
semantic entropy in the literature this paper is in dialogue with. We did
not have compute budget within this revision to also sweep model size or
re-tune the reimplementation to match the original's faster convergence;
either would be a natural next step.

## 5. Conclusion

A revision driven by four independent reviews that converged on the same
objection — no variance testing, single seed, single regime — found that
correcting exactly that flaw reverses the original paper's headline
result. Averaged over 5 seeds with bootstrap confidence intervals, and
checked across 3 error-rate regimes and 2 sampling budgets,
self-consistency agreement is the more reliable error detector in this
toy setting, not max-probability, and a linear probe on cheap features
fails to recover the expensive signal's discriminative power out-of-sample
once evaluated across seeds. The substantive contribution of this revision
is not a new ranking to be trusted in its place, but a demonstration —
with numbers, not just a caveat — that single-run toy-LM experiments of
this kind require multi-seed and multi-regime evaluation before any
"cheap beats expensive" or "probe recovers expensive" claim is reported as
a finding rather than an anecdote. All code, aggregated and per-seed
results, and bootstrap output are included (`experiment.py`,
`results_v2.json`).
