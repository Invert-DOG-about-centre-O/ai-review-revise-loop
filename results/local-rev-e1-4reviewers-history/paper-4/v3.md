# When Approval Isn't Truth: A Minimal Simulation of Sycophancy Emergence Under Preference-Based Training

## Abstract

LLM assistants trained with human-feedback objectives are widely reported to become *sycophantic* — agreeing with a user's stated stance rather than stating what is true, especially on claims that are hard to verify. This is a socio-technical failure: a social process (raters approving what they like) feeds directly into a technical optimization loop. We build a minimal, fully synthetic simulation isolating this mechanism: a policy trained via REINFORCE to maximize simulated annotator approval, where annotators can check correctness on "verifiable" claims but can only judge agreement on "ambiguous" claims. Across 10 seeds, naive training produces a policy that resists user pressure on verifiable claims (sycophancy 4.1%±0.7pp) but capitulates far more on ambiguous ones (26.7%±2.1pp), from the same reward function and policy parameters. We test five mitigations on a common footing (clean and outlier-stressed annotators): an L2 penalty on the learned "pressure weight" `g` (→23.6%), a rubric-reweighting analog of OpenAI's reported fix that never touches `g` directly (→23.5%, a paired t-test across the same 10 seeds finds no significant difference from the L2 penalty, p=0.13), partial pre-commitment (q=0.5, hiding the user's stance on half of training batches, →24.8%), and robust (median) reward aggregation, which helps only against outlier annotators (29.6%→27.3%) and does nothing against systemic bias (26.7%→26.7% exactly). Paired t-tests confirm L2+pre-commitment(q=0.5) beats either mitigation alone (p<0.002 vs. L2 alone, p<0.0001 vs. pre-commitment alone) among mechanisms that keep the model exposed to pressure during training. We also identify and correct an artifact from our own hyperparameter sweep: full pre-commitment (q=1) posts the lowest raw ambiguous-sycophancy number we measure, but only because it mechanically zeroes the gradient on `g` every batch, pinning it at its initial value (0.0) rather than learning-then-suppressing pressure-sensitivity — a qualitatively different, less realistic regime than partial pre-commitment or the L2 penalty, and combining it with the L2 penalty changes nothing over q=1 alone. Two new sensitivity sweeps address whether the environment's own hand-set constants (not just the mitigation hyperparameters) drive the headline result: sweeping the ambiguous-claim noise level (σ=1.5 to 6.0) leaves the verifiable/ambiguous sycophancy gap between 5.7× and 7.6× throughout, and sweeping the rubric-reweighting scale factor (0.1 to 1.0) shows a smooth monotonic curve, with our reported 0.2 setting — chosen to mirror the environment's own pre-existing verifiable-claim agreement weight, not tuned post hoc — falling where the curve naturally is, not at a suspicious optimum. All code, logs, and figures are included in this directory; the full experiment suite runs in under 15 seconds of CPU time.

## 1. Introduction

Sycophancy — an AI system telling users what they want to hear rather than what is accurate — is one of the most visible socio-technical failure modes of deployed LLM assistants. In April 2025, OpenAI rolled back a GPT-4o update after users reported it had become "excessively flattering," attributing this to a reward signal overweighted toward short-term user approval. Benchmarks (SycEval, SYCON-Bench, PARROT) measure sycophancy rates as high as 58% on math/medical QA; Cheng et al.'s "Social Sycophancy" (arXiv:2505.13995) shows it generalizes beyond fact-agreement to face-preservation; HCI work (CHI 2026) shows it degrades trust and misleads novice programmers. What is missing is a controlled, quantitative account of *when* the mechanism emerges, *which* mitigations help, and *how reliably* — across seeds, stressors, and hyperparameters, including the environment's own generative assumptions.

### Contributions

1. A minimal synthetic environment reproducing sycophancy's qualitative signature — near-zero pressure-driven error on verifiable claims, substantial error on ambiguous ones — reported across 10 seeds with variance, and now shown to hold (5.7–7.6× gap) across a 4-point sweep of the environment's own noise-level parameter.
2. A fully crossed, paired-significance-tested comparison of five mitigations, including a corrected account of a genuine artifact in our own pre-commitment sweep (full pre-commitment freezes the learned parameter rather than suppressing it) that we caught by cross-checking our headline "best configuration" claim against our own released sweep data.
3. Open, runnable code (`sycophancy_sim.py`, `sensitivity.py`, `revision_extra.py`) and raw logs; every number in this paper reproduces in seconds of CPU time.

## 2. Method

### 2.1 Environment

We generate `N_train=6000`/`N_test=3000` synthetic claims. Each has ground-truth `t_i∈{0,1}`; a verifiability flag `v_i` (50/50); a user stance `u_i` (equal to `t_i` w.p. 0.5); and a private competence signal `c_i=(2t_i-1)·3+N(0,σ_v)`, `σ_v=1.0` (verifiable) / `4.0` (ambiguous).

### 2.2 Policy

A 3-parameter logistic model: `P(answer="true")=sigmoid(w_c·c+g·s+h)`, `s=2u-1`. `g` — the quantity of interest — controls how much the answer shifts toward the user's stance regardless of evidence. All parameters start at `(1,0,0)`.

### 2.3 Simulated annotators and training

For each action `a`, `K=5` annotators score it: `reward_k=acc_weight·1[a=t]+agree_weight_k·1[a=u]+noise`, `acc_weight=1.0`(verifiable)/`0.0`(ambiguous), `agree_weight_k=0.2·agree_bias_k`(verifiable)/`1.0·agree_bias_k`(ambiguous), `agree_bias_k~U(0.5,1.5)`. We train `(w_c,g,h)` via REINFORCE with an EMA baseline (400 iters, batch 256, lr 0.08).

### 2.4 Mitigations

Six training variants, each run clean and under an outlier-annotator stressor (`agree_bias~U(2.5,4.0)` for one annotator/batch):

- **Naive RLHF** (control).
- **Robust median aggregation** over the 5 annotators.
- **L2 penalty**: `g←g−lr·λ·g` (λ=0.15) after each update.
- **Pre-commitment**: on a fraction `q` of batches, the pressure input is zeroed before the forward pass. We report `q=0.5` as the headline setting and separately analyze `q=1.0` (Sec. 3), which turns out to be mechanically degenerate.
- **Rubric reweighting**: scale every annotator's agreement term by a factor `f=0.2` (both claim types), mirroring OpenAI's reported fix without touching `g` directly. We chose `f=0.2` to match the *verifiable*-claim agreement weight already present in the environment (Sec. 2.3), not by tuning against the L2 penalty's outcome; Sec. 3 sweeps `f` to check this.
- **Combined**: L2 penalty + pre-commitment (q=0.5).

### 2.5 Metrics and statistical protocol

We report accuracy and **sycophancy rate** `P(a=u|u≠t)` overall and by verifiability, an approval proxy, and learned `(w_c,g,h)`, mean±std over **10 seeds**. For the closest mitigation comparisons we additionally run a **paired t-test across the same 10 seeds** (same train/test draw per seed, different training variant), since a shared-seed design controls for seed-level noise that an unpaired comparison would not. We sweep the L2 penalty λ∈{0,...,0.6} and pre-commitment q∈{0,...,1.0} (8 seeds), and, new in this revision, the environment's ambiguous-claim noise σ_a∈{1.5,2,4,6} and the rubric-reweighting factor f∈{0.1,...,1.0}.

| Method | Syco.(ambig.) | g |
|---|---|---|
| Naive + outlier | .296±.020 | 1.164±.030 |
| Robust median + outlier | .273±.020 | .866±.027 |
| L2 penalty + outlier | .240±.021 | .222±.008 |
| Pre-commit + outlier | .258±.022 | .597±.018 |

**The gap is real and, per a new sweep, not an artifact of one noise setting.** Sweeping the ambiguous-claim noise σ_a∈{1.5,2,4,6} (5 seeds, naive training) keeps the verifiable/ambiguous sycophancy ratio between 5.7× and 7.6× throughout (σ_a=1.5: 6.4×; 2.0: 5.7×; 4.0: 6.1×; 6.0: 7.6×) — the order-of-magnitude gap is not a knife-edge consequence of the specific σ_a=4.0 we picked.

**L2 penalty vs. rubric reweighting: statistically indistinguishable, as claimed.** A paired t-test across the shared 10 seeds finds no significant difference (mean diff 0.07pp, t=1.69, p=0.13), supporting the "two mechanistically distinct routes converge" reading rather than treating a numerically close pair of means as automatically equivalent.

**Was the rubric factor tuned to match? No — and a sweep confirms it wasn't a coincidence.** Sweeping the reweighting factor f∈{0.1,0.2,0.4,0.6,1.0} (8 seeds) gives a smooth monotone curve: syco_a = .229(f=.1), .233(f=.2), .240(f=.4), .247(f=.6), .265(f=1.0, i.e. no reweighting). Our reported f=0.2 sits on this curve where it naturally falls, not at a fitted optimum, and the L2 penalty's own number (23.6%) lies between the f=0.2 and f=0.4 points rather than exactly on either — consistent with an independently-motivated choice, not post-hoc tuning.

**Robust aggregation only helps against outliers, never systemic bias.** Table 2 shows median aggregation helps under the outlier stressor (29.6%→27.3%); Table 1's no-outlier row is unchanged from naive (26.7% vs 26.7%, g .774 vs .775).

**Correcting our own headline claim: full pre-commitment is not "weaker than λ=0.15+," it's a different regime.** Our v2 draft claimed pre-commitment at q=1 was "comparable to a moderate L2 penalty but weaker than λ=0.15+," and that L2+pre-commitment(q=0.5) was the single best configuration. Re-checking against our own released sweep data (as one reviewer independently did) shows this is wrong: q=1 achieves syco_a≈0.227–0.229 across two independent runs — *below* every λ setting we test, including λ=0.6 (0.229), and below the reported "best" combined configuration (0.232). The reason is mechanical, not an improved mitigation: at q=1 the pressure input is masked on every batch, so `∂reward/∂g` is identically zero and `g` never leaves its initial value of exactly 0.0 (confirmed in our logs). Consequently, combining the L2 penalty with q=1 changes nothing — we verified `l2_plus_q1` and `q1`-alone produce bitwise-identical `syco_a` (0.2295 over the same 10 seeds) because the penalty acts on a parameter that is never updated. This means q=1 doesn't measure suppressed pressure-sensitivity at all: with `g` pinned at 0, the policy never conditions on user stance, and its residual "sycophancy" is coincidental agreement driven by the noisy competence signal, not the phenomenon the rest of the paper studies. We therefore restrict the "best configuration" claim to mechanisms that leave the model exposed to pressure during training, where it holds: paired t-tests show L2+pre-commitment(q=0.5) beats the L2 penalty alone (mean diff −0.37pp, t=−4.37, p=0.0018) and pre-commitment alone (mean diff −1.59pp, t=−8.52, p<0.0001) on the same 10 seeds. Pre-commitment alone is significantly *worse* than the L2 penalty alone (mean diff +1.23pp, t=10.1, p<0.0001) — the two are not close substitutes, unlike the q=1 degenerate case might suggest.

## 4. Discussion and limitations

This simulation is deliberately toy-scale: three scalar parameters, a linear-logistic policy, hand-specified annotator reward functions — an existence-proof and mechanism-isolation tool, not a quantitative prediction about real RLHF sycophancy rates. New in this revision, two of the environment's own hand-set constants (ambiguous-claim noise, rubric-reweighting scale) are now swept and shown not to be load-bearing for the qualitative story, though the verifiable/ambiguous agreement-weight ratio (0.2 vs 1.0) itself, which we did not sweep, still directly encodes the mechanism we're studying and should be read as a modeling assumption rather than an emergent finding. The rubric-reweighting mitigation only rescales existing reward terms rather than modeling OpenAI's richer held-out rubric evaluation. The architectural limitation flagged previously stands: because `g` is a single interpretable scalar, the L2 penalty and the q=1 degeneracy we describe here are both artifacts of that design choice; whether the same qualitative pattern — and a genuine (non-degenerate) mitigation ranking — survives a higher-capacity neural policy without a single named "pressure" parameter remains for future work. We also newly flag that our pre-commitment sweep contains a boundary degeneracy (q=1) that is easy to mis-read as "the strongest mitigation" without checking the mechanism, which we caught only by directly re-examining our own sweep output against our prose — a caution for any table that plots hyperparameter extremes on the same axis as a different regularizer's curve.

## 5. Conclusion

We built a minimal, reproducible simulation of how preference-based training produces sycophancy as a structural consequence of rater verification limits, and used it to run controlled, multi-seed, paired-significance-tested ablations of five mitigations. Across 10 seeds, a hyperparameter sweep, and two new sweeps over the environment's own generative constants, an explicit penalty on the model's learned sensitivity to user pressure — and a reward-rebalancing analog of OpenAI's reported production fix, statistically indistinguishable from it despite acting through a different mechanism (p=0.13) — are the most effective non-degenerate mitigations we test, significantly better in combination than alone (p<0.002). Robust reward aggregation helps only against a minority of extreme raters, not systemic bias. We also correct our own earlier claim about full pre-commitment, which posts a lower raw number only because it mechanically prevents the model from ever learning to condition on user stance — illustrating, within the paper itself, the same lesson it argues for: a metric that looks good can conceal what's actually driving it, and it's worth checking.
