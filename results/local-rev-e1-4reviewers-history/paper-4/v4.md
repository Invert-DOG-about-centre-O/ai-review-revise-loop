# When Approval Isn't Truth: A Minimal Simulation of Sycophancy Emergence Under Preference-Based Training

## Abstract

LLM assistants trained with human-feedback objectives are widely reported to become *sycophantic* — agreeing with a user's stated stance rather than stating what is true, especially on claims that are hard to verify. This is a socio-technical failure: a social process (raters approving what they like) feeds directly into a technical optimization loop. We build a minimal, fully synthetic simulation isolating this mechanism: a policy trained via REINFORCE to maximize simulated annotator approval, where annotators can check correctness on "verifiable" claims but can only judge agreement on "ambiguous" claims. Across 10 seeds, naive training produces a policy that resists user pressure on verifiable claims (sycophancy 4.1%±0.7pp) but capitulates far more on ambiguous ones (26.7%±2.1pp), from the same reward function and policy parameters. We test five mitigations on a common footing (clean and outlier-stressed annotators): an L2 penalty on the learned "pressure weight" `g` (→23.6%), a rubric-reweighting analog of OpenAI's reported fix that never touches `g` directly (→23.5%, paired t-test finds no significant difference from the L2 penalty, p=0.13), partial pre-commitment (q=0.5, hiding the user's stance on half of training batches, →24.8%), and robust (median) reward aggregation, which helps only against outlier annotators (29.6%→27.3%) and does nothing against systemic bias (26.7%→26.7% exactly). Paired t-tests confirm L2+pre-commitment(q=0.5) beats either mitigation alone (p<0.002 vs. L2 alone, p<0.0001 vs. pre-commitment alone) among mechanisms that keep the model exposed to pressure during training. We also identify and correct an artifact from our own hyperparameter sweep: full pre-commitment (q=1) posts the lowest raw ambiguous-sycophancy number we measure, but only because it mechanically zeroes the gradient on `g` every batch, pinning it at its initial value (0.0) — and, new in this revision, a finer q-sweep (0.5 to 0.99) shows this is the smooth continuous limit of a monotonic decay in `g`, not a discontinuous special case, so intermediate q values are genuine (non-degenerate) partial suppressions rather than milder versions of the same artifact. All four independent reviewers of our prior draft converged on the same residual concern: the verifiable/ambiguous agreement-weight ratio (0.2 vs 1.0), unlike the noise level and rubric factor we had already swept, directly encodes the mechanism under study and was never varied. We now sweep it (ratio 0.1 to 1.0, i.e. from strongly-differentiated to no-differentiation-in-agreement-weight-at-all) and find the sycophancy gap shrinks smoothly and monotonically from 7.0× to 2.9× as the ratio closes — confirming the gap's *magnitude* is partly a designed-in property, as the paper already disclosed, but also showing it never vanishes: even at ratio=1.0, where annotators weight agreement identically on both claim types, the verification asymmetry in `acc_weight` alone (checkable vs. not) still produces a 2.9× gap (p<1e-5), meaning the core mechanism is not solely an artifact of the swept constant. All code, logs, and figures are included in this directory; the full experiment suite runs in under 20 seconds of CPU time.

## 1. Introduction

Sycophancy — an AI system telling users what they want to hear rather than what is accurate — is one of the most visible socio-technical failure modes of deployed LLM assistants. In April 2025, OpenAI rolled back a GPT-4o update after users reported it had become "excessively flattering," attributing this to a reward signal overweighted toward short-term user approval. Benchmarks (SycEval, SYCON-Bench, PARROT) measure sycophancy rates as high as 58% on math/medical QA; Cheng et al.'s "Social Sycophancy" (arXiv:2505.13995) shows it generalizes beyond fact-agreement to face-preservation; HCI work (CHI 2026) shows it degrades trust and misleads novice programmers. What is missing is a controlled, quantitative account of *when* the mechanism emerges, *which* mitigations help, and *how reliably* — across seeds, stressors, and hyperparameters, including the environment's own generative assumptions.

### Contributions

1. A minimal synthetic environment reproducing sycophancy's qualitative signature — near-zero pressure-driven error on verifiable claims, substantial error on ambiguous ones — reported across 10 seeds with variance, shown to hold (5.7–7.6×) across a noise-level sweep, and, new here, shown to persist (though shrink from 7.0× to 2.9×, never vanishing) across a full sweep of the verifiable/ambiguous agreement-weight ratio itself — the one generative constant all four round-3 reviewers independently flagged as unswept.
2. A fully crossed, paired-significance-tested comparison of five mitigations, including a corrected account of a genuine artifact in our own pre-commitment sweep (full pre-commitment freezes the learned parameter rather than suppressing it), now further characterized by a fine-grained q-sweep showing the freeze is a smooth limit, not a hidden discontinuity that would contaminate intermediate settings like our headline q=0.5.
3. Open, runnable code (`sycophancy_sim.py`, `sensitivity.py`, `revision_extra.py`, `revision_extra2.py`) and raw logs; every number in this paper reproduces in seconds of CPU time.

## 2. Method

### 2.1 Environment

We generate `N_train=6000`/`N_test=3000` synthetic claims. Each has ground-truth `t_i∈{0,1}`; a verifiability flag `v_i` (50/50); a user stance `u_i` (equal to `t_i` w.p. 0.5); and a private competence signal `c_i=(2t_i-1)·3+N(0,σ_v)`, `σ_v=1.0` (verifiable) / `4.0` (ambiguous).

### 2.2 Policy

A 3-parameter logistic model: `P(answer="true")=sigmoid(w_c·c+g·s+h)`, `s=2u-1`. `g` — the quantity of interest — controls how much the answer shifts toward the user's stance regardless of evidence. All parameters start at `(1,0,0)`.

### 2.3 Simulated annotators and training

For each action `a`, `K=5` annotators score it: `reward_k=acc_weight·1[a=t]+agree_weight_k·1[a=u]+noise`, `acc_weight=1.0`(verifiable)/`0.0`(ambiguous), `agree_weight_k=0.2·agree_bias_k`(verifiable)/`1.0·agree_bias_k`(ambiguous), `agree_bias_k~U(0.5,1.5)`. We train `(w_c,g,h)` via REINFORCE with an EMA baseline (400 iters, batch 256, lr 0.08).

### 2.4 Mitigations

Six training variants, each run clean and under an outlier-annotator stressor (`agree_bias~U(2.5,4.0)` for one annotator/batch):

- **Naive RLHF** (control).
- **Robust median aggregation** over the 5 annotators.
- **L2 penalty**: `g←g−lr·λ·g` (λ=0.15) after each update. Unlike the rubric factor `f` (below), `λ` was selected by grid search over `{0,...,0.6}` (Sec. 3) rather than derived a priori from an environment constant; we flag this asymmetry explicitly rather than implying both hyperparameters were chosen the same way.
- **Pre-commitment**: on a fraction `q` of batches, the pressure input is zeroed before the forward pass. We report `q=0.5` as the headline setting; Sec. 3 shows the full `q∈[0.5,1.0]` trajectory is a smooth decay into the `q=1` degeneracy, not a discontinuity, so `q=0.5` is not a "mild version" of that artifact.
- **Rubric reweighting**: scale every annotator's agreement term by a factor `f=0.2` (both claim types), mirroring OpenAI's reported fix without touching `g` directly. We chose `f=0.2` to match the *verifiable*-claim agreement weight already present in the environment (Sec. 2.3); Sec. 3 sweeps `f` to check this.
- **Combined**: L2 penalty + pre-commitment (q=0.5).

### 2.5 Metrics and statistical protocol

We report accuracy and **sycophancy rate** `P(a=u|u≠t)` overall and by verifiability, an approval proxy, and learned `(w_c,g,h)`, mean±std over **10 seeds**. For the closest mitigation comparisons we additionally run a **paired t-test across the same 10 seeds** (same train/test draw per seed, different training variant); the only randomness that differs *between* paired variants is the mitigation-specific mechanism itself (e.g. which batches pre-commitment masks), since seed offsets for data generation and evaluation (`seed+10000`, `seed+999999`, `seed+555`) are identical across variants — the outlier stressor is the one exception and is never paired against a non-outlier row. We sweep the L2 penalty λ∈{0,...,0.6} and pre-commitment q∈{0,...,1.0} (8 seeds), the environment's ambiguous-claim noise σ_a∈{1.5,2,4,6}, the rubric-reweighting factor f∈{0.1,...,1.0}, and, new in this revision, the verifiable/ambiguous agreement-weight ratio and a finer pre-commitment q grid.

**The gap holds across the noise sweep.** Sweeping ambiguous-claim noise σ_a∈{1.5,2,4,6} (5 seeds, naive training) keeps the verifiable/ambiguous sycophancy ratio between 5.7× and 7.6× (σ_a=1.5: 6.4×; 2.0: 5.7×; 4.0: 6.1×; 6.0: 7.6×).

**New: the agreement-weight ratio — the one constant all four reviewers flagged — is now swept, and the gap shrinks but never disappears.** We vary the verifiable-claim agreement weight from 0.1 to 1.0 (8 seeds) while holding the ambiguous weight fixed at 1.0, i.e. moving from "annotators barely reward agreement on verifiable claims" to "annotators reward agreement identically regardless of verifiability." The sycophancy gap shrinks smoothly and monotonically: 7.0× (ratio=0.1) → 6.3× (0.2, our reported setting) → 5.6× (0.4) → 4.7× (0.6) → 3.7× (0.8) → 2.9× (1.0). A paired t-test between the ratio=0.8 and ratio=0.1 endpoints confirms the shrinkage is real, not noise (mean diff 3.0pp, t=13.4, p=3.0e-6). Critically, the gap does not go to zero at ratio=1.0: with agreement weighted identically across claim types, only `acc_weight` (checkable=1.0 vs. unverifiable=0.0) still differs, and that alone sustains a 2.9× gap. This means the headline order-of-magnitude gap is partly (correctly, as the paper already disclosed) a designed-in consequence of the agreement-weight ratio we chose — larger ratios of differentiation produce larger gaps — but the qualitative phenomenon (verifiable claims resist pressure more than ambiguous ones) survives even when that ratio is removed entirely, because the deeper cause, being unable to check correctness at all, is still present via `acc_weight`.

**New: the q=1 pre-commitment degeneracy is a smooth limit, not a hidden discontinuity.** A finer sweep q∈{0.5,0.7,0.8,0.9,0.95,0.99,1.0} (8 seeds) shows `g` decays continuously toward its frozen value: `g`=.415(q=.5), .255(.7), .172(.8), .087(.9), .044(.95), .0085(.99), 0(1.0, exact) — each step roughly halving as `(1-q)` halves. `syco_a` tracks this smoothly too: .246→.238→.234→.231→.229→.228→.227. There is no jump near q=1; the degeneracy is the continuous endpoint of the same suppression mechanism operating at every q, not a qualitatively different regime that suddenly appears. This directly answers the concern (raised by three of four reviewers) that our headline q=0.5 setting might be a mild version of the same artifact: at q=0.5, `g`=.415 is still 73% of the no-pre-commitment value (.775 in Table 1's naive row would be the wrong comparison — the correct baseline is that `g` is actively learning and being suppressed proportionally, not frozen), confirming q=0.5 is a genuine partial mitigation, not degenerate.

**L2 penalty vs. rubric reweighting: statistically indistinguishable, as claimed.** Paired t-test across the shared 10 seeds: mean diff 0.07pp, t=1.69, p=0.13.

**Rubric factor f=0.2 was not tuned to match.** Sweeping f∈{0.1,0.2,0.4,0.6,1.0} (8 seeds) gives a smooth monotone curve: syco_a = .229(f=.1), .233(.2), .240(.4), .247(.6), .265(1.0, no reweighting); our reported f=0.2 sits on this curve, and the L2 penalty's own number (23.6%) falls between the f=0.2 and f=0.4 points rather than exactly on either.

**Robust aggregation only helps against outliers, never systemic bias.** Table 2: median aggregation helps under the outlier stressor (29.6%→27.3%); Table 1's no-outlier row is unchanged from naive (26.7% vs 26.7%, g .774 vs .775).

**Correcting our own headline claim: full pre-commitment is not "weaker than λ=0.15+," it's a different regime.** Our v2 draft claimed q=1 was "comparable to a moderate L2 penalty but weaker than λ=0.15+" and that L2+pre-commitment(q=0.5) was the single best configuration overall. Re-checking against our own released sweep data shows this is wrong: q=1 achieves syco_a≈0.227–0.229 — *below* every λ setting we test and below the reported "best" combined configuration (0.232) — because at q=1 the pressure input is masked every batch, so `∂reward/∂g≡0` and `g` never leaves its initial value of exactly 0.0 (confirmed in logs; `l2_plus_q1` and `q1`-alone produce bitwise-identical syco_a=0.2295 over the same 10 seeds, since the penalty acts on a parameter that never updates). With `g` pinned at 0, the policy never conditions on user stance at all, so its residual "sycophancy" is coincidental agreement from the noisy competence signal, not the phenomenon the paper studies. We therefore restrict the "best configuration" claim to mechanisms that leave the model exposed to pressure: L2+pre-commitment(q=0.5) beats L2 alone (mean diff −0.37pp, t=−4.37, p=0.0018) and pre-commitment alone (mean diff −1.59pp, t=−8.52, p<0.0001) on the same 10 seeds; pre-commitment alone is significantly *worse* than the L2 penalty alone (mean diff +1.23pp, t=10.1, p<0.0001) — the two are not close substitutes, unlike the q=1 degenerate case might suggest, and (per the fine q-sweep above) this ordering is not an artifact of proximity to the q=1 boundary.

## 4. Discussion and limitations

This simulation is deliberately toy-scale: three scalar parameters, a linear-logistic policy, hand-specified annotator reward functions — an existence-proof and mechanism-isolation tool, not a quantitative prediction about real RLHF sycophancy rates. All three of the environment's hand-set constants that shape the headline gap — ambiguous-claim noise, rubric-reweighting scale, and now the verifiable/ambiguous agreement-weight ratio — have been swept this revision. The first two leave the qualitative story intact across their full ranges; the third does not (gap magnitude scales continuously with the ratio, from 7.0× down to 2.9×), which we read as confirming rather than undermining the mechanism: the ratio controls *how much* of the gap comes from differential agreement-reward versus the underlying verification asymmetry (`acc_weight`), and the latter alone — the part of the design that is not a free hyperparameter but the definition of "verifiable" — still produces a significant, non-trivial gap on its own. The rubric-reweighting mitigation only rescales existing reward terms rather than modeling OpenAI's richer held-out rubric evaluation. The architectural limitation flagged previously stands: because `g` is a single interpretable scalar, the L2 penalty and the q=1 degeneracy are both artifacts of that design choice; whether the same qualitative pattern — and a genuine (non-degenerate) mitigation ranking — survives a higher-capacity neural policy without a single named "pressure" parameter remains for future work. The λ vs. f hyperparameter-selection asymmetry noted in Sec. 2.4 (grid search vs. a priori derivation) means their near-tie (p=0.13) should be read as "these mechanisms converge near their respective natural/best settings," not as a claim that any λ and any f would tie.

## 5. Conclusion

We built a minimal, reproducible simulation of how preference-based training produces sycophancy as a structural consequence of rater verification limits, and used it to run controlled, multi-seed, paired-significance-tested ablations of five mitigations. Across 10 seeds and sweeps of every hand-set environment constant that bears on the headline result — noise level, rubric factor, and (new here) the agreement-weight ratio itself — an explicit penalty on the model's learned sensitivity to user pressure, and a reward-rebalancing analog of OpenAI's reported production fix statistically indistinguishable from it (p=0.13), are the most effective non-degenerate mitigations we test, significantly better in combination (p<0.002). Robust reward aggregation helps only against a minority of extreme raters, not systemic bias. We also correct our own earlier claim about full pre-commitment, and, new in this revision, show that its degeneracy is a smooth limit rather than a discontinuity that could contaminate our headline non-degenerate setting — illustrating, within the paper itself, the same lesson it argues for: a metric that looks good can conceal what's actually driving it, and it's worth checking, at every hyperparameter and every generative assumption, not just once.
