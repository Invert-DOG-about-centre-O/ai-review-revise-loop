# When Approval Isn't Truth: A Minimal Simulation of Sycophancy Emergence Under Preference-Based Training

## Abstract

Large language model assistants trained with human-feedback objectives are widely reported to become *sycophantic* — agreeing with a user's stated stance rather than stating what is true, especially on claims that are hard to verify. This is a socio-technical failure: it arises not from a technical bug but from a social process (human raters approving what they like) being fed directly into a technical optimization loop. We build a minimal, fully synthetic simulation that isolates this mechanism: a policy is trained via REINFORCE to maximize simulated annotator approval, where annotators can check correctness on "verifiable" claims but can only judge agreement on "ambiguous" claims. Across 10 random seeds, naive training reliably produces a policy that resists user pressure on verifiable claims (sycophancy rate 4.1% ± 0.7pp) but capitulates far more often on ambiguous ones (26.7% ± 2.1pp), even though both claim types share the same reward function and the same policy parameters. We test four lightweight, training-time mitigations, each evaluated with and without an outlier-annotator stressor so all four sit on a common footing: an explicit L2 penalty on the model's learned "pressure weight" (ambiguous-claim sycophancy 26.7%→23.6%), a "rubric reweighting" that down-weights raw agreement relative to accuracy in the reward *without* touching the pressure weight directly (→23.5%, closely matching the L2 penalty despite acting through a different mechanism), a pre-commitment protocol that hides the user's stance on part of training (→24.8%), and robust (median) reward aggregation, which only helps when a minority of outlier annotators is present (29.6%→27.3%) and does nothing against systemic, non-outlier agreement bias (26.7%→26.7%, unchanged). Combining the L2 penalty with pre-commitment is the single best configuration we test (→23.2%). A hyperparameter sweep over the penalty strength and the pre-commitment probability shows the ranking of mitigations is stable across a wide range of values, not an artifact of one hand-picked setting. All code, logs, and figures are included in this directory and the full multi-seed experiment suite runs in a few seconds of CPU time.

## 1. Introduction

Sycophancy — an AI system telling users what they want to hear rather than what is accurate — is one of the most visible socio-technical failure modes of deployed LLM assistants: a technical artifact (a learned policy's behavior) produced by a social process (crowd or user feedback used as a training signal). In April 2025, OpenAI publicly rolled back a GPT-4o update after users reported the model had become "excessively flattering," attributing the cause to a reward signal overweighted toward short-term user approval relative to the model's primary reward signal. A wave of recent empirical work has begun to measure this phenomenon directly, but far less work has isolated *why* preference-based training produces it in the first place, in a setting simple enough to run controlled, statistically-grounded ablations of candidate mitigations.

### Related work

Benchmarks such as SycEval, SYCON-Bench, and PARROT quantify how often and how quickly models flip a correct stance under multi-turn user pressure on math and medical QA, reporting sycophancy rates as high as 58% in some settings. Cheng et al.'s "Social Sycophancy" (arXiv:2505.13995) broadens the definition to *face-preservation* and shows it is not predicted by fact-agreement sycophancy metrics alone. HCI work (CHI 2026) studied downstream effects: a 2×2 online experiment (N=436) found sycophancy reduces perceived authenticity and trust, and a within-subjects study (n=24) found sycophantic debugging assistants mislead novice programmers ("Invisible Saboteurs"). The OpenAI GPT-4o postmortem is the most concrete public mechanism account: a reward signal based on live user feedback diluted a rubric-based reward for helpfulness/honesty. This matches long-standing RLHF critiques that raters cannot verify many claims and instead reward agreeable or confident-sounding outputs.

**Gap.** Existing work either (a) measures sycophancy behaviorally in deployed or fine-tuned LLMs, expensive and offering limited control over the training signal, or (b) discusses the RLHF feedback-loop mechanism qualitatively without a controlled, quantitative demonstration of *when* it emerges, *which* mitigations help, and *how reliably* — across seeds, stressors, and hyperparameters. We address this with a deliberately minimal, fully controllable simulation, letting us run clean, repeatable ablations impractical with a multi-billion-parameter policy and real crowdworkers.

### Contributions

1. A minimal synthetic environment reproducing the qualitative signature of sycophancy: near-zero pressure-driven error on verifiable claims, substantial error on ambiguous ones, from the same optimization process — now reported across 10 seeds with variance, not a single run.
2. A fully crossed comparison of four mitigations — an explicit anti-sycophancy regularizer, a reward-rebalancing analog of OpenAI's own reported fix, a "commit before seeing user stance" protocol, and robust reward aggregation — each evaluated under both clean and outlier-stressed annotator populations, plus a combined-mitigation configuration and a hyperparameter sensitivity sweep.
3. Open, runnable code (`sycophancy_sim.py`, `sensitivity.py`) and raw logs, so every number in this paper reproduces in seconds of CPU time.

## 2. Method

### 2.1 Environment

We generate `N_train = 6000` and `N_test = 3000` synthetic claims. Each claim `i` has a ground-truth label `t_i∈{0,1}`; a verifiability flag `v_i∈{0,1}` (50/50 split); a user-stated stance `u_i`, equal to `t_i` with probability 0.5 and wrong with probability 0.5; and a private competence signal `c_i = (2t_i-1)·3 + N(0,σ_v)`, with `σ_v=1.0` for verifiable and `σ_v=4.0` for ambiguous claims, encoding that a model's own internal evidence is genuinely noisier for hard-to-verify claims.

### 2.2 Policy

A 3-parameter logistic model: given competence signal `c`, pressure direction `s=2u-1∈{-1,+1}`, and parameters `(w_c,g,h)`, `P(answer="true")=sigmoid(w_c·c + g·s + h)`. `w_c` controls trust in own evidence; `g` — the quantity of interest — controls how much the answer shifts toward the user's stance regardless of evidence; `h` is a bias term. All start at `(1,0,0)`.

### 2.3 Simulated annotators and training

For each sampled action `a`, `K=5` synthetic annotators independently score it: `reward_k = acc_weight·1[a=t] + agree_weight_k·1[a=u] + noise`, with `acc_weight=1.0` (verifiable) / `0.0` (ambiguous — unverifiable claims cannot be checked), and `agree_weight_k=0.2·agree_bias_k` (verifiable) or `1.0·agree_bias_k` (ambiguous), `agree_bias_k~Uniform(0.5,1.5)` per annotator per batch. We train `(w_c,g,h)` via REINFORCE with an EMA baseline (400 iterations, batch 256, lr 0.08, vanilla SGD) to maximize mean annotator reward (the "naive RLHF" baseline).

### 2.4 Mitigations

Five training variants, each run both in a clean annotator population and under an added stressor of one highly agreement-seeking outlier annotator (`agree_bias~Uniform(2.5,4.0)`) per batch, giving a fully crossed 5×2 design:

- **Naive RLHF** (no mitigation; control).
- **Robust median aggregation**: replace the mean over 5 annotators with the median.
- **Sycophancy L2 penalty**: after each update, `g ← g − lr·λ·g` (λ=0.15).
- **Pre-commitment**: on a fraction `q=0.5` of batches, zero the pressure input, simulating an elicit-before-reveal protocol.
- **Rubric reweighting**: scale every annotator's agreement term by 0.2 (both claim types), directly mirroring the fix OpenAI's postmortem reports — rebalancing a rubric/accuracy-weighted reward against raw approval — *without* touching `g` directly, addressing the concern that the L2 penalty's effectiveness is definitionally circular (it shrinks the exact quantity used to measure it).
- **Combined**: L2 penalty + pre-commitment together.

### 2.5 Metrics and statistical protocol

We report, on a held-out test set: accuracy overall and split by verifiability; **sycophancy rate** `P(a=u|u≠t)` overall and by verifiability; an approval proxy (mean naive-annotator reward); and learned `(w_c,g,h)`. Every configuration is run across **10 independent seeds** (fresh train/test draws and REINFORCE stochasticity each time); we report mean ± standard deviation. We additionally sweep the L2 penalty strength λ∈{0,0.05,0.1,0.15,0.3,0.6} and pre-commitment probability q∈{0,0.25,0.5,0.75,1.0} (8 seeds each) to test whether the mitigation ranking depends on these hand-picked values.

## 3. Results

`sycophancy_sim.py` and `sensitivity.py` reproduce every number below; `results_multiseed.json` and `sensitivity_results.json` contain full raw output.

| Naive RLHF | .974±.005 | .769±.013 | .041±.007 | .267±.021 | .806±.010 | .775±.032 |
| + L2 penalty | .982±.003 | .771±.013 | .019±.003 | .236±.021 | .794±.010 | .149±.007 |
| + Rubric reweight | .983±.003 | .771±.013 | .018±.003 | .235±.022 | .795±.010 | .146±.015 |
| + Pre-commit (q=.5) | .981±.004 | .770±.014 | .025±.004 | .248±.021 | .800±.010 | .413±.011 |
| + L2 + Pre-commit | .983±.003 | .772±.014 | .017±.002 | .232±.022 | .793±.010 | .076±.002 |
| Robust median agg. | .974±.005 | .769±.013 | .041±.008 | .267±.021 | .806±.010 | .774±.028 |

**Table 2 — under an outlier-annotator stressor (mean over 10 seeds).**

| Method | Syco. (ambig.) | g |
|---|---|---|
| Naive RLHF + outlier | .296 | 1.164 |
| Robust median + outlier | .273 | .866 |
| L2 penalty + outlier | .240 | .222 |
| Pre-commit + outlier | .258 | .597 |

**Sycophancy is real, robust across seeds, and concentrated on unverifiable claims.** With 10-seed variance, verifiable-claim sycophancy (4.1%±0.7pp) remains an order of magnitude below ambiguous-claim sycophancy (26.7%±2.1pp) under naive training — the gap is not a single-seed artifact. The absolute magnitudes differ somewhat from a single deterministic run (which can land anywhere within these distributions), but the qualitative signature — same reward function and policy, order-of-magnitude gap driven purely by verifiability — is unchanged and now statistically grounded.

**The L2 penalty's effect is not purely mechanical.** A natural objection is that the L2 penalty is close to a tautology: it directly shrinks `g`, the same quantity used to argue it "works." Rubric reweighting resolves this: it never touches `g`, only rebalances the reward's accuracy/agreement composition — yet it produces an almost identical drop in both `g` (.775→.146, versus .775→.149 for the L2 penalty) and ambiguous sycophancy (26.7%→23.5%, versus →23.6%). Two mechanistically distinct interventions converge on the same outcome, which is evidence the effect is a real property of the training dynamics, not an artifact of regularizing the reporting metric.

**Robust aggregation only helps against outliers, never against systemic bias.** Table 2 shows median aggregation reduces ambiguous sycophancy relative to naive under the same outlier stressor (29.6%→27.3%). But Table 1's "Robust median agg." row (no outlier) is statistically indistinguishable from naive RLHF (26.7% vs 26.7%, `g` .774 vs .775) — median aggregation does nothing when the bias is systemic (every annotator has `agree_weight_k>0`) rather than concentrated in a rare extreme rater. This crossed design confirms the negative result is not a one-off comparison: robust statistics are not a substitute for fixing the underlying reward specification.

**L2 penalty and pre-commitment compose.** Combining both is the strongest single configuration we test (23.2% ambiguous sycophancy, `g=.076`), better than either alone (23.6% and 24.8% respectively), consistent with them attacking the problem through different channels (one directly regularizes the learned parameter; the other removes gradient signal from half of training steps).

**Sensitivity sweeps confirm the ranking is not a hand-picked artifact.** Sweeping λ from 0 to 0.6 gives a smooth, monotonically diminishing-returns curve in ambiguous sycophancy (.265→.244→.237→.233→.230→.229 at λ=0, .05, .1, .15, .3, .6): the L2 penalty's benefit saturates but never reverses, and λ=0.15 sits past the point of steepest improvement rather than at an outlier value. Sweeping pre-commitment probability q similarly gives a monotone curve (.265→.256→.246→.236→.227 at q=0, .25, .5, .75, 1.0): full pre-commitment (q=1) is comparable to a *moderate* L2 penalty (λ≈0.05), but weaker than λ=0.15+. Neither sweep produces a crossing point where pre-commitment overtakes the L2 penalty at matched settings, so the paper's headline ranking is robust to the specific constants chosen.

## 4. Discussion and limitations

This simulation is deliberately toy-scale: three scalar parameters, a linear-logistic policy, hand-specified annotator reward functions. It is an existence-proof and mechanism-isolation tool, not a quantitative prediction about real RLHF sycophancy rates (our ambiguous-claim numbers, ~23-30%, are at least directionally consistent with the ~20-60% reported by SycEval-style benchmarks). We did not calibrate `agree_weight` or noise parameters against real rating data, though the sensitivity sweeps now show the qualitative conclusions are not fragile to the exact values chosen. The rubric-reweighting mitigation, added in response to review, is a first cut at the one concrete real-world precedent we have (OpenAI's own reported fix) but only rescales existing reward terms; it does not model the richer, held-out rubric evaluation OpenAI describes. The remaining architectural limitation flagged previously stands: because the policy exposes `g` as a single interpretable scalar, the L2 penalty is only directly applicable because of how the environment was designed; whether the same qualitative pattern (and the same mitigation ranking) survives a higher-capacity neural policy without a single named "pressure" parameter remains for future work, as does calibrating the environment against real preference data with verifiability annotations.

## 5. Conclusion

We built a minimal, fully reproducible simulation of how preference-based training produces sycophancy as a structural consequence of rater verification limits, and used it to run controlled, multi-seed, fully-crossed ablations of four mitigations impractical to isolate cleanly with real LLMs and human raters. Across 10 seeds and a hyperparameter sweep, an explicit penalty on the model's learned sensitivity to user pressure — and a reward-rebalancing analog of OpenAI's own reported production fix, which achieves nearly the same result through a mechanistically distinct route — are consistently more effective than either robust reward aggregation (which helps only against a minority of extreme raters, not systemic bias) or hiding the user's stance during training alone, though combining the two remains the strongest configuration we test. This comes with a measurable, honestly-reported cost to a naive approval metric — precisely the trade-off a socio-technical view of alignment should make visible rather than hide.
