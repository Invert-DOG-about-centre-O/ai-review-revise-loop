# Cheap Signals Can Beat Expensive Ones at Discrimination, But Not at Calibration: A Controlled Comparison of Uncertainty Proxies for LLM Error Detection

## Abstract

Detecting when a language model's answer is wrong is a central open problem in probabilistic methods for LLMs. Sampling-based methods such as semantic entropy and self-consistency are widely reported as strong hallucination detectors, but they cost K forward passes per query, motivating recent work on cheap single-pass proxies. We run a fully controlled, offline experiment that isolates the cost/accuracy trade-off: we train a small character-level Transformer from scratch on a deterministic two-digit addition task (so ground truth is unambiguous and ablation is exact) and compare four uncertainty signals — first-token entropy, greedy sequence log-probability (both single forward pass), and self-consistency frequency / semantic entropy over K=8 samples — as predictors of greedy-answer correctness. We find that the free single-pass log-probability signal achieves *higher* AUROC (0.640) for detecting wrong answers than either 8-sample semantic entropy (0.614) or self-consistency (0.603), and that the sampling-based advantage over log-probability never materializes at any tested budget K∈{1,...,8}. However, self-consistency frequency is dramatically better *calibrated* as a probability estimate (ECE=0.024) than log-probability-derived confidence (ECE=0.290, roughly 12× worse), despite being the weaker discriminator. This dissociation between discrimination and calibration — measured on a controlled, reproducible testbed — suggests that the choice between cheap and expensive uncertainty signals should depend on whether the downstream use case needs ranking (discrimination) or well-calibrated probabilities, not on a single aggregate "hallucination detection" metric. Code, logs, and raw results are included alongside this paper.

## 1. Introduction

Large language models routinely produce fluent but incorrect statements ("hallucinations"), and a large recent literature studies how to detect these failures using signals derived from the model's own probabilistic behavior. Two families dominate:

- **Single-pass signals**: the token-level probability or entropy of the model's own greedy/generated output, obtainable from one forward pass at essentially zero marginal cost.
- **Sampling-based signals**: draw K completions (temperature > 0) and measure the spread of the resulting distribution, either by exact/semantic clustering entropy (*semantic entropy*, Kuhn et al. 2023) or by simple agreement rate (*self-consistency*, Wang et al. 2022). These require K forward-pass-equivalents per query.

Sampling-based methods, especially semantic entropy, are frequently reported as more reliable than single-pass baselines because they capture uncertainty over *meaning* rather than surface tokens (Kuhn et al., 2023; Farquhar et al., 2024). But this reliability comes at a real inference-cost multiple, which has motivated follow-up work distilling semantic entropy into cheap probes trained on hidden states ("Semantic Entropy Probes", Kossen et al., 2024) and other single-pass approximations. A 2025 KDD survey (Liu et al., 2025) organizes this landscape into input/reasoning/parameter/prediction uncertainty and explicitly flags "computational constraints" as a key open challenge for deploying sampling-based UQ. A related 2025 paper (arXiv:2504.13677) shows that UQ evaluations can be confounded by spurious factors like response length, underscoring that head-to-head comparisons need tightly controlled testbeds. Separately, verbalized-confidence methods ask the model to self-report a numeric confidence and are notoriously miscalibrated without further correction (Xiong et al., 2024).

What is less commonly reported, because it requires a controlled ground-truth setting, is a direct, apples-to-apples comparison of what each signal family is actually good *for*: ranking answers by correctness (discrimination, measured by AUROC) versus producing probabilities that match empirical accuracy (calibration, measured by ECE/Brier). Real LLM benchmarks confound this comparison with unknown ground truth (requiring an auxiliary judge), topic-dependent difficulty, and models whose training data is opaque. We instead use a synthetic, deterministic task with a small from-scratch model, which lets us (a) know ground truth exactly, (b) control model capacity and training so that the error rate is realistic (not near 0% or 100%), and (c) sweep the sampling budget K exactly via resampling from a fixed pool of draws.

**Contributions.** (1) We build a minimal, fully offline, CPU-only testbed for comparing LLM uncertainty signals with exact ground truth. (2) We show that on this testbed, a free single-pass signal (mean log-probability of the greedy answer) discriminates correct from incorrect answers *better* than 8-sample semantic entropy or self-consistency, and that this gap persists as K grows from 1 to 8. (3) We show the reverse holds for calibration: self-consistency frequency is nearly 12x better calibrated (ECE) than a log-probability-derived confidence. (4) We argue this discrimination/calibration dissociation is a useful lens for choosing among UQ methods in practice, and release all code and raw outputs for reproducibility.

## 2. Related Work

**Semantic entropy and sampling-based UQ.** Kuhn et al. (2023) introduced semantic entropy, clustering sampled generations by bidirectional entailment before computing Shannon entropy over clusters, and Farquhar et al. (2024, *Nature*) extended this to detect confabulations across open-domain QA. Wang et al. (2022) proposed self-consistency (majority-vote agreement over sampled chain-of-thought traces) primarily as an accuracy-boosting decoding strategy, which has also been repurposed as an uncertainty signal via the agreement rate itself. Both require K ≥ several samples per query.

**Cheap single-pass proxies.** Kossen et al. (2024) train linear probes on hidden states to approximate semantic entropy from a single forward pass ("semantic entropy probes"), reducing the sampling overhead "to almost zero" but requiring labeled training data for the probe. Duan et al. (2023) and related token-level entropy/logprob approaches use the model's own generation probabilities directly, with no extra training or sampling, as our single-pass baselines do.

**Surveys and evaluation critiques.** Liu et al. (2025, KDD) and a companion arXiv survey (2412.05563) taxonomize UQ methods and list computational cost and evaluation confounds as open problems. ArXiv:2504.13677 specifically shows that response-length differences between correct and incorrect answers can spuriously inflate UQ benchmark numbers, a concern our fixed-length numeric-answer task sidesteps by construction (answers are 1–3 digits regardless of correctness). Semantic Energy (arXiv:2508.14496) argues semantic entropy under-uses logit information and proposes an energy-based alternative operating on penultimate-layer logits — consistent with our finding that log-probability-derived signals are competitive with sampling-based clustering.

**Where we differ.** Prior comparisons are typically run on large pretrained models over natural-language QA benchmarks, where ground truth requires an LLM judge or human annotation and results are confounded by many uncontrolled factors. We instead isolate the discrimination-vs-calibration question on a synthetic, deterministic task with a small from-scratch model, trading ecological realism for experimental control and full reproducibility on a CPU laptop budget.

## 3. Method

**Task.** Two-digit addition rendered as characters, e.g. `"41+67="` → `"108"`. Operands are drawn uniformly from [1, 98]; answers range 2–196 and are rendered as their minimal decimal string. This gives exact, unambiguous ground truth and near-uniform answer length regardless of correctness, controlling for the length-confound noted above.

**Model.** A 2-layer, 4-head causal Transformer (d_model=48, ~0.1M parameters) with learned positional embeddings, operating over a 16-symbol character vocabulary (digits, `+`, `=`, space, pad, eos). We train it from scratch with Adam (lr=3e-3) for 600 steps of batch size 64 on freshly sampled addition problems (an implicit infinite-data / few-epoch regime). This is deliberately small and under-trained so that greedy accuracy lands well below ceiling (we obtain 32.8%), giving a realistic mix of correct/incorrect answers to evaluate uncertainty signals against — the interesting regime for hallucination detection.

**Uncertainty signals**, computed per test question (N=400, held out via a separate random seed):
1. **First-token entropy** — Shannon entropy of the softmax distribution over the first answer character, from the single forward pass at the end of the prompt.
2. **Mean log-probability** — length-normalized sum of log-probabilities of the greedy-decoded answer characters (free byproduct of greedy decoding).
3. **Self-consistency confidence** — draw K=8 samples at temperature 1.0, parse each to an integer (or mark as a parse failure), and take the fraction agreeing with the modal (most frequent) parsed value.
4. **Semantic entropy** — Shannon entropy of the empirical distribution over distinct parsed integer answers among the same K=8 samples. Because answers are numeric, exact-value clustering *is* semantic clustering (two strings mean the same thing iff they denote the same integer), so no separate entailment model is needed — this removes a further confound (entailment-model quality) from the comparison.

**Evaluation.** (a) AUROC of each signal for separating wrong vs. correct greedy answers (rank-based, tie-corrected Mann–Whitney U). (b) Calibration of two probability-like signals — `exp(mean_logp)` and self-consistency frequency — via Expected Calibration Error (10 equal-width bins) and Brier score against the binary correctness label. (c) A cost-accuracy sweep: for K ∈ {1,2,3,4,6,8}, we subsample K of the 8 stored samples (50 bootstrap draws per K) and recompute semantic entropy / self-consistency AUROC, tracing out how detection quality scales with sampling budget.

## 4. Results

Model training took 19.5s; the full 400-question evaluation (greedy decode + first-token entropy + 8 stochastic samples per question) took 19.6s; total wall-clock for the experiment was 39.1s on a 12-core CPU laptop, well within budget. Greedy accuracy was 32.8% (131/400 correct), giving 269 "wrong" and 131 "correct" examples for detection.

**Discrimination (AUROC for flagging wrong answers):**

| Signal | Cost | AUROC |
|---|---|---|
| First-token entropy | 1 pass | 0.524 |
| **Mean log-probability (neg.)** | **1 pass** | **0.640** |
| Semantic entropy | K=8 samples | 0.614 |
| Self-consistency (neg.) | K=8 samples | 0.603 |

The free log-probability signal is the single best detector, beating both 8-sample sampling-based methods. First-token entropy alone (using only the first character's distribution) is close to chance (0.524), confirming that most of the discriminative signal lives in the full generated sequence's probability, not in the initial branch point.

**Cost-accuracy sweep.** As K grows from 1 to 8, semantic-entropy AUROC rises monotonically from 0.500 (K=1, uninformative — a single sample cannot show disagreement) to 0.615, and self-consistency from 0.500 to 0.603, tracking each other closely at every K. Neither ever crosses the log-probability baseline (0.640) at any tested budget (see `results.png`, left panel). This indicates the gap is not a "not enough samples yet" artifact within the range we could afford to test.

**Calibration.** Despite its weaker discrimination, self-consistency frequency is a far better-calibrated confidence estimate: ECE=0.024 vs. 0.290 for `exp(mean_logp)` (Brier 0.213 vs. 0.293). The reliability tables (in `analysis_log.txt`) show why: `exp(mean_logp)` is systematically overconfident (e.g., in the [0.6,0.7) confidence bin, empirical accuracy is only 0.389; in [0.7,0.8), only 0.432), a well-known property of raw sequence probabilities from an under-trained model, whereas self-consistency frequency tracks empirical accuracy almost exactly in every bin (e.g., confidence 0.375 vs. accuracy 0.372).

**Summary of the dissociation.** The best *ranker* of correct vs. incorrect answers here is free; the best-*calibrated probability* costs 8x more compute. Neither signal dominates the other on both axes.

## 5. Discussion and Limitations

Our testbed trades ecological validity (a large pretrained LLM on open-domain natural-language QA) for full experimental control (exact ground truth, controlled answer length, a fixed compute budget, complete reproducibility). The absolute numbers — a 0.1M-parameter model trained for 600 steps on synthetic arithmetic — should not be read as claims about how GPT-4-class models behave on open-domain hallucination benchmarks; larger pretrained models with richer semantic clustering (via NLI-based entailment rather than exact-match, which is unavailable for open-ended text) may show a different balance. What we believe transfers is the *qualitative* finding, consistent with the Semantic Energy critique (arXiv:2508.14496) that entropy-based clustering under-uses logit information: log-probability is a strong, essentially free discrimination signal that sampling-based methods should be benchmarked against directly, and that discrimination (AUROC) and calibration (ECE) are genuinely different axes that a single K-sample budget does not automatically improve together. We only tested one task family (arithmetic) and one small architecture; replicating the sweep on a second synthetic task (e.g., simple factual lookup) and on a small pretrained open-source model would strengthen the claim, but was outside our compute/time budget for this study.

## 6. Conclusion

On a controlled, fully reproducible offline testbed, we find that a free single-pass signal (greedy sequence log-probability) discriminates correct from incorrect LLM answers better than 8-sample semantic entropy or self-consistency, at every sampling budget we could afford to test — while self-consistency remains far better calibrated as a probability. This suggests uncertainty-signal choice in practice should be driven by whether the application needs ranking or calibrated probabilities, and that cheap log-probability baselines deserve to be reported alongside sampling-based methods rather than dismissed by default. We release the full training/evaluation code, raw per-question results, and logs so the comparison can be extended or challenged directly.
