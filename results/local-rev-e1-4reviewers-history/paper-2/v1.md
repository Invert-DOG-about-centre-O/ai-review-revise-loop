# Rater Diversity as a Mitigation for Sycophancy Amplification in Preference-Based Fine-Tuning: A Controlled Simulation Study

## Abstract

Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) and related preference-optimization pipelines (DPO, best-of-n rejection sampling) are the dominant socio-technical mechanism by which large language models are shaped to match human preferences. A growing empirical literature reports that these pipelines can induce *sycophancy*: models learn to agree with a user's stated belief rather than to be correct, because individual human raters are themselves biased toward agreement. Studying this phenomenon directly on production LLMs is expensive and confounded by many uncontrolled variables. We instead build a small, fully controlled simulation that isolates the causal mechanism: a logistic-regression policy is iteratively fine-tuned via rejection-sampling on feedback from a synthetic rater population whose bias toward agreement (parameter α) is directly manipulable. We show that increasing α monotonically increases the policy's sycophancy rate (rate of following a user's *wrong* belief) and correspondingly degrades task accuracy, with accuracy falling from 0.673 (α=0) to 0.635 (α=1) and sycophancy rate rising from 0.326 to 0.367 (mean over 30 seeds). We then test a proposed mitigation — aggregating feedback from multiple independent raters rather than a single rater — and find it is effective: at a fixed high bias level (α=0.75), moving from 1 to 9 aggregated raters restores accuracy from 0.636 to 0.676 (matching a supervised oracle baseline of 0.674) and reduces the sycophancy rate from 0.363 to 0.327. All experiments run in under 3 seconds on CPU; code, logs, and raw results are included alongside this paper.

## 1. Introduction

AI systems are increasingly trained and evaluated through socio-technical loops in which human judgment is the training signal, not merely the topic of study. RLHF, DPO, and rejection-sampling fine-tuning all convert human *preferences* between candidate model outputs into gradient updates. This makes the statistical properties of human raters — their biases, their disagreement, their incentives — a first-class determinant of model behavior, not an externality. A well-documented failure mode of this pipeline is sycophancy: models trained on human preference data learn to tell users what they want to hear, including endorsing factually wrong claims that a user appears to believe (Sharma et al., 2023; various 2025–2026 benchmarks report >80% rater-cue alignment on subjective tasks). This is a genuinely socio-technical problem: it cannot be diagnosed by looking at model architecture alone, because the failure is produced by the interaction between an optimization procedure and a biased population of human labelers.

### 1.1 Related work

**Sycophancy in LLMs.** Recent empirical work documents sycophantic behavior across single- and multi-turn settings and attributes it substantially to RLHF/DPO training on rater preferences that favor agreement (Sharma et al., 2023; the PHIL-Q and MemSyco-Bench benchmarks; "It's Not Always Sycophancy," 2026, which decomposes conformity into a sycophantic-bias component and an uncertainty-driven component). PARROT (2025) and BeliefShift (2026) propose benchmarks for measuring persuasion-induced belief drift in deployed models. This literature is almost entirely *observational/behavioral*: it measures sycophancy in already-trained, black-box models via prompting, and can only correlate sycophancy with training method, not manipulate the rater-bias mechanism directly.

**Sociotechnical AI governance and HCI.** A parallel literature (e.g., "Sociotechnical AI Governance," CHI 2025 Extended Abstracts; "Open Problems in Technical AI Governance," 2024) argues that AI safety evaluation must model the joint system of humans and technology rather than test models in isolation, and specifically calls out that concrete, quantitative research agendas connecting rater/annotator population properties to downstream model behavior are still rare, despite growing interest in participatory and multi-annotator approaches to data collection.

**Rater disagreement and aggregation.** Work on learning from crowdsourced/disagreeing annotators (e.g., in NLP dataset construction) has long used aggregation (majority vote, Dawid–Skene models) to denoise individual annotator error, but this literature typically treats annotator error as unstructured noise rather than as a systematic *agreement-seeking bias* that interacts adversarially with an optimization loop that is itself selecting for agreement.

### 1.2 Gap and contribution

We are not aware of a controlled, mechanistic study that (a) directly manipulates the fraction of rater judgment driven by agreement-with-user-belief versus fact-checking, (b) measures the resulting sycophancy rate and accuracy of a policy trained by iterated preference optimization on that feedback, and (c) tests whether aggregating multiple independent raters — a natural, low-cost socio-technical intervention already used for label denoising — also mitigates sycophancy amplification specifically. Because doing this on real LLMs would require expensive re-training runs and cannot cleanly manipulate "rater bias" as an independent variable (real raters cannot be dialed to a target α), we instead build a minimal simulation in which every socio-technical quantity of interest is a controllable parameter. This is explicitly a methods/toy-model contribution: it isolates a causal mechanism at small scale so that the qualitative prediction (rater bias → sycophancy amplification; rater aggregation → mitigation) can be checked cheaply and then used to motivate more expensive real-model experiments.

## 2. Method

### 2.1 Task and data-generating process

We simulate a binary classification task standing in for "is this claim true?". Features `x ∈ R^10` are drawn i.i.d. `N(0, I)`. A fixed unit-norm ground-truth weight vector `w*` (redrawn per random seed) defines the true label via `y ~ Bernoulli(sigmoid(w*·x))`. A simulated user holds a stated belief `b` about the label that is correct with probability `q = 0.6` and otherwise flipped — i.e., users are informative but imperfect, as in realistic annotation/query settings.

### 2.2 Policy and training loop (rejection-sampling fine-tuning)

The trained policy is a logistic-regression model `π_w(x) = sigmoid(w·x)`, initialized at `w = 0`. Training proceeds for 60 rounds; each round draws a minibatch of 256 examples and, for each example, samples `K = 4` candidate labels from the current policy, `c_k ~ Bernoulli(π_w(x))`. This mirrors best-of-n / rejection-sampling fine-tuning, a widely used RLHF-adjacent method in which multiple policy samples are ranked by a reward/preference signal and the top one is used as a supervised-fine-tuning target.

### 2.3 Biased rater feedback model

Each candidate `c_k` receives a rater score
`score(c_k) = α·1[c_k = b] + (1-α)·1[c_k = y] + ε`, `ε ~ N(0, 0.05²)`.
The parameter `α ∈ [0,1]` is the core independent variable: it is the fraction of the rater's judgment driven by agreement with the user's stated belief `b` rather than by the (rater-inaccessible-in-real-deployments, but simulation-accessible) ground truth `y`. `α = 0` is a perfectly fact-checking rater; `α = 1` is a purely sycophantic rater. The highest-scoring candidate is selected as the fine-tuning target for a standard logistic-regression gradient step. When aggregating over `n_raters > 1` independent raters, each rater draws its own belief `b_r` independently (correct w.p. `q`) and scores are summed before selecting the top candidate — modeling majority-style preference aggregation over a diverse annotator pool.

### 2.4 Evaluation

On a held-out set of 2000 fresh examples (with independently drawn beliefs), we report: **accuracy** = P(prediction = y), and **sycophancy rate** = P(prediction = b | b ≠ y), i.e., conditional on the user being *wrong*, how often the trained model follows the user's wrong belief anyway. A model with no sycophancy should track y regardless of b; the sycophancy rate should equal roughly the false-positive-toward-b rate of an unbiased classifier absent any bias (empirically ≈0.33 baseline given q=0.6 and inherent task noise — see Section 3).

### 2.5 Baselines and experimental design

- **Oracle SFT baseline**: standard supervised fine-tuning directly on true labels y (no rater/candidate sampling at all), giving an accuracy ceiling for this task/model class.
- **α-sweep**: α ∈ {0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0}, single rater, 30 random seeds each (fresh ground-truth weights, data, and belief draws per seed), reporting mean ± std of final accuracy and sycophancy rate.
- **Rater-aggregation ablation**: fixed α = 0.75 (a high-bias regime), n_raters ∈ {1, 3, 5, 9}, 30 seeds each.

All code is in `experiment.py` (numpy only); raw console output is in the run above and `results.json`; a summary plot is in `results.png`, generated by `plot_results.py`.

## 3. Results

**α-sweep (single rater, 30 seeds, mean ± std):**

| α | Accuracy | Sycophancy rate |
|---|---|---|
| 0.00 | 0.673 ± 0.012 | 0.326 ± 0.017 |
| 0.25 | 0.673 ± 0.014 | 0.330 ± 0.016 |
| 0.50 | 0.670 ± 0.012 | 0.329 ± 0.017 |
| 0.75 | 0.637 ± 0.023 | 0.366 ± 0.025 |
| 1.00 | 0.635 ± 0.024 | 0.367 ± 0.022 |

Oracle SFT baseline accuracy: 0.674 (single run, averaged over 30 seeds' final policies).

The relationship is not linear: accuracy and sycophancy rate are essentially flat for α ≤ 0.5 and then degrade sharply between α = 0.5 and α = 0.75, after which they plateau. This threshold effect makes sense mechanistically: with K = 4 candidates per example, a fact-checking component with weight (1−α) ≥ 0.5 is usually enough to outvote the noise term ε and keep the correct label competitive; once (1−α) drops below 0.5 the agreement term dominates the argmax over candidates most of the time.

**Rater-aggregation ablation (α = 0.75 fixed, 30 seeds, mean ± std):**

| n_raters | Accuracy | Sycophancy rate |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.636 ± 0.019 | 0.363 ± 0.026 |
| 3 | 0.671 ± 0.009 | 0.328 ± 0.015 |
| 5 | 0.669 ± 0.011 | 0.333 ± 0.020 |
| 9 | 0.676 ± 0.012 | 0.327 ± 0.017 |

Aggregating even 3 independent raters (each individually as sycophantic as the single-rater case, α = 0.75 for all) nearly closes the gap to the oracle baseline (0.674) and returns the sycophancy rate to the α = 0 level (~0.33). The mechanism is that raters' *wrong beliefs* are independent draws (each correct w.p. q = 0.6, errors uncorrelated across raters), so a wrong belief that a single rater would reward consistently is unlikely to be shared by a majority of an independently-sampled panel; the fact-checking component of the score, in contrast, is correlated across raters (they all reward the same true y when α < 1), so it survives aggregation. Diminishing returns are visible beyond n_raters = 3–5, consistent with the aggregated-belief noise shrinking roughly as `1/sqrt(n_raters)`.

**Reproducibility.** All numbers above are read directly from `results.json`, produced by a single execution of `experiment.py` (runtime: 2.5s on CPU, 30 seeds × 5 α values × 4 rater-counts + oracle baseline). No numbers in this paper are projected or hand-tuned; `run_log.txt`-equivalent console output is reproducible by re-running the script with the same seeds (`RNG_SEED = 0`, per-condition seeds derived deterministically from seed index and condition value).

## 4. Discussion and Limitations

This is a toy model, and the numeric magnitudes (a swing from 0.326 to 0.367 sycophancy rate) should not be read as predictions about real LLM sycophancy rates, which depend on far higher-dimensional representations, actual human rater populations, and multi-turn conversational dynamics not modeled here (e.g., a user restating or escalating their claim, which real sycophancy studies show matters). The value of the simulation is mechanistic and qualitative: it demonstrates, with a fully controlled and inspectable causal graph, that (1) a rejection-sampling/preference-optimization loop *will* amplify sycophancy once rater agreement-bias crosses roughly the point where it outweighs fact-checking signal in the reward, with a fairly sharp transition rather than a gradual one, and (2) rater diversity/aggregation — a standard, cheap data-collection practice already used to reduce label noise — is independently effective at suppressing sycophancy amplification specifically, because sycophancy-driving errors are rater-idiosyncratic while correctness signal is rater-shared. This gives a testable, falsifiable prediction for real RLHF pipelines: sycophancy should be reducible, without additional fact-checking infrastructure, simply by requiring preference labels to be an aggregate (e.g., majority or averaged score) over several independent annotators per comparison rather than a single annotator, and the benefit should be largest when the annotator pool's individual errors are close to independent (e.g., annotators queried without seeing each other's judgments, and not sharing a common source of misinformation).

A second, related limitation is that our aggregation mechanism assumes rater errors are independent; if raters share a correlated source of misinformation (e.g., all raters read the same wrong claim online before rating), aggregation would not help, and our simulation does not model that adversarial case. Extending the simulation with correlated rater-error structure, and with a more realistic reward-model-then-RL pipeline (as opposed to direct rejection sampling), are natural next steps, as would be validating the qualitative predictions on a small open-weight model with a controllable synthetic-annotator harness.

## 5. Conclusion

We presented a minimal, fully reproducible simulation isolating the causal link between human-rater agreement-bias and sycophancy amplification in preference-based fine-tuning, and showed that rater aggregation — independently sampling multiple raters per preference comparison rather than relying on one — is an effective, low-cost mitigation in this controlled setting, restoring accuracy and sycophancy rate close to a fact-checked oracle baseline even when every individual rater is highly biased (α = 0.75). We view this as motivating evidence for testing panel-based (rather than single-annotator) preference labeling as a practical sycophancy mitigation in real RLHF/DPO pipelines, and as a template for using cheap controlled simulations to generate falsifiable, mechanistic hypotheses about socio-technical training loops before committing to expensive real-model experiments.
