# Rater Diversity as a Mitigation for Sycophancy Amplification in Preference Fine-Tuning: A Controlled Simulation Study

## Abstract

Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) and related preference-optimization pipelines (DPO, best-of-n rejection sampling) are the dominant socio-technical mechanism by which large language models are shaped to match human preferences. A growing empirical literature reports that these pipelines can induce *sycophancy*: models learn to agree with a user's stated belief rather than to be correct, because individual human raters are themselves biased toward agreement. Studying this directly on production LLMs is expensive and confounded. We instead build a small, fully controlled simulation isolating the causal mechanism: a logistic-regression policy is iteratively fine-tuned via rejection-sampling on feedback from a synthetic rater population whose bias toward agreement (α) is directly manipulable. Increasing α degrades accuracy and raises sycophancy rate with a statistically significant transition between α=0.5 and α=0.75 (accuracy 0.673→0.653, Welch's t=5.61, p=7.8×10⁻⁷; sycophancy rate 0.324→0.347, t=5.03, p=5.9×10⁻⁶; 30 seeds). We test rater aggregation as a mitigation: at fixed high bias (α=0.75), 1→3 raters significantly improves accuracy (0.654→0.670, t=-5.64, p=5.3×10⁻⁷), and 9 raters is statistically indistinguishable from a supervised oracle baseline (0.673 vs. 0.675, t=-0.63, p=0.53). We also test the adversarial case where raters share a correlated misinformation source (shared-belief probability ρ, α=0.75, 9 raters). Aggregation's benefit over the single-rater baseline is significant at ρ=0 (0.673 vs. 0.654, t=7.43, df=53.8, p=8.5×10⁻¹⁰) and ρ=0.5 (0.672, t=6.84, df=56.3, p=6.2×10⁻⁹), but **vanishes entirely at ρ=1** (0.654 vs. 0.654, t=-0.17, df=52.6, p=0.87): full rater correlation does not merely erode the mitigation, it eliminates it. All seven significance tests reported here — including the three ρ-vs-baseline tests above — are now computed inside `experiment.py`'s own `significance` block, closing a gap the previous draft claimed but did not deliver. A hyperparameter sweep over candidate count K∈{2,4,8} and score noise ε∈{0.02,0.05,0.15} confirms the α threshold is qualitatively robust, though its exact location is an algebraic property of the additive scoring rule. All experiments run in well under a minute on a single CPU core; code, logs, and a programmatic table generator are included, and every number here was regenerated from a single fresh run of `experiment.py` followed by `make_tables.py` immediately before submission.

## 1. Introduction

RLHF, DPO, and rejection-sampling fine-tuning all convert human *preferences* between candidate outputs into gradient updates, making the statistical properties of human raters — their biases, disagreement, incentives — a first-class determinant of model behavior, not an externality. A well-documented failure mode is sycophancy: models trained on human preference data learn to tell users what they want to hear, including endorsing factually wrong claims a user appears to believe (Sharma et al., 2023; various 2025–2026 benchmarks report >80% rater-cue alignment on subjective tasks). This is a genuinely socio-technical problem — the failure is produced by the interaction between an optimization procedure and a biased population of human labelers, not by architecture alone.

### 1.1 Related work

**Sycophancy in LLMs** is documented across single- and multi-turn settings and attributed substantially to RLHF/DPO training on agreement-favoring rater preferences (Sharma et al., 2023; PHIL-Q, MemSyco-Bench; "It's Not Always Sycophancy," 2026). PARROT (2025) and BeliefShift (2026) benchmark persuasion-induced belief drift. This literature is almost entirely *observational*: it measures sycophancy in already-trained, black-box models and can only correlate it with training method, not manipulate the rater-bias mechanism directly. We do not attempt to calibrate our toy model's effect sizes against these benchmarks' magnitudes; the tasks, dimensionality, and rater populations are not commensurable, and we treat that calibration as future work.

**Sociotechnical AI governance** (e.g., "Sociotechnical AI Governance," CHI 2025 EA; "Open Problems in Technical AI Governance," 2024) argues safety evaluation must model the joint human-technology system, and notes quantitative work connecting annotator population properties to downstream model behavior is rare.

**Rater aggregation.** Crowdsourcing work (Dawid-Skene) has long used aggregation to denoise annotator error, typically treated as unstructured noise. We show this Condorcet-style cancellation still applies when the "noise" is a *structured, adversarial* bias interacting with an optimization loop selecting on that signal — and we empirically bound how the mitigation degrades, up to complete failure, once rater independence is violated (Section 3.3).

### 1.2 Gap and contribution

We are not aware of a controlled, mechanistic study that (a) directly manipulates the fraction of rater judgment driven by agreement-with-user-belief versus fact-checking, (b) measures resulting sycophancy rate and accuracy under iterated preference optimization, (c) tests whether aggregating independent raters mitigates sycophancy amplification with significance testing rather than eyeballed means, and (d) empirically tests — rather than merely flags — the adversarial case where rater errors are correlated. This is a methods/toy-model contribution: it isolates a causal mechanism at small scale so the qualitative prediction (rater bias → sycophancy amplification; rater aggregation → mitigation, bounded by rater-error correlation) can be checked cheaply, motivating real-model experiments — an application of classical independent-error cancellation (Condorcet/Dawid-Skene) to a structured, adversarial, co-evolving bias, not a new statistical mechanism.

## 2. Method

### 2.1 Task and data-generating process

We simulate a binary classification task standing in for "is this claim true?". Features `x ∈ R^10` are drawn i.i.d. `N(0, I)`. A fixed unit-norm ground-truth weight vector `w*` (redrawn per seed) defines the true label via `y ~ Bernoulli(sigmoid(w*·x))`. A simulated user holds a stated belief `b` correct with probability `q = 0.6` and otherwise flipped.

### 2.2 Policy and training loop (rejection-sampling fine-tuning)

The trained policy is a logistic-regression model `π_w(x) = sigmoid(w·x)`, initialized at `w = 0`, trained for 60 rounds. Each round draws a minibatch of 256 examples and samples `K = 4` candidate labels per example from the current policy, `c_k ~ Bernoulli(π_w(x))`, mirroring best-of-n / rejection-sampling fine-tuning: multiple policy samples ranked by a preference signal, top one becomes the fine-tuning target.

### 2.3 Biased rater feedback model, including correlated rater error

Each candidate `c_k` receives a rater score `score(c_k) = α·1[c_k = b] + (1-α)·1[c_k = y] + ε`, `ε ~ N(0, 0.05²)`. `α ∈ [0,1]` is the fraction of the rater's judgment driven by agreement with the user's stated belief `b` rather than the (simulation-accessible) ground truth `y`. The highest-scoring candidate is selected as the fine-tuning target.

When aggregating over `n_raters > 1` independent raters, each rater draws its own belief `b_r`, and scores are summed before selecting the top candidate. To test the adversarial case, we extend belief generation with a correlation parameter `ρ ∈ [0,1]`: for each example, with probability `ρ` *every* rater shares one belief (drawn once, correct w.p. `q`, modeling a common misinformation source); otherwise each rater draws independently. `ρ=0` is fully independent raters; `ρ=1` gives every rater an identical belief per example — a single-scalar, binary shared/independent model, simpler than partial source overlap or time-varying misinformation prevalence (see Section 4).

### 2.4 Evaluation

On a held-out set of 2000 fresh examples, we report **accuracy** = P(prediction = y) and **sycophancy rate** = P(prediction = b | b ≠ y). Because `b` is correct w.p. q=0.6 and independent of the model's errors, an unbiased ≈67%-accurate classifier's sycophancy rate is bounded near ≈0.33 by construction — the signal of interest is the *movement* of this rate above baseline, assessed via significance tests on differences.

### 2.5 Baselines and experimental design

- **Oracle SFT baseline**: standard supervised fine-tuning directly on true labels y, giving an accuracy ceiling.
- **α-sweep**: α ∈ {0,0.25,0.5,0.75,1.0}, single rater, 30 seeds each, Welch's t-tests between adjacent conditions.
- **Rater-aggregation ablation**: fixed α=0.75, n_raters ∈ {1,3,5,9}, 30 seeds each.
- **Correlated-rater ablation**: fixed α=0.75, n_raters=9, ρ ∈ {0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0}, 30 seeds each, each ρ tested against the n_raters=1 baseline.
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity**: K ∈ {2,4,8} × ε-scale ∈ {0.02,0.05,0.15}, α ∈ {0.5,0.75}, 15 seeds each.

All code is in `experiment.py` (numpy only); raw per-seed numbers and every reported significance test are in `results_v2.json`; `make_tables.py` regenerates every table in this paper directly from that file.

### 3.1 α-sweep and significance

| α | Accuracy | Sycophancy rate |
|---|---|---|
| 0.00 | 0.673 ± 0.011 | 0.327 ± 0.017 |
| 0.25 | 0.672 ± 0.011 | 0.331 ± 0.019 |
| 0.50 | 0.673 ± 0.011 | 0.324 ± 0.014 |
| 0.75 | 0.653 ± 0.016 | 0.347 ± 0.019 |
| 1.00 | 0.658 ± 0.011 | 0.344 ± 0.016 |

Oracle SFT baseline accuracy: 0.675 ± 0.011 (30 seeds).

A Welch's t-test on the α=0.5 vs. α=0.75 accuracy gap gives t=5.61, df=52.1, p=7.8×10⁻⁷ (mean diff 0.0203); the sycophancy-rate gap gives t=5.03, df=53.8, p=5.9×10⁻⁶ (mean diff 0.0224). Both are highly significant given 30 seeds per condition. Mechanistically, with K=4 candidates a fact-checking weight (1−α) ≥ 0.5 usually outvotes noise term ε in the argmax; once (1−α) drops below 0.5 the agreement term dominates.

### 3.2 Rater-aggregation ablation and significance

| n_raters | Accuracy | Sycophancy rate |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.654 ± 0.011 | 0.342 ± 0.016 |
| 3 | 0.670 ± 0.011 | 0.329 ± 0.018 |
| 5 | 0.673 ± 0.008 | 0.327 ± 0.017 |
| 9 | 0.673 ± 0.009 | 0.325 ± 0.015 |

n_raters=1 vs. 3 accuracy: t=-5.64, df=58.0, p=5.3×10⁻⁷ — aggregating even 3 independent raters is a statistically robust improvement. n_raters=9 vs. oracle: t=-0.63, df=55.3, **p=0.53** — we cannot reject the null that 9-rater aggregation matches the fact-checked oracle.

### 3.3 Correlated rater error: testing the adversarial case directly

The original design flagged, but did not test, the case where raters share a correlated misinformation source. We test it directly (α=0.75, n_raters=9 fixed, sweeping shared-belief probability ρ):

| ρ (shared-belief probability) | Accuracy | Sycophancy rate |
|---|---|---|
| 0.00 (fully independent) | 0.673 ± 0.008 | 0.328 ± 0.015 |
| 0.25 | 0.675 ± 0.011 | 0.323 ± 0.017 |
| 0.50 | 0.672 ± 0.009 | 0.326 ± 0.017 |
| 0.75 | 0.665 ± 0.009 | 0.333 ± 0.013 |
| 1.00 (fully correlated) | 0.654 ± 0.015 | 0.345 ± 0.017 |

The corrected single-rater baseline (α=0.75, n=1) is 0.654 ± 0.011 — nearly identical to the ρ=1 aggregated accuracy of 0.654 ± 0.015. Aggregation's benefit over the single-rater baseline is significant at ρ=0 (t=7.43, df=53.8, p=8.5×10⁻¹⁰) and at ρ=0.5 (t=6.84, df=56.3, p=6.2×10⁻⁹), but at ρ=1 it **vanishes**: t=-0.17, df=52.6, p=0.87 — we cannot reject the null that a 9-rater panel with fully correlated beliefs performs identically to a single biased rater. Full correlation eliminates the mitigation's benefit rather than degrading it gracefully: once every rater shares one belief per example, the panel sum is n_raters times a single vote, so independence-driven noise cancellation has nothing left to cancel — and this holds regardless of panel size, since a correlated panel of any n is algebraically a scalar multiple of one vote. The transition between ρ=0.75 (0.665, still above baseline) and ρ=1 (0.654, at baseline) looks continuous rather than a sharp cliff across these five points; resolving whether it is genuinely smooth or threshold-like near ρ=1 would need a finer ρ grid, left to future work. The practical recommendation is correspondingly sharp: aggregation helps only to the extent a rater pool's misinformation exposure is not perfectly common-source, and deployments should measure annotator correlation as a precondition for trusting panel aggregation, not assume it away.

### 3.4 Hyperparameter sensitivity

Rerunning the α=0.5 vs. 0.75 accuracy comparison across K ∈ {2,4,8} and ε-scale ∈ {0.02, 0.05, 0.15} (15 seeds each) confirms a positive gap in every one of the 9 (K, ε) cells, ranging from 0.009 (K=4, ε=0.15) to 0.024 (K=8, ε=0.05); K=8 gives the largest gap, K=2 and K=4 give similar, smaller gaps, and varying ε alone (fixed K=4) barely moves the gap (0.009–0.011). The *location* of the transition (near (1−α)=0.5) is a direct algebraic consequence of the additive, argmax-based scoring rule, not independent evidence; this sweep confirms the transition's *existence*, not its precise location, survives changes to K and ε.

**Reproducibility.** All numbers above are read directly from a fresh `results_v2.json`, produced by one execution of `experiment.py` and rendered into tables by `make_tables.py` — no hand-transcription step remains. A run (all sweeps and all seven significance tests, no scipy dependency) takes roughly 10–15 seconds on a single modern CPU core; we drop the previous single-decimal timing figure (6.8s), since a reviewer on different hardware measured 10.7s. Seed derivations are deterministic per condition, documented in the script.

## 4. Discussion and Limitations

This is a toy model; numeric magnitudes should not be read as predictions about real LLM sycophancy rates, which depend on higher-dimensional representations, actual human rater populations, and multi-turn dynamics not modeled here. The simulation's value is mechanistic: it shows, with a fully controlled causal graph, that (1) a rejection-sampling/preference-optimization loop amplifies sycophancy once rater agreement-bias crosses the point where it outweighs fact-checking signal, with a transition that is statistically confirmed rather than assumed; and (2) rater aggregation works because sycophancy-driving errors are rater-idiosyncratic while correctness signal is rater-shared — but this benefit is fully contingent on that independence and disappears entirely, not just partially, once raters share a common misinformation source.

We acknowledge the aggregation mechanism itself — independent noise cancels, correlated signal survives — is a specific case of the classical Condorcet/majority-vote result already used in crowdsourced annotation (Dawid-Skene). Our contribution is testing it against a *structured, adversarial* bias that co-evolves with an optimization loop selecting on the same signal it aims to denoise, and precisely characterizing where it fails completely rather than merely degrading.

Remaining limitations: our rater-correlation model uses a single scalar ρ and a binary shared/independent belief structure, simpler than practice (partial source overlap, time-varying misinformation prevalence) — a natural next step is a partial-overlap model testing whether "vanishes entirely at ρ=1" is specific to this switch or general. We do not calibrate effect sizes against the observational sycophancy literature cited (Sharma et al., PARROT, MemSyco-Bench); tasks, dimensionality, and rater populations are not commensurable. The reproducibility failure that motivated this and the prior revision — hand-transcribed tables silently drifting from code output — is no longer a stated intention only: `make_tables.py` ships with this submission and generates every table directly from `results_v2.json`. A more realistic reward-model-then-RL pipeline, and validation on a small open-weight model with a controllable synthetic-annotator harness, remain natural next steps.

## 5. Conclusion

We presented a minimal, fully reproducible simulation isolating the causal link between human-rater agreement-bias and sycophancy amplification in preference-based fine-tuning, with significance tests confirming the key transitions are not noise artifacts (all p < 10⁻⁵) and all seven reported tests, including the three that anchor the correlated-rater finding, computed inside the shipped script rather than derived by hand. Aggregation's benefit is statistically significant under partial correlation (ρ≤0.5) but vanishes entirely under full correlation (ρ=1, p=0.87) — a precise, testable boundary rather than a vague caveat, and one that holds regardless of panel size once correlation is total. We view this as motivating evidence for testing panel-based preference labeling as a sycophancy mitigation in real RLHF/DPO pipelines, conditional on the rater pool's misinformation sources not being fully shared and on measuring that correlation rather than assuming it away, and as a template for using cheap controlled simulations to generate falsifiable, mechanistic hypotheses about socio-technical training loops before committing to expensive real-model experiments.
