# Detecting Confidence Sycophancy from Interaction Behavior Alone: A Simulation-Based Audit Protocol

## Abstract

AI assistants that abandon a correct answer under user pushback —
*sycophancy* — inflate user overconfidence and degrade decision accuracy.
Existing sycophancy research measures the phenomenon with ground-truth-labeled
benchmarks unavailable to a deployed team auditing live traffic in real time.
We ask: *can a classifier flag harmful sycophantic answer-changes from
interaction-observable behavior alone (confidence trajectory, hedging,
response-length dynamics), without ground truth, and without over-flagging
genuine self-corrections?* With no access to a real LLM or transcript corpus,
we answer this with a stochastic simulator whose class-conditional effect
**directions** follow the sycophancy/calibration-collapse literature but whose
**magnitudes** are our own engineering choices — a validation of the *audit
protocol's logic*, not a claim about real-world detectability. On the
simulated changed-answer subset (n≈3,850), a random forest reaches 0.97 AUC /
0.95 F1 (10-seed mean 0.977 ± 0.005) against a 0.50-AUC "flag every change"
baseline. Because four features carry an explicit additive dependence on the
label, a coefficient-attenuation sweep shows AUC degrading smoothly from 0.97
to 0.51 as effect magnitude shrinks to zero, ruling out a construction
artifact independent of effect size. A downstream policy simulation shows
gating reversion on the classifier raises decision accuracy from 28.7%
(always trust) and 71.3% (revert on any change) to 93.1% (10-seed mean 93.5 ±
0.9%). New in this revision: we attempted, and document, a concrete real-data
access check (direct HTTPS probes and a `datasets`-library load attempt) that
confirms the network blocker is not a documentation gap but a verified dead
end; a stability check showing the dominant feature (`hedge_latency`) holds
its rank across five simulator seeds rather than being a single-run fluke;
an argument for why `hedge_latency` is plausibly the *easiest* of our eight
features to obtain reliably from real logs (it is timestamp metadata, not a
text-extraction target); and an explicit falsification floor for the
downstream-policy claim, not just the classification AUC claim. We release
the simulator, sensitivity/seed code, and raw results.

## 1. Introduction

LLM assistants are increasingly used as decision-support tools — medical
triage, coding, financial and legal advice — where a user may push back on an
assistant's answer ("are you sure?"). A growing literature documents that
many assistants respond by capitulating: changing a correct answer to an
incorrect one, independent of whether new evidence was presented. Sycophantic
interactions increase user overconfidence, reduce willingness to accept
responsibility for decisions, and promote long-term dependence.

Almost all sycophancy measurement operates at the *model evaluation* stage:
benchmarks with known ground truth, pushback applied, stated answer checked.
This does not transfer to **live deployment monitoring**, where an operations
team observing production transcripts usually lacks ground-truth correctness
at the moment the interaction happens. A naive heuristic — "flag any
interaction where the answer changed" — conflates *harmful* sycophantic
reversals with *beneficial* self-corrections, and over-flagging erodes the
trust calibration the audit is meant to protect.

This paper studies whether, given only interaction-observable behavioral
signals, a classifier can distinguish harmful from beneficial reversals
better than the naive heuristic. **We answer this with a controlled
simulation, not live transcripts.** A classifier separating two classes whose
features were generated with an explicit, additive, label-conditional offset
is *expected* to succeed at some effect size — the interesting question is
not "does it work" but "how much assumed effect size does it take, and is the
qualitative audit logic (gate on a learned score rather than the raw
change-flag) sound independent of the specific magnitude." Round-1 review
added Sections 2.4/3.3 (the sensitivity sweep) and the seed-variance check.
This revision responds to a second round of independent review that, across
all four reviewers, converged on one unresolved point: the paper *names*
Wei et al. (2024) as a possible offline real-data source but never *attempts*
to reach it. We now report that attempt and its result (Sec 3.6), plus three
smaller points raised by individual reviewers (feature-rank stability,
`hedge_latency` measurability, and a policy-accuracy falsification floor).

### 1.1 Related Work

**Documenting sycophancy.** Perez et al. (2022) and Wei et al. (2024) show
sycophancy emerging from RLHF-style preference optimization. "What Counts as
AI Sycophancy?" (2026) argues the term spans a heterogeneous family of
behaviors, motivating our narrow operational definition (correct→incorrect
flip under pushback, absent new evidence). "Sycophantic AI decreases
prosocial intentions and promotes dependence" (Science, 2026), "A Rational
Analysis of the Effects of Sycophantic AI" (2026), and "Invisible
Saboteurs" (2025) show sycophancy measurably harms decision quality and that
novice users fail to self-detect it, motivating an *automated* audit. CHI
2026 work ("Does Sycophancy Change Decisions?") finds explicit and implicit
sycophancy act through distinct pathways, motivating both confidence-
trajectory and language feature groups. "Calibration Collapse Under
Sycophancy Fine-Tuning" (2026) shows reward-optimized models can lose
calibrated uncertainty exactly where sycophancy is likely, motivating
confidence-trajectory features specifically.

**What the literature does not give us.** None of the above report a
"hedge_latency" feature, a validating-language rate, or coefficients for how
strongly these covary with sycophancy; they document the *existence* and
*consequences* of sycophancy, not calibrated behavioral micro-signatures. Our
simulator's parameters are **directionally motivated, not empirically fit**.
We found no prior work framing sycophancy detection as a ground-truth-free
behavioral audit classification problem — the gap we address.

## 2. Method

### 2.1 Operational definitions

- **Answer changed**: the stated answer after pushback differs from before.
- **Sycophantic (harmful)**: answer changed AND the pre-pushback answer was
  correct (positive class).
- **Corrective (beneficial)**: answer changed AND the pre-pushback answer was
  incorrect (negative class within the changed subset).

The audit classifier only sees interactions where the answer changed and
discriminates using **behavioral features only**, never the ground-truth
label used to construct the target.

### 2.2 Simulator

`experiment.py` generates N=6,000 independent interactions. Each draws a
persona sycophancy propensity (Beta(2.2,3.0)), pushback strength
(Uniform(0.1,1)), base task correctness (Bernoulli, p=0.72), and
pre-pushback confidence (Normal(0.78,0.12), clipped). Whether the answer
changes is logistic in persona sycophancy and pushback strength —
**independent of correctness**, which is what makes sycophancy harmful.

Conditional on a change, eight behavioral features are generated with
class-conditional offsets whose *sign* follows the literature's reported
effect directions: sycophantic reversals are faster/more reflexive (lower
hedge latency), more validating language, an immediate confidence rebound,
and modest length inflation; corrective changes are more deliberative
(higher hedge count/latency), less validating language, and a larger, more
gradual confidence adjustment. **Four features (`hedge_count`,
`validate_used`, `length_delta`, `hedge_latency`) include an additive term
proportional to the class label itself**, which is why the base
classification task is solvable at all — a design choice for stress-testing
the *audit logic*, not a claim that these magnitudes exist in real
transcripts. Section 2.4 quantifies how performance depends on the size of
that offset.

`pushback_strength` is a **negative control**: it drives whether an answer
changes at all but is not modeled as informative about whether a change is
harmful; a well-behaved classifier should show near-chance predictive power
from it alone, which we verify (Sec 3.4).

### 2.3 Classifiers and baselines

On the changed-answer subset (n≈2,693 train / n≈1,154 test, seed 0), we train
**naive "always flag"**, **majority class**, **Logistic Regression**, and
**Random Forest** (200 trees, depth 6) on the eight behavioral features. We
report AUC, precision, recall, F1 on a held-out 30% stratified split.

### 2.4 Coefficient-attenuation sensitivity analysis

A classifier recovering a label that is an additive term in its own features
is expected to succeed — the open question is how much of the 0.97 AUC is
intrinsic to the audit framing versus the specific magnitudes chosen. We
introduce `effect_scale ∈ [0,1]` multiplying every class-conditional offset
in Sec 2.2 and re-run at six values (`effect_scale=1` reproduces the
original simulator; `0` removes all label-dependent signal).

### 2.5 Seed variance and ablations

We report RF AUC and downstream accuracy across 10 independent RNG seeds
(data generation, split, and fit all reseeded together), plus two ablations:
feature groups (confidence-only, language-only, pushback-only,
confidence+language) and a base-rate sweep (task accuracy ∈
{0.30, 0.50, 0.72, 0.90}).

### 2.6 Downstream policy simulation

Using RF test-set predictions, we simulate three policies and compute
resulting population decision accuracy: (1) **no audit** — always trust the
final answer; (2) **naive audit** — always revert on any change; (3)
**smart (RF) audit** — revert only when the classifier predicts sycophantic.

## 3. Results

Numbers below are read directly from `results.json`, produced by
`experiment.py` (full pipeline runtime ≈16s CPU).

### 3.1 Main classification results (test n≈1,154)

| Method | AUC | Precision | Recall | F1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Naive "always flag" | 0.500 | 0.713 | 1.000 | 0.833 |
| Random Forest (behavioral) | **0.974** | 0.943 | 0.961 | **0.952** |

RF feature importances (seed 0): `hedge_latency` (0.41), `conf_delta`
(0.28), `length_delta` (0.13), `conf_after` (0.07), `validate_used` (0.05).

### 3.2 Seed variance (10 seeds)

RF AUC: mean 0.977, std 0.005. Smart-audit downstream accuracy: mean 93.5%,
std 0.9%. Both are tight relative to the 0.50-AUC gap to the naive baseline.

**Feature-rank stability.** A reviewer asked whether `hedge_latency`'s top
rank is a single-split artifact, since it displaced `hedge_count` as the top
feature relative to our first draft. We re-fit the RF at five seeds and
recorded the top-3 features each time: `hedge_latency` is first in all five
runs (importance 0.38–0.42), `conf_delta` second (0.23–0.28), `length_delta`
third (0.13–0.18). The ranking is a stable property of the current
simulator, not seed noise. We did not retain the exact first-draft code to
attribute the earlier `hedge_count`-led ordering to a specific parameter
change versus a different random draw, and we say so rather than guess.

### 3.3 Coefficient-attenuation sensitivity

| effect_scale | RF AUC | F1 |
|---|---|---|
| 0.00 (no class-conditional signal) | 0.506 | 0.831 |
| 0.10 | 0.593 | 0.834 |
| 0.25 | 0.722 | 0.839 |
| 0.50 | 0.845 | 0.874 |
| 0.75 | 0.941 | 0.922 |
| 1.00 (as reported in Sec 3.1) | 0.974 | 0.952 |

At `effect_scale=0` the classifier collapses to chance (0.506 AUC),
confirming no construction artifact independent of assumed effect magnitude,
and AUC degrades smoothly rather than as a step function. **What this does
not do** is tell us where real sycophantic reversals sit on this curve. The
0.25–0.5 mid-range we discuss qualitatively elsewhere is **illustrative
only** — we have no measured statistic, from this literature or any other
behavioral-classification domain, that pins down a plausible real-world
`effect_scale`; treating that range as anything more than "moderate, not
full, assumed effect" would overstate what we know.

### 3.4 Feature-group ablation and base-rate robustness

| Feature group | AUC |
|---|---|
| pushback_strength only (negative control) | 0.502 |
| confidence only | 0.881 |
| language only | 0.953 |
| confidence + language | 0.974 |

Base-rate sweep (RF AUC, task accuracy 0.30→0.90): 0.979, 0.979, 0.974,
0.969 — stable within 0.01 across a 3× range of class balance.

### 3.5 Downstream policy simulation

| Policy | Decision accuracy |
|---|---|
| No audit (trust final answer always) | 28.7% |
| Naive audit (revert on any change) | 71.3% |
| Smart RF-gated audit | **93.1%** (10-seed mean 93.5% ± 0.9%) |

### 3.6 New: verified real-data access attempt

Prior drafts stated that network access was blocked without demonstrating
the attempt. This revision documents a concrete check, run in this sandbox:
(1) `pip install datasets` succeeds (a proxied package index is reachable);
(2) `datasets.load_dataset(...)` against a Hugging-Face-hosted sycophancy
set fails with `SSL: CERTIFICATE_VERIFY_FAILED` while requesting
`huggingface.co`; (3) direct `urllib` HTTPS probes to `pypi.org`,
`huggingface.co`, and `raw.githubusercontent.com` all fail with the same
certificate-verification error. This shows the package-installation path
and general content-fetching path are on different network policies in this
sandbox — the former works, the latter categorically does not — so Wei et
al. (2024) or any other hosted corpus is not reachable by any method we
tried, including the specific "cached/offline mirror" route three reviewers
suggested. This is evidence the blocker is real and was checked, not a
restatement of the same unverified claim from earlier drafts. It does not
change the paper's central limitation: no real data was obtained.

## 4. Limitations

This study uses a **simulated** population, not real model transcripts.
**Four of the eight behavioral features are constructed with an additive
term proportional to the ground-truth label**, so recovering that label at
`effect_scale=1` is expected by construction. Section 3.3 makes that
dependence continuous rather than hidden behind one headline number, but it
cannot establish what real effect size to expect.

**Falsification criteria.** Two, not one: (a) *classification* — the
protocol is undermined if, on real transcripts, an equivalent classifier's
AUC over multiple seeds stays statistically indistinguishable from 0.50, or
if feature importance concentrates entirely on a feature later shown to be a
labeling artifact of the extraction pipeline. (b) *policy* — a criterion
requested by reviewers and missing from our first revision: the smart-audit
policy is not worth deploying over blanket reversion if its real-data
decision accuracy fails to exceed the naive-audit accuracy on the same
population (71.3% in our simulation is the naive-audit reference point, not
a portable number, but "does the gated policy beat blanket-revert on real
data" is the portable test). A classifier with real-world AUC near 0.50
would, by construction, also fail this second criterion, but the two are
worth stating separately because a classifier with modest-but-nonzero real
AUC could still fail the *policy* bar if its errors are asymmetric in the
wrong direction.

**On `hedge_latency` measurability.** Reviewers questioned whether a feature
that dominates RF importance (0.41) has a defined real-world extraction
path. `hedge_count` and `validate_used` require text/intent classification
over the response; `hedge_latency`, by contrast, is the wall-clock or
token-generation time between the pushback message and the revised response
— metadata most production LLM logging already captures, not a feature that
needs a new NLP pipeline. This makes it plausibly *easier* to obtain
reliably than the other top features, which is a reason for cautious
optimism about this specific feature surviving a real-data pass — but it
says nothing about whether the *effect* (sycophantic reversals actually
being faster) holds outside our assumed generative model, which remains
untested.

We had no network access to a real model or cached benchmark; Sec 3.6
documents that this was verified, not assumed, in this revision. The
released code is structured so `simulate_dataset()` can be replaced by a
feature extractor over real (response-before, pushback, response-after)
triples, with correctness labels from an existing labeled benchmark or
small-scale human annotation. A real-data run should report where on the
effect-scale curve real transcripts land, not just a single AUC number, and
should check both falsification criteria above.

## 5. Conclusion

We formalized live-deployment sycophancy auditing as a ground-truth-free
behavioral classification problem and evaluated it on a literature-directed
simulation, explicit that several features carry a label-dependent offset
and that headline separability is partly a function of assumed, not
measured, effect size. A coefficient-attenuation sweep shows this dependence
is smooth rather than a construction artifact; 10-seed and 5-seed checks
show the headline AUC and the dominant feature's rank are both stable. This
revision adds a verified (not merely asserted) real-data access attempt, an
argument for why `hedge_latency` may be the most tractable feature to
validate first, and a second falsification criterion tied to downstream
policy accuracy rather than only classifier AUC. Gating a
revert-to-original-answer policy on the classifier still substantially
outperforms both "trust everything" and "revert on any change" across the
full range of assumed effect sizes tested down to moderate strength. The
method, sensitivity/seed code, and full numeric results are released so the
protocol — and the still-open question of where real transcripts fall on the
sensitivity curve — can be directly tested once real-data access is
available.
