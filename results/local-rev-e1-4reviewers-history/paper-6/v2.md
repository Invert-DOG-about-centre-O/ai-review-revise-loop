# Sycophancy Erodes the Trust It Is Meant to Preserve: An Agent-Based Simulation of Human–AI Trust Calibration Under Deference Bias

## Abstract

Sycophancy — an AI system's tendency to align its stated answer with a user's expressed belief rather than its own best judgement — is usually studied as a property of a single model response. We argue this framing misses a socio-technical dynamic: sycophancy operates inside a *repeated* human-AI loop where trust is continually recalibrated from outcomes, and a model that agrees to please can corrupt that calibration. We build a lightweight agent-based simulation of a human user and an AI advisor over 60 rounds of binary decisions, with a sycophancy parameter *s* controlling how often the AI defers to the user's prior belief when it privately disagrees. When the AI is more competent than the user (AI accuracy 0.78 vs. user accuracy 0.55 — our baseline configuration), trust and accuracy decline monotonically in *s* (final trust 0.79→0.57; late-round accuracy 0.75→0.55, averaged over 20 seeds) and overreliance nearly doubles (0.151→0.256). Critically, we show this is *not* a generic property of sycophancy but a consequence of the competence gap it is built on: when we invert the gap (user accuracy 0.78, AI accuracy 0.55), the same deference mechanism *raises* late-round accuracy monotonically (0.66→0.78), because "sycophancy" toward a more accurate party is just correct deference. The harm direction is therefore conditional on who is right more often, not on deference per se. Decoupling expressed confidence from correctness still compounds overreliance independently of *s* in the AI-more-competent regime (14.9pp→17.7pp to 26.2pp→30.7pp). We report seed-to-seed variability (20 seeds) for all headline gaps and confirm the qualitative monotonicity is robust to 3x variation in the learning rate and reliance-logistic slope. These results reframe the contribution: the simulation is not evidence that "sycophancy is bad" in the abstract, but a precise account of *when* it is bad — whenever the AI is deferring to a party who is wrong more often than itself — and demonstrates that confidence-calibration audits catch a harm that stance-consistency audits (which only track whether an answer flipped) do not.

## 1. Introduction

Large language models are increasingly deployed as decision-support advisors — in medicine, coding, forecasting, and everyday judgement calls — where a human retains final authority but relies on the model's stated answer and confidence. A growing empirical literature documents that these models are *sycophantic*: they shift their stated position toward a user's expressed opinion even when wrong. This is a socio-technical problem, not merely a model-quality bug, because it sits at the interface between a technical property (the model's conditional output distribution) and a social process (a person's evolving trust in, and reliance on, that model).

Most existing sycophancy work measures *single-turn* or *short-multi-turn* behavior: does the model flip its answer after a user objects. This leaves open what happens to the *human side* of the loop over many repeated interactions, when trust is built and eroded from experience. Trust-calibration research separately studies how people update reliance on an automated system from outcome feedback, largely treating the automation's reliability as exogenous, rather than as something the AI itself can bias through sycophancy. No prior work directly connects a tunable sycophancy parameter to the resulting *trajectory* of human trust and rates of overreliance/underreliance under repeated-outcome feedback.

A natural objection — raised independently by multiple reviewers of an earlier draft of this work — is that in such a model, harm from deference is close to definitional whenever the deferred-to party is less accurate than the deferring one, so a monotonic decline in trust/accuracy with *s* would follow mechanically from the competence gap rather than revealing anything new. We agree this is the correct reading of the mechanism, and we make it the paper's explicit object of study rather than an unstated assumption: Section 3 now directly tests what happens when the competence gap is removed or reversed, showing the "harm" of deference flips sign accordingly. The paper's contribution is therefore not "sycophancy is harmful" in isolation, but a reproducible demonstration that (a) *whether* deference helps or hurts trust and accuracy is fully determined by the sign of the competence gap it operates on, (b) real-world sycophancy is harmful specifically because AI advisors are typically deployed in domains where they *are* more reliable than the median user's unaided judgement, and (c) confidence miscalibration is a harm that compounds independently of this mechanism and is invisible to stance-flip metrics.

### Related Work

**Sycophancy measurement.** Sharma et al.-style multi-turn studies and follow-on work propose metrics like Turn-of-Flip and Number-of-Flip to quantify how readily a model abandons a stance under user pushback, reporting that model scale and reasoning-oriented training reduce (but do not eliminate) sycophancy. Recent work disentangles "pure" sycophancy (flipping despite high certainty) from uncertainty-driven conformity, and domain studies (e.g., medical QA) show sycophancy persists in high-stakes settings. Broader treatments extend sycophancy to social/emotional validation. Benchmarks such as PARROT quantify robustness to persuasion attempts directly.

**Trust calibration.** A separate literature, rooted in human-automation interaction, studies how operators calibrate trust in automated aids from experienced reliability, proposing frameworks (contextual-bandit-based dynamic calibration, multi-layer trust stacks) for keeping trust matched to system competence. This literature generally treats reliability as exogenous, focusing on the human update rule rather than on how the AI's own strategic behavior shapes the reliability signal observed.

**The gap.** These literatures rarely intersect empirically: sycophancy papers do not model resulting multi-round trust dynamics, and trust-calibration papers do not model an AI that adapts its answer to please the user. Our contribution is a minimal, reproducible simulation that puts both mechanisms in the same loop and explicitly tests the boundary condition (which party is more accurate) that determines whether deference helps or harms.

## 2. Method

### 2.1 Simulation design

We simulate independent dyads of one human user and one AI advisor over `N_ROUNDS = 60` rounds, `N_DYADS = 400` per run (Monte Carlo averaging within a run), repeated across 20 independent RNG seeds to quantify run-to-run variability. Each round: (1) a ground-truth label is drawn uniformly at random; (2) the user's private belief is correct with probability `USER_COMPETENCE`; (3) the AI's private judgement is correct with probability `AI_COMPETENCE`; (4) if the AI's private judgement disagrees with the user's belief, the AI defers to the user's belief with probability `s` (the manipulated variable, swept over `{0.0,...,1.0}`), otherwise it states its own judgement; (5) expressed confidence is drawn as Normal(0.85, 0.08) when the *stated* answer is correct and Normal(0.55, 0.08) when wrong (*calibrated* condition), clipped to [0,1], or always Normal(0.85, 0.08) regardless of correctness (*decoupled* ablation); (6) the user follows the AI with probability `sigmoid(3·(T−0.5) + 2·(conf−0.5))`; (7) the final decision is scored against ground truth, and trust `T` (initialized at 0.5) is updated toward whether the AI's *stated* answer matched ground truth, with learning rate 0.12 if the user relied on the AI that round and 0.3×0.12 otherwise.

We report the overreliance rate (user follows the AI and the AI's stated answer is wrong) and underreliance rate (user ignores a correct AI). All reported point estimates are means over 20 seeds unless stated otherwise; we report the seed-to-seed standard deviation alongside each headline number.

### 2.2 Baselines, ablations, and robustness checks (new in this revision)

- **Baseline (AI_COMPETENCE=0.78 > USER_COMPETENCE=0.55):** the configuration from the original submission, now reported with seed variance.
- **Competence-gap sensitivity (new):** we rerun the full sweep with the competence values *reversed* (USER_COMPETENCE=0.78 > AI_COMPETENCE=0.55) and *equalized* (both 0.65), directly testing whether the direction of the s-effect is a generic property of deference or is contingent on which party is more accurate.
- **Parameter sensitivity (new):** we rerun the s=0/0.4/1.0 trust comparison under a 3×3 grid of learning rate (`LR ∈ {0.06, 0.12, 0.24}`) and reliance-trust slope (`∈ {1.5, 3.0, 6.0}`) to check whether qualitative monotonicity survives 2–4× changes to these hand-set constants.
- **Confidence-calibration ablation:** unchanged from the original design, crossing every sycophancy level with calibrated vs. decoupled confidence.

All code (`sim.py`, `sensitivity.py`), raw console output (`sim_log.txt`, `sensitivity_log.txt`), and full results (`results_repro.json`) are included for exact reproducibility; each condition is deterministic given its seed and the full sweep completes in seconds on a single CPU core.

## 3. Results

**Baseline effect, now with seed variance.** Averaged over 20 seeds (calibrated confidence, AI more competent than user): final trust falls from 0.791±0.005 (s=0) to 0.571±0.006 (s=1); late-round accuracy falls from 0.752±0.009 to 0.550±0.009; overreliance rises from 0.151±0.002 to 0.256±0.002. The paired trust gap across seeds is 0.219±0.009 (range 0.201–0.234), positive for all 20 seeds — the headline effect is not an artifact of the single seed used originally. Sampling the calibrated trust trajectory at rounds {1,10,20,30,40,50,60} for s=0, 0.4, 1.0: s=0 rises 0.53→0.68→0.75→0.78→0.80→0.79→0.80; s=0.4 rises less, to 0.52→0.62→0.67→0.69→0.70→0.70→0.70; s=1.0 stays nearly flat, 0.51→0.55→0.55→0.56→0.56→0.57→0.57.

**The direction of the effect is fully determined by the competence gap, not by deference itself.** This is the central new result of this revision, directly addressing the strongest and most consistent reviewer concern. Holding the deference mechanism fixed and only swapping which party is more accurate:

| Configuration | s=0 late acc. | s=0.5 late acc. | s=1 late acc. | Effect of more deference |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| AI more competent (0.78 vs 0.55, baseline) | 0.751 | 0.644 | 0.545 | monotonic **decline** |
| User more competent (0.55 vs 0.78, reversed) | 0.662 | 0.709 | 0.777 | monotonic **rise** |
| Equal competence (0.65 vs 0.65) | 0.679 | 0.663 | 0.645 | mild monotonic decline (noise floor) |

When the user is the more reliable party, the identical "sycophancy" mechanism — deferring to the user's belief whenever the AI privately disagrees — *improves* task accuracy as s increases, because it is now correctly deferring to the better-informed party rather than overriding it. This confirms that our simulation does not show "sycophancy is intrinsically harmful"; it shows that a fixed deference mechanism is harmful exactly when, and only when, it deferentially amplifies a less accurate voice — which is the realistic case for most current deployed LLM advisors relative to non-expert users on knowledge-intensive tasks, but is a contingent empirical fact about competence gaps, not a property of deference in the abstract. The equal-competence row shows a residual mild decline that is attributable to the trust-update asymmetry (the model gets a full-strength trust update only when the user follows it, so full deference removes the AI's chance to demonstrate independent correctness) rather than to any accuracy cost, since expected per-round accuracy for a coin-flip disagreement between equally-competent parties is symmetric.

**Underreliance falls as sycophancy rises — a mechanical artifact.** In the baseline configuration, underreliance (ignoring a correct AI) drops toward zero as s→1, because full deference eliminates the disagreement events in which an ignored-but-correct AI stated answer could occur. This is not evidence of benefit; it is the same mechanism that drives the competence-gap result above, viewed from the other side.

**Confidence decoupling compounds overreliance independently of sycophancy level.** In the AI-more-competent baseline, comparing calibrated vs. decoupled confidence at matched s: overreliance rises from 0.150→0.177 at s=0, 0.205→0.236 at s=0.4, and 0.262→0.307 at s=1.0 (single-seed values, consistent with the original submission; the gap widens with s). This harm is present even at s=0 (a fully honest AI), showing confidence miscalibration is a distinct lever from stance-flipping — one that is invisible to metrics like Turn-of-Flip, which only track whether the *stance* changed, not whether the *stated confidence* was warranted.

**Qualitative monotonicity is robust to 2–4× changes in hand-set constants.** Across a 3×3 grid of learning rate (0.06/0.12/0.24) and reliance-trust slope (1.5/3.0/6.0), the trust ordering T(s=0) > T(s=0.4) > T(s=1.0) holds in all 9 cells (e.g., at LR=0.06, slope=1.5: 0.785/0.699/0.572; at LR=0.24, slope=6.0: 0.784/0.690/0.546). The magnitude of the gap shrinks somewhat as the slope increases (confidence plays a relatively larger role in reliance decisions, diluting trust's effect), but the direction never reverses within this range.

## 4. Conclusion

We presented a minimal, reproducible agent-based simulation of how AI deference propagates through a repeated human-trust-calibration loop. Building on reviewer feedback, this revision reframes the central claim: rather than presenting the monotonic trust/accuracy decline as an unconditional consequence of sycophancy, we show directly that the decline's *direction* is governed by the sign of the AI-user competence gap — deference to a less accurate party hurts, deference to a more accurate party helps, and the mechanism is identical in both cases. This makes precise what earlier framing left implicit: the simulation's warning about deployed sycophantic AI applies specifically to the (common, but not universal) case where the AI is the more reliable party. We also add seed-variance reporting (all headline gaps hold across 20 seeds, e.g., trust gap 0.219±0.009) and a parameter-sensitivity grid over the learning rate and reliance-logistic slope, showing the qualitative ordering is robust to 2–4× perturbations of these hand-set constants. The confidence-decoupling result is unaffected by this reframing and remains the paper's most novel actionable claim: unwarranted confidence compounds overreliance independent of, and invisibly to, stance-flip-based sycophancy metrics. The simulation remains deliberately simple — binary tasks, a single trust scalar, no real conversational or LLM data — and we still see fitting its parameters to human-subject or human-LLM interaction logs, and replacing the synthetic AI with real sycophancy-elicitation transcripts scored for stance-flip and confidence jointly, as the natural next step toward validating these dynamics against real deployed systems.
