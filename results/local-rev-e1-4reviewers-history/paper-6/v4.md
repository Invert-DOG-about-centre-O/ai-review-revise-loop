# Sycophancy Erodes the Trust It Is Meant to Preserve: An Agent-Based Simulation of Human–AI Trust Calibration Under Deference Bias

## Abstract

Sycophancy — an AI system's tendency to align its stated answer with a user's expressed belief rather than its own best judgement — is usually studied as a property of a single model response. We argue this framing misses a socio-technical dynamic: sycophancy operates inside a *repeated* human-AI loop where trust is continually recalibrated from outcomes, and a model that agrees to please can corrupt that calibration. We build a lightweight agent-based simulation of a human user and an AI advisor over 60 rounds of binary decisions, with a sycophancy parameter *s* controlling how often the AI defers to the user's prior belief when it privately disagrees. When the AI is more competent than the user (AI accuracy 0.78 vs. user accuracy 0.55 — our baseline configuration), trust and accuracy decline monotonically in *s* (final trust 0.79→0.57; late-round accuracy 0.75→0.55, averaged over 20 seeds) and overreliance nearly doubles (0.151→0.256); the paired s=0-vs-s=1 trust gap is significant across seeds (paired t-test t=106.5, p=7.6e-28). Critically, this is *not* a generic property of sycophancy but a consequence of the competence gap it operates on: inverting the gap (user 0.78, AI 0.55) makes the same deference mechanism *raise* late-round accuracy monotonically (0.66→0.78), because "sycophancy" toward a more accurate party is just correct deference — harm direction is conditional on who is right more often, not on deference per se. Decoupling expressed confidence from correctness compounds overreliance independently of *s* in the AI-more-competent regime (overreliance 0.151→0.174 at s=0, up to 0.256→0.307 at s=1.0, over 20 seeds). Under equal competence we trace a mild residual accuracy decline to a confidence-mediated selection effect — calibrated confidence lets the user implicitly aggregate information by following the AI more when it is more likely right — and, going beyond the two-endpoint test in the previous draft, we now sweep this comparison across the *full* s range: the calibrated-vs-decoupled accuracy gap shrinks smoothly and monotonically from +0.030 at s=0 to −0.001 at s=1.0, confirming the aggregation benefit degrades gradually with deference rather than only differing at the endpoints. We report seed-to-seed variability for all headline gaps and confirm the qualitative monotonicity is robust to 2–4x variation in the learning rate and reliance-logistic slope. These results reframe the contribution: not evidence that "sycophancy is bad" in the abstract, but a precise account of *when* it is bad — whenever the AI defers to a party who is wrong more often than itself — and a demonstration that confidence-calibration audits catch a harm that stance-consistency audits (which only track whether an answer flipped) do not.

## 1. Introduction

Large language models are increasingly deployed as decision-support advisors — in medicine, coding, forecasting, and everyday judgement calls — where a human retains final authority but relies on the model's stated answer and confidence. A growing empirical literature documents that these models are *sycophantic*: they shift their stated position toward a user's expressed opinion even when wrong. This is a socio-technical problem, not merely a model-quality bug, because it sits at the interface between a technical property (the model's conditional output distribution) and a social process (a person's evolving trust in, and reliance on, that model).

Most existing sycophancy work measures *single-turn* or *short-multi-turn* behavior: does the model flip its answer after a user objects. This leaves open what happens to the *human side* of the loop over many repeated interactions, when trust is built and eroded from experience. Trust-calibration research separately studies how people update reliance on an automated system from outcome feedback, largely treating the automation's reliability as exogenous, rather than as something the AI itself can bias through sycophancy. No prior work directly connects a tunable sycophancy parameter to the resulting *trajectory* of human trust and rates of overreliance/underreliance under repeated-outcome feedback.

A natural objection — raised independently by multiple reviewers across earlier drafts of this work — is that in such a model, harm from deference is close to definitional whenever the deferred-to party is less accurate than the deferring one, so a monotonic decline in trust/accuracy with *s* would follow mechanically from the competence gap rather than revealing anything new. We agree this is the correct reading of the mechanism and make it the paper's explicit object of study: Section 3 directly tests what happens when the competence gap is removed or reversed, showing the "harm" of deference flips sign accordingly. The contribution is therefore not "sycophancy is harmful" in isolation, but a reproducible demonstration that (a) *whether* deference helps or hurts trust and accuracy is fully determined by the sign of the competence gap it operates on, (b) real-world sycophancy is harmful specifically in domains where AI advisors are more reliable than the median user's unaided judgement, and (c) confidence miscalibration is a harm that compounds independently of this mechanism and is invisible to stance-flip metrics. (b) is a plausibility argument, not a validated empirical fact about deployment (Section 3). Reviewers of the previous draft also converged on one further open question: whether the confidence-mediated mechanism behind (c) held only at the two endpoints we had tested (s=0, s=1) or across the full deference range. This revision closes that gap directly (Section 3).

### Related Work

**Sycophancy measurement.** Sharma et al.-style multi-turn studies and follow-on work propose metrics like Turn-of-Flip and Number-of-Flip to quantify how readily a model abandons a stance under user pushback, reporting that model scale and reasoning-oriented training reduce (but do not eliminate) sycophancy. Recent work disentangles "pure" sycophancy (flipping despite high certainty) from uncertainty-driven conformity, and domain studies (e.g., medical QA) show sycophancy persists in high-stakes settings. Broader treatments extend sycophancy to social/emotional validation; benchmarks such as PARROT quantify robustness to persuasion attempts directly.

**Trust calibration.** A separate literature, rooted in human-automation interaction, studies how operators calibrate trust in automated aids from experienced reliability, proposing frameworks (contextual-bandit calibration, multi-layer trust stacks) for keeping trust matched to system competence — but treats reliability as exogenous, focused on the human update rule rather than on how the AI's own strategic behavior shapes the reliability signal observed.

**The gap.** These literatures rarely intersect empirically: sycophancy papers do not model resulting multi-round trust dynamics, and trust-calibration papers do not model an AI that adapts its answer to please the user. Our contribution is a minimal, reproducible simulation that puts both mechanisms in one loop and tests the boundary condition (which party is more accurate) that determines whether deference helps or harms.

## 2. Method

### 2.1 Simulation design

We simulate independent dyads of one human user and one AI advisor over `N_ROUNDS = 60` rounds, `N_DYADS = 400` per run (Monte Carlo averaging within a run), repeated across 20 independent RNG seeds to quantify run-to-run variability. Each round: (1) a ground-truth label is drawn uniformly at random; (2) the user's private belief is correct with probability `USER_COMPETENCE`; (3) the AI's private judgement is correct with probability `AI_COMPETENCE`; (4) if the AI's private judgement disagrees with the user's belief, the AI defers to the user's belief with probability `s` (the manipulated variable, swept over `{0.0,...,1.0}`), otherwise it states its own judgement; (5) expressed confidence is drawn as Normal(0.85, 0.08) when the *stated* answer is correct and Normal(0.55, 0.08) when wrong (*calibrated*), clipped to [0,1], or always Normal(0.85, 0.08) regardless of correctness (*decoupled* ablation); (6) the user follows the AI with probability `sigmoid(3·(T−0.5) + 2·(conf−0.5))`; (7) the final decision is scored against ground truth, and trust `T` (init 0.5) is updated toward whether the AI's *stated* answer matched ground truth, learning rate 0.12 if the user relied on the AI that round, 0.3×0.12 otherwise.

We report the overreliance rate (user follows the AI and the AI's stated answer is wrong) and underreliance rate (user ignores a correct AI). All reported point estimates are means over 20 seeds unless stated otherwise; we report the seed-to-seed standard deviation alongside each headline number.

### 2.2 Baselines, ablations, and robustness checks

- **Baseline (AI_COMPETENCE=0.78 > USER_COMPETENCE=0.55):** the original configuration, with seed variance and a paired t-test.
- **Competence-gap sensitivity:** full sweep with competence *reversed* (USER=0.78 > AI=0.55) and *equalized* (both 0.65).
- **Parameter sensitivity:** s=0/0.4/1.0 trust comparison under a 3×3 grid of learning rate (`LR ∈ {0.06, 0.12, 0.24}`) and reliance-trust slope (`∈ {1.5, 3.0, 6.0}`).
- **Confidence-calibration ablation:** overreliance under calibrated vs. decoupled confidence, across the same 20 seeds as the headline gap.
- **Mechanism test for the equal-competence decline, full sweep (new):** the previous draft tested the confidence-selection explanation only at s=0 and s=1; reviewers asked whether it held at intermediate deference. We now compare calibrated vs. decoupled confidence at all six swept s values (0.0–1.0, step 0.2) under equal competence.

All code (`sim.py`, `sensitivity.py`), raw output (`sim_log.txt`, `sensitivity_log.txt`), and full results (`results_repro.json`) are included; each condition is deterministic given its seed and the full sweep completes in seconds on one CPU core.

## 3. Results

**Baseline effect, with seed variance and significance.** Averaged over 20 seeds (calibrated confidence, AI more competent than user): final trust falls from 0.791±0.005 (s=0) to 0.571±0.006 (s=1); late-round accuracy falls from 0.752±0.009 to 0.550±0.009; overreliance rises from 0.151±0.002 to 0.256±0.002. The paired trust gap across seeds is 0.219±0.009 (range 0.201–0.234), positive for all 20 seeds, and a paired t-test on the seed-matched trust values confirms this is not seed noise (t=106.5, p=7.6e-28). Sampling the calibrated trust trajectory at rounds {1,10,20,30,40,50,60} for s=0, 0.4, 1.0: s=0 rises 0.53→0.68→0.75→0.78→0.80→0.79→0.80; s=0.4 rises less, to 0.52→0.62→0.67→0.69→0.70→0.70→0.70; s=1.0 stays nearly flat, 0.51→0.55→0.55→0.56→0.56→0.57→0.57.

**The direction of the effect is fully determined by the competence gap, not by deference itself.** Holding the deference mechanism fixed and only swapping which party is more accurate:

| Configuration | s=0 late acc. | s=0.5 late acc. | s=1 late acc. | Effect of more deference |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| AI more competent (0.78 vs 0.55, baseline) | 0.751 | 0.644 | 0.545 | monotonic **decline** |
| User more competent (0.55 vs 0.78, reversed) | 0.662 | 0.709 | 0.777 | monotonic **rise** |
| Equal competence (0.65 vs 0.65) | 0.679 | 0.663 | 0.645 | mild monotonic decline |

When the user is the more reliable party, the identical deference mechanism *improves* task accuracy as s increases, because it is now correctly deferring to the better-informed party. The claim that deployed LLM advisors are typically more reliable than the median user is, we acknowledge, not something this simulation can establish: it is a plausibility argument grounded in the fact that most cited sycophancy studies operate on structured tasks (medical QA, coding, closed-form QA) where published model accuracy is compared against expert or ground-truth answers, not a measured non-expert baseline. We are not aware of a study that jointly measures LLM accuracy and matched non-expert human accuracy on the same sycophancy-eliciting task, and flag that gap explicitly; our real-world relevance claim is conditional ("when this holds"), not unconditional.

**Underreliance falls as sycophancy rises — a mechanical artifact.** In the baseline configuration, underreliance (ignoring a correct AI) drops toward zero as s→1, because full deference eliminates the disagreement events in which an ignored-but-correct AI stated answer could occur. This is the same mechanism that drives the competence-gap result above, viewed from the other side, not independent evidence of benefit.

**Confidence decoupling compounds overreliance independently of sycophancy level.** Overreliance rises from 0.151±0.002→0.174±0.002 at s=0, 0.200±0.003→0.234±0.002 at s=0.4, and 0.256±0.002→0.307±0.002 at s=1.0 (calibrated → decoupled, 20-seed means). The gap widens with s and is present even at s=0 (a fully honest AI), showing confidence miscalibration is a distinct lever from stance-flipping, invisible to metrics like Turn-of-Flip, which track only whether the stance changed.

**The equal-competence residual decline is a confidence-selection effect that degrades gradually with s — now confirmed across the full sweep.** We attribute the mild accuracy decline under equal competence (0.679→0.645 as s: 0→1) to a confidence-mediated selection effect rather than the trust-update rule: at s=1, the AI's stated answer equals the user's belief every round (whenever they disagree it states the user's belief; whenever they agree its private judgement already matches), pinning accuracy at exactly `USER_COMPETENCE` (0.65) regardless of who is "followed." At s=0, the AI states its own independent judgement, and *calibrated* confidence (higher when correct) makes the user more likely to follow the AI precisely when it is more likely right — implicit information aggregation that decoupled (uninformative) confidence removes. Disabling the asymmetric trust-update rule (`weak_mult=1.0` instead of 0.3) leaves the decline essentially unchanged (0.680→0.663→0.645 vs. 0.679→0.663→0.645), ruling out the trust-update rule as the cause. The previous draft confirmed this only at the s=0 and s=1 endpoints, leaving open whether it held smoothly in between. We now sweep the calibrated-vs-decoupled accuracy gap across all six values of s: +0.030 (s=0.0), +0.023 (0.2), +0.017 (0.4), +0.010 (0.6), +0.003 (0.8), −0.001 (1.0) — a smooth, near-monotonic decay to zero, matching the prediction that deference progressively removes the disagreement events through which the aggregation benefit operates, rather than the discontinuous jump that would suggest the endpoint comparison was cherry-picked.

**Qualitative monotonicity is robust to 2–4× changes in hand-set constants.** Across a 3×3 grid of learning rate (0.06/0.12/0.24) and reliance-trust slope (1.5/3.0/6.0), the trust ordering T(s=0) > T(s=0.4) > T(s=1.0) holds in all 9 cells (e.g., at LR=0.06, slope=1.5: 0.785/0.699/0.572; at LR=0.24, slope=6.0: 0.784/0.690/0.546). The magnitude of the gap shrinks somewhat as the slope increases, but the direction never reverses within this range.

## 4. Conclusion

We presented a minimal, reproducible agent-based simulation of how AI deference propagates through a repeated human-trust-calibration loop. The central claim is that the monotonic trust/accuracy decline is not an unconditional consequence of sycophancy, but is governed by the sign of the AI-user competence gap — deference to a less accurate party hurts, deference to a more accurate party helps, via the identical mechanism, and the baseline gap is statistically robust to seed noise (paired t-test, p=7.6e-28). The premise this rests on for real-world relevance — that deployed LLM advisors are typically more reliable than the users they serve — remains a plausibility argument, not something validated here or in the cited literature; no study we are aware of jointly measures both quantities on the same task.

This revision closes the remaining methodological gap flagged by reviewers: the confidence-mediated selection effect behind the equal-competence residual decline was previously verified only at the s=0 and s=1 endpoints. We now show, across the full s sweep, that the calibrated-vs-decoupled accuracy gap shrinks smoothly from +0.030 to −0.001 as deference increases from 0 to 1 — direct evidence the mechanism operates continuously rather than as an endpoint artifact.

The confidence-decoupling result remains the paper's most novel actionable claim: unwarranted confidence compounds overreliance independent of, and invisibly to, stance-flip-based sycophancy metrics. The simulation remains deliberately simple — binary tasks, a single trust scalar, no real conversational or LLM data — and fitting its parameters to human-subject or human-LLM interaction logs, and replacing the synthetic AI with real sycophancy-elicitation transcripts scored for stance-flip and confidence jointly, remain the natural next steps. A lower-cost next step raised in review is checking whether existing sycophancy benchmarks that report both model accuracy and a human/crowdworker baseline could proxy the competence-gap premise (b); we did not locate one meeting that bar during this revision, but flag it as the most tractable near-term path to moving (b) beyond a plausibility argument.
