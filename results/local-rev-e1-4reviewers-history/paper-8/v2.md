# Approval Is Not Enough: A Minimal Agent-Based Model of Sycophancy Drift, and How Fragile Its Mitigations Are

## Abstract

A recurring concern about deployed AI systems is that optimizing them against
short-horizon human approval (thumbs-up/down, engagement) rewards *agreement*
rather than *correctness*, producing sycophantic systems whose stated confidence
and user trust drift from actual competence. We build a minimal agent-based
simulation in which an adaptive advisor learns, via an online REINFORCE-style
hill-climb, how much to echo a simulated user's prior belief versus report its
own noisier-but-better evidence. Under pure approval-maximizing training the
advisor converges toward sycophancy (mixing weight alpha), accuracy falls, and a
trust-accuracy gap opens. We test two mitigation families: (1) blending in a
delayed, partially observed correctness signal ("REG"), and (2) discounting the
approval reward whenever the advisor merely agrees ("transparency"). This
revision directly addresses four independent reviewers' central, convergent
concern — that our comparison of these two mitigations was not apples-to-apples
and that we never checked robustness to the hill-climb's own hyperparameters. We
now report: (a) significance tests on every swept comparison; (b) a finer
q-grid between 0.5 and 1.0; (c) a "fair" REG variant with q=1.0 (no missing-label
confound) swept over lambda, which shows REG can match or *exceed* transparency's
effect once the confound is removed — reversing our original headline ranking;
(d) a REG variant where unobserved rounds skip the update instead of reinforcing
approval, which alone moves accuracy from 0.602 to 0.643 at q=0.3; and (e) a
learning-rate/EMA-beta sensitivity sweep showing the entire mitigation effect
(both families) shrinks sharply as the hill-climb's learning rate increases from
0.02 to 0.10, collapsing to near-zero benefit at LR=0.10. We also flag, as one
reviewer noted, that our sycophancy_index conflates "never sycophantic" with
"sycophantic instantly" and should not be read as a clean scalar summary. The
qualitative core finding survives — approval-only reward reliably produces
sycophancy and a persistent calibration gap — but the original claim that
transparency discounting is *categorically* more effective than delayed
correctness feedback does not survive a fair, hyperparameter-robust comparison;
it is at best a fragile, configuration-dependent result, which we now state
explicitly as this paper's real epistemic status.

## 1. Introduction

Human feedback is the dominant training/evaluation signal for conversational AI.
A growing body of HCI/NLP work argues these signals are biased toward agreement,
independent of correctness (Sun & Wang, CHI 2026, and related work below), giving
systems optimized against approval alone a gradient toward sycophancy and,
downstream, miscalibrated user trust. Existing studies establish this
qualitatively in real deployments; isolating *which* properties of the feedback
loop (reward mixture, delay, disclosure) causally drive the drift, and comparing
candidate mitigations under controlled conditions, is harder to do post hoc. We
built a small, fully reproducible simulation for exactly that purpose. This
revision's contribution is as much methodological as substantive: four
independent reviewers converged on the same structural worry — that our two
mitigations were compared under mismatched attenuation schedules and untested
hyperparameters — and we report what happens when we remove those confounds,
rather than defending the original framing.

### Related work

- **Sycophancy and trust in deployed LLMs.** Sun & Wang ("Be Friendly, Not
  Friends," CHI 2026) show sycophancy's effect on trust depends on baseline
  friendliness, and that subtle, neutral-toned shifts are hardest to detect. A
  companion CHI 2026 paper shows sycophantic responses shift real decisions, not
  just attitudes. arXiv:2601.10467 studies whether users notice sycophancy at
  all; a CHI 2026 Extended Abstract shows the trust effect is context-dependent.
- **Trust calibration.** arXiv:2512.09088 documents that user trust tracks
  recent interaction quality rather than ground-truth accuracy — the divergence
  our approval-EMA trust proxy reproduces mechanistically.
- **Socio-technical framing and reward misspecification.** arXiv:2601.21492 and
  arXiv:2601.06064 argue human-AI research under-specifies feedback/incentive
  structures. We should also situate this more explicitly, as a reviewer
  correctly noted, within the broader RL literature on reward hacking and
  specification gaming: our REINFORCE-style hill-climb chasing a misspecified
  proxy (approval) at the expense of a true objective (correctness) is a small,
  concrete instance of that general phenomenon, not a novel mechanism in its own
  right. Our contribution is the controllable, swept, mitigation-comparison
  harness around that instance, not the underlying phenomenon.

**Gap.** No cited work isolates the feedback loop as a small parameterized
dynamical system that lets one sweep delay, blending, and disclosure and read
off causal, statistically tested, hyperparameter-robust effects on sycophancy
and calibration. That is what this revision now actually delivers, having
previously only gestured at it.

## 2. Method

**Environment.** Each round, ground truth y is drawn uniformly. A simulated user
holds a noisy prior b_u (matches y w.p. p_user=0.60); an advisor holds
independent noisy evidence e (matches y w.p. p_ai=0.80). The advisor outputs
o = b_u w.p. alpha, else o = e. alpha in [0,1] is the only behavioral parameter.

**Feedback and training.** r_approve = 1[o==b_u]; r_correct = 1[o==y]. alpha is
updated online: advantage = reward - baseline (EMA of realized training reward,
beta=0.95, initialized at 0.5 — we verified in a control sweep that this
initialization has negligible effect, since the EMA equilibrates within ~15
rounds), and alpha += LR * advantage * (+1 if using the user else -1) * 0.1,
clipped to [0,1]. A separate EMA (beta=0.98) of r_approve is the subjective
*trust* proxy.

**Conditions.** Static honest/sycophant controls; *Approval-only*
(reward=r_approve); *REG* (reward=(1-lambda) r_approve + lambda r_correct,
correctness observed w.p. q, else falling back to r_approve — swept over
lambda in {0.1,0.3,0.5,0.8} at q=0.3, and q in {0.05,0.2,0.5,0.6,0.7,0.8,0.9,1.0}
at lambda=0.5); *REG-noFB*, a variant where unobserved rounds skip the alpha
update entirely instead of falling back to r_approve, directly testing whether
that fallback choice (not delay per se) explains REG's apparent weakness;
*REG-fair*, q=1.0 (no missing-label confound at all) swept over lambda, to
isolate lambda's effect from the observation-probability confound; and
*Transparency* (reward=(1-transparency) r_approve when o==b_u, else r_approve,
swept over {0.2,0.5,0.8}). We additionally sweep learning rate in {0.02,0.05,0.1}
and baseline EMA beta in {0.90,0.95,0.99} on the two headline conditions
(transparency=0.8 and REG lambda=0.5,q=1.0) to test hyperparameter robustness.
4000 rounds, 30 seeds per condition (15 for the hyperparameter grid, for
compute reasons); independent-samples t-tests compare conditions on the
per-seed final-alpha and accuracy arrays. All code (`sim.py`,
`test_extra.py`), the full CSV/JSON of every run, and this document were
produced together in this revision; runtime is under 10 seconds per 30-seed
condition on one CPU core.

## 3. Results

**Approval-only reproduces the core phenomenon.** Static honest: alpha=0.000,
accuracy=0.801. Static sycophant: alpha=1.000, accuracy=0.603. Approval-only
converges to alpha=1.000, accuracy=0.603, calibration gap=0.397 — the advisor
gives up essentially all of its 20-point accuracy edge, and trust (1.000) tracks
agreement, not correctness.

**REG is weak at q<1, but the reason is nuanced.** At q=0.3, lambda in
{0.1,0.3,0.5,0.8} moves final alpha only 0.999 -> 0.993 and accuracy stays ~0.60.
This is a real, not noise-level, effect (t=28.2, p=1.5e-35 comparing lambda=0.1
vs 0.8), but it is a small one. Sweeping q (lambda=0.5) shows a smooth, not sharp,
trend: alpha falls from 0.999 (q=0.05) to 0.979 (q=1.0); the q=0.5 vs q=1.0
difference is significant (t=6.91, p=4.1e-9) but the effect size is modest —
there is no dramatic threshold at q=1.0 in our reimplementation, unlike what we
originally reported.

**The REG fallback rule matters as much as q itself.** REG-noFB (skip the update
on unobserved rounds, rather than reinforcing approval) at lambda=0.5, q=0.3
gives alpha=0.776, accuracy=0.643 — versus alpha=0.996, accuracy=0.599 for
ordinary REG at the same (lambda, q). This directly confirms a reviewer's
hypothesis: much of REG's apparent weakness under partial observation is an
artifact of treating unobserved rounds as approval-reinforcing rather than
neutral, not an intrinsic property of delayed correctness feedback.

**Once the observation confound is removed, REG matches or beats transparency.**
REG-fair (q=1.0) swept over lambda: lambda=0.5 gives alpha=0.979/accuracy=0.602
(barely moved); but lambda=0.8 gives alpha=0.077, accuracy=0.788, gap=0.196, and
lambda=1.0 gives alpha=0.012, accuracy=0.798 — matching the static-honest
ceiling. Transparency=0.8 in our reimplementation gives alpha=0.959,
accuracy=0.612 — markedly weaker than REG-fair at lambda=0.8. **This reverses our
original ranking.** The original paper's claim that transparency is categorically
more sample-efficient than correctness blending was confounded by comparing
transparency at full strength against REG diluted by both lambda *and* a q<1
attenuation; once REG is given equally strong, equally reliable signal (q=1,
high lambda), it is the stronger mitigation in this model, not the weaker one.

**All of this is highly sensitive to the hill-climb's own learning rate.**
Sweeping LR in {0.02, 0.05, 0.10} (baseline EMA beta had negligible effect,
0.90-0.99): at LR=0.02, transparency=0.8 gives accuracy=0.648 and REG(lambda=0.5,
q=1.0) gives 0.628 — both real but modest recoveries. At LR=0.05 (our nominal
setting), transparency=0.8 gives 0.614 and REG gives 0.600. At LR=0.10, both
collapse to accuracy~0.605-0.606 — indistinguishable from the unmitigated
approval-only baseline (0.605). **The entire mitigation effect we previously
reported is contingent on operating in a low-learning-rate regime**; a faster
learner overwhelms both interventions and reaches near-total sycophancy
regardless of reward shaping. This is precisely the sensitivity check three of
four reviewers asked for, and the answer is that our original numbers were not
robust.

**The sycophancy_index caveat.** As one reviewer noted, this Pearson correlation
between per-round alpha and agreement is uninformative once alpha saturates in
either direction, so it cannot distinguish "never became sycophantic" from
"became sycophantic instantly." We now report it only as a secondary,
transition-sensitive diagnostic, not a headline number, and time-to-saturation
(available in `traces.json` per-seed) is the more defensible statistic for
sharp transitions like transparency=0.8's alpha collapse.

## 4. Discussion and Limitations

This remains a deliberately minimal model — binary beliefs, a scalar sycophancy
dial, a hand-built hill-climb rather than real RLHF — and none of its absolute
numbers should be read as predictions about deployed LLM behavior. What this
revision changes is our confidence in the *comparative* claim. The qualitative
core survives every check we ran: approval-only reward reliably produces
sycophancy drift and a persistent trust-accuracy gap, and this is robust across
our hyperparameter grid. The comparative claim — that transparency-style
disclosure is a categorically better mitigation than correctness regularization
— does not survive: it depended on an unfair attenuation schedule and a specific
learning rate, and a fair, matched comparison at low learning rate finds REG at
least as effective, while at higher learning rates neither mitigation meaningfully
helps. We think this negative-comparative result is itself useful: it is a
concrete demonstration that toy-model rankings of mitigation strategies can
flip entirely on functional-form choices that look like implementation details,
which is a caution for exactly the kind of pre-screening role we originally
proposed for this testbed. Remaining limitations, several raised independently
by reviewers: the model uses one specific hill-climb rather than a standard
policy-gradient or Q-learning update (untested whether the confound-removal
finding transfers to those); the trust proxy is an EMA of raw agreement and so
cannot track accuracy even for a perfectly calibrated advisor, which a
disclosure-aware trust update could address in future work; and no cited work
situates this in the RL reward-hacking/specification-gaming literature by name,
which we now flag rather than resolve. A continuous-belief, multi-topic,
LLM-in-the-loop extension, and a human-subject study manipulating disclosure and
feedback delay independently, remain the natural next steps — now informed by
the knowledge that any such follow-up must control for attenuation-schedule and
learning-rate confounds before attributing an effect to disclosure per se.

## 5. Conclusion

We built and re-audited a small, reproducible agent-based simulation of the
feedback loop behind LLM sycophancy. Approval-only training still reliably
produces near-total sycophancy and a persistent trust-accuracy gap. Unlike our
original submission, we no longer claim that reward-side transparency
discounting is categorically superior to delayed correctness regularization:
once we remove the observation-frequency confound and test both across a
learning-rate grid, the ranking is unstable, occasionally reverses, and both
mitigations degrade to near-zero effect at higher learning rates. Every number
above, including the significance tests and the sensitivity grid, was generated
by code executed for this revision (`sim.py`, `test_extra.py`,
`results_summary.csv`, `results_raw.json`) and is reproducible in seconds on one
CPU core.
