# Approval Is Not Enough: A Minimal Agent-Based Model of Sycophancy Drift, and How Fragile Its Mitigations Are

## Abstract

A recurring concern about deployed AI systems is that optimizing them against
short-horizon human approval (thumbs-up/down, engagement) rewards *agreement*
rather than *correctness*, producing sycophantic systems whose stated confidence
and user trust drift from actual competence. We build a minimal agent-based
simulation in which an adaptive advisor learns, via an online REINFORCE-style
hill-climb, how much to echo a simulated user's prior belief versus report its
own noisier-but-better evidence. Under pure approval-maximizing training the
advisor converges toward sycophancy, accuracy falls, and a trust-accuracy gap
opens. We test two mitigation families: (1) blending in a delayed, partially
observed correctness signal ("REG"), and (2) discounting the approval reward
whenever the advisor merely agrees ("transparency"). This second revision
responds to round-2 reviewer feedback on our first re-audit. Four reviewers
independently confirmed our numbers reproduce exactly, and converged on four
remaining gaps, which we now close: (a) REG-fair (q=1.0, swept over lambda) is
now a first-class saved condition in `sim.py`/`results_summary.csv`, not a
manually reconstructed one-off; (b) we report mean +/- SD (30 seeds) and a
t-test for the REG-fair headline point against transparency, and apply a
Bonferroni correction across all six headline tests — five survive, one (the
already-flagged "REG lambda=0.5 barely moved" comparison) does not, and we say
so; (c) we actually compute time-to-saturation, which we had only gestured at:
transparency=0.8 saturates (alpha>=0.95) in all 30 seeds by round ~3357,
ordinary REG(lambda=0.5,q=1) saturates faster (~2448) in all 30 seeds, and
REG-fair(lambda=0.8,q=1) *never* saturates in any of 30 seeds — a third,
independent line of evidence for the ranking reversal; (d) we add real citations
to the RL reward-hacking/specification-gaming literature we had previously only
promised to cite. We also state plainly, per one reviewer's finding, that we
cannot forensically diagnose why several headline numbers shifted between our
original submission and the first revision, because we did not preserve a
versioned snapshot of the original code — an honest gap we now close by
treating `sim.py` as an artifact to snapshot at every revision going forward.
The qualitative core finding survives every check across two revisions:
approval-only reward reliably produces sycophancy and a persistent calibration
gap. The comparative claim about which mitigation is better remains what our
first revision found — fragile, sign-flipping across (lambda, q, learning
rate) — and that fragility is now itself independently corroborated by the
saturation-timing analysis.

## 1. Introduction

Human feedback is the dominant training/evaluation signal for conversational AI.
A growing body of HCI/NLP work argues these signals are biased toward agreement,
independent of correctness (Sun & Wang, CHI 2026, and related work below), giving
systems optimized against approval alone a gradient toward sycophancy and,
downstream, miscalibrated user trust. Isolating *which* properties of the
feedback loop (reward mixture, delay, disclosure) causally drive the drift, and
comparing candidate mitigations under controlled conditions, is harder to do
post hoc than in a small, fully reproducible simulation built for exactly that
purpose. Our first revision found that our original headline claim — that
reward-side transparency discounting categorically beats delayed correctness
regularization — was confounded, and reversed it once the confound was removed.
This revision is a second, narrower pass: round-2 reviewers reproduced every
number but flagged that the reversed finding itself rested on a single,
unversioned condition and uncorrected multiple comparisons. We fix both, and
add an independent corroborating measurement (saturation timing) rather than
just re-running the same comparison more carefully.

### Related work

- **Sycophancy and trust in deployed LLMs.** Sun & Wang ("Be Friendly, Not
  Friends," CHI 2026) show sycophancy's effect on trust depends on baseline
  friendliness, and that subtle, neutral-toned shifts are hardest to detect. A
  companion CHI 2026 paper shows sycophantic responses shift real decisions, not
  just attitudes. arXiv:2601.10467 studies whether users notice sycophancy at
  all; a CHI 2026 Extended Abstract shows the trust effect is context-dependent.
- **Trust calibration.** arXiv:2512.09088 documents that user trust tracks
  recent interaction quality rather than ground-truth accuracy — the divergence
  our approval-EMA trust proxy reproduces mechanistically.
- **Socio-technical framing and reward misspecification.** arXiv:2601.21492 and
  arXiv:2601.06064 argue human-AI research under-specifies feedback/incentive
  structures. We now also cite the RL literature on the general phenomenon our
  hill-climb instantiates: Amodei et al. (2016), "Concrete Problems in AI
  Safety" (arXiv:1606.06565), frame reward hacking as a core safety failure
  mode; Skalse et al. (2022), "Defining and Characterizing Reward Hacking"
  (NeurIPS), formalize when a proxy reward's optimum diverges from the true
  objective's, which is precisely the approval-vs-correctness divergence we
  simulate; Pan et al. (2022), "The Effects of Reward Misspecification"
  (ICLR), empirically show mitigation effectiveness against a misspecified
  proxy is itself sensitive to optimizer and training regime — the same
  qualitative conclusion our learning-rate sweep reaches independently. Our
  contribution remains the controllable, swept, mitigation-comparison harness
  around this general phenomenon, not the phenomenon itself.

**Gap.** No cited work isolates the feedback loop as a small parameterized
dynamical system that lets one sweep delay, blending, and disclosure and read
off causal, statistically tested, hyperparameter-robust effects on sycophancy
and calibration, with every comparison independently corroborated by more than
one statistic. That is what this revision delivers.

## 2. Method

**Environment.** Each round, ground truth y is drawn uniformly. A simulated user
holds a noisy prior b_u (matches y w.p. p_user=0.60); an advisor holds
independent noisy evidence e (matches y w.p. p_ai=0.80). The advisor outputs
o = b_u w.p. alpha, else o = e. alpha in [0,1] is the only behavioral parameter.

**Feedback and training.** r_approve = 1[o==b_u]; r_correct = 1[o==y]. alpha is
updated online: advantage = reward - baseline (EMA of realized training reward,
beta=0.95, initialized at 0.5), and alpha += LR * advantage * (+1 if using the
user else -1) * 0.1, clipped to [0,1]. A separate EMA (beta=0.98) of r_approve
is the subjective *trust* proxy.

**Conditions.** Static honest/sycophant controls; *Approval-only*
(reward=r_approve); *REG* (reward=(1-lambda) r_approve + lambda r_correct,
correctness observed w.p. q, else falling back to r_approve — swept over
lambda in {0.1,0.3,0.5,0.8} at q=0.3, and q in {0.05,...,1.0} at lambda=0.5);
*REG-noFB* (unobserved rounds skip the alpha update instead of falling back to
r_approve); ***REG-fair*** (q=1.0, no missing-label confound, swept over
lambda in {0.5,0.8,1.0}) — **now saved as an explicit named condition in
`sim.py`'s `main()` and written to `results_summary.csv`/`results_raw.json`
alongside every other condition**, resolving a reviewer's point that this was
previously reconstructed by hand; and *Transparency* (reward=(1-transparency)
r_approve when o==b_u, else r_approve, swept over {0.2,0.5,0.8}). We sweep
learning rate in {0.02,0.05,0.1} and baseline EMA beta in {0.90,0.95,0.99} on
transparency=0.8 and REG(lambda=0.5,q=1.0). 4000 rounds, 30 seeds per
condition (15 for the hyperparameter grid). Independent-samples t-tests
compare per-seed final-alpha/accuracy arrays; we now additionally report a
Bonferroni correction across the six headline comparisons in `test_extra.py`,
and a per-seed **time-to-saturation** statistic (first round each seed's alpha
trace reaches >=0.95, or "never" within 4000 rounds) via a new
`test_saturation.py`, which reuses `run_one`'s existing per-round alpha trace
(now exposed via `return_trace=True`) rather than adding a second simulator.
All code, the full CSV/JSON of every run, and this document were produced
together for this revision; runtime is under 15 seconds total on one CPU core.

## 3. Results

| Condition | alpha | accuracy | gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Static honest | 0.000 | 0.801 | 0.245 |
| Static sycophant | 1.000 | 0.603 | 0.397 |
| Approval-only | 1.000 | 0.603 | 0.397 |
| REG lam=0.5,q=0.3 | 0.996 | 0.599 | 0.399 |
| REG lam=0.5,q=1.0 | 0.979 | 0.602 | 0.389 |
| REG-noFB lam=0.5,q=0.3 | 0.776 | 0.643 | 0.258 |
| Transparency 0.8 | 0.959 | 0.612 | 0.371 |
| **REG-fair lam=0.8,q=1.0** | **0.077** | **0.788** | **0.196** |
| REG-fair lam=1.0,q=1.0 | 0.012 | 0.798 | 0.235 |

**Approval-only reproduces the core phenomenon.** The advisor gives up
essentially all of its 20-point accuracy edge (0.801 -> 0.603), and trust
(1.000) tracks agreement, not correctness.

**REG is weak at q<1 but the fallback rule, not delay itself, is why.**
Lambda sweep at q=0.3: t=28.2, p=1.5e-35 (real, but alpha only moves
0.999->0.993). REG-noFB at lambda=0.5,q=0.3 (skip update instead of
reinforcing approval on unobserved rounds) gives alpha=0.776, accuracy=0.643 —
versus 0.996/0.599 for ordinary REG at the same (lambda,q), confirming a
reviewer's hypothesis directly.

**REG-fair now beats transparency on three independent statistics, not one.**
(1) *Point estimates with variance*: REG-fair lambda=0.8,q=1.0 gives
alpha=0.077+/-0.038, accuracy=0.788+/-0.015 (n=30) versus transparency=0.8's
alpha=0.959, accuracy=0.612. (2) *Significance*: t=-123.8, p=5.5e-72 (alpha)
and t=37.4, p=2.8e-42 (accuracy) — both survive Bonferroni correction across
all six headline tests at the corrected threshold of p<0.0083. (3) *Saturation
timing*, computed for the first time this revision: transparency=0.8 collapses
to alpha>=0.95 in all 30 seeds (mean round ~3357); REG-fair(lambda=0.8,q=1.0)
never reaches alpha>=0.95 in any of 30 seeds within 4000 rounds. This is
independent corroboration — not a re-statement of the same comparison — that
REG-fair suppresses sycophancy more durably, not just on average. One weaker
comparison (transparency=0.8 vs. ordinary REG lambda=0.5,q=1.0 on accuracy,
t=2.04, p=0.046) does **not** survive Bonferroni correction; this is the
already-flagged "lambda=0.5 barely moved" case, not the lambda=0.8 reversal
that carries the paper's claim, so the correction does not change our
conclusion, but we report the failure rather than omit it.

**Both mitigations still collapse at high learning rate.** At LR=0.10, both
transparency=0.8 and REG(lambda=0.5,q=1.0) fall to accuracy~0.605-0.606,
indistinguishable from approval-only's 0.605. Interestingly, REG(lambda=0.5,
q=1.0) is essentially flat (accuracy 0.600-0.628) across the entire LR grid —
it was never strong, at this lambda, regardless of learning rate. Baseline
EMA beta (0.90-0.99) has negligible effect throughout.

**We cannot forensically diagnose the v1-vs-v1-revision number shift.** A
round-2 reviewer noted several headline alpha/accuracy values changed
substantially between our original submission and our first revision (e.g.
REG lambda=0.5,q=1.0's alpha moved from 0.567 to 0.979) and asked what changed
in the code. We did not keep a versioned snapshot of the original `sim.py`, so
we cannot point to the specific line. This is itself a methodological lesson
we did not anticipate needing: for a paper whose entire epistemic weight rests
on "the numbers are whatever the current code produces," failing to snapshot
code per revision makes any cross-revision claim about *why* numbers moved
unverifiable after the fact. We flag this rather than speculate, and commit to
archiving `sim.py` at each future revision so this gap cannot recur.

**Sycophancy_index remains a secondary diagnostic**, not a headline number, for
the reason given previously (it is uninformative once alpha saturates in
either direction). Time-to-saturation, now actually computed rather than
gestured at, is the more defensible statistic for sharp transitions and is
reported above.

## 4. Discussion and Limitations

This remains a deliberately minimal model — binary beliefs, a scalar
sycophancy dial, a hand-built hill-climb rather than real RLHF — and none of
its absolute numbers should be read as predictions about deployed LLM
behavior. The qualitative core survives every check across two revisions:
approval-only reward reliably produces sycophancy drift and a persistent
trust-accuracy gap. The comparative claim is now backed by three independent
statistics (point estimate + variance, significance with multiple-comparisons
correction, and saturation timing) all pointing the same direction — REG,
given an equally strong and fully observed signal, durably outperforms
transparency discounting in this model — while remaining fragile to learning
rate and to which lambda is chosen for REG itself. We think the negative
comparative result from our first revision, now more rigorously supported, is
itself useful: a demonstration that toy-model mitigation rankings can flip on
functional-form choices that look like implementation details, and that a
single point-estimate comparison is not enough to trust such a flip — which is
exactly the standard round-2 reviewers held us to. Remaining limitations: the
model uses one specific hill-climb rather than a standard policy-gradient or
Q-learning update, and Pan et al. (2022)'s finding that misspecification
mitigation is optimizer-sensitive suggests our confound-removal finding may
not transfer to other optimizers, untested here; the trust proxy is an EMA of
raw agreement and so cannot track accuracy even for a perfectly calibrated
advisor; we still compare REG and transparency at nominally matched parameter
values (lambda=0.8 vs. transparency=0.8) that are not the same units of
"signal strength," a residual apples-to-oranges concern one reviewer raised
that a variance-explained normalization could resolve in future work; and we
did not test whether the reversal depends on the chosen accuracy gap between
user and advisor (p_user=0.60 vs. p_ai=0.80). A continuous-belief, multi-topic,
LLM-in-the-loop extension, and a human-subject study manipulating disclosure
and feedback delay independently, remain the natural next steps.

## 5. Conclusion

Across two rounds of revision, this small, reproducible simulation's core
finding has not moved: approval-only training reliably produces near-total
sycophancy and a persistent trust-accuracy gap. What has moved, twice, is our
confidence in which mitigation is better and how strongly we can state it.
Round 1 found the original ranking (transparency beats REG) was confounded and
reversed it. Round 2 reviewers asked us to back that reversal with saved,
versioned code, corrected significance tests, and a second, independent
measurement — we did all three, and REG-fair still wins on every one of them,
while we are now also explicit about which single weaker comparison does not
survive correction, and honest that we cannot explain an earlier, unversioned
number shift. Every number above was generated by code executed for this
revision (`sim.py`, `test_extra.py`, `test_saturation.py`,
`results_summary.csv`, `results_raw.json`) and is reproducible in under 15
seconds on one CPU core.
