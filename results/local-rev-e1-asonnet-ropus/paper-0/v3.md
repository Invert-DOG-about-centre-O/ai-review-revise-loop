# Recommender Policy, Not Individual Open-Mindedness, Drives Filter-Bubble Formation — Under What Conditions? An Agent-Based Simulation Study

## Abstract

Concerns that engagement-optimizing recommender systems fragment public opinion rest on observational data (no causal isolation) or small human-subject studies. We build a lightweight, closed-loop agent-based simulation — agents with scalar opinions interacting over many rounds with a content pool the population itself generates — and compare three recommender policies (random, engagement-maximizing, epsilon-greedy diversity-aware). Engagement-maximization roughly doubles final opinion variance vs. random (0.330 vs 0.162) and raises extreme-opinion share from 0.3% to 29.1%; a modest diversity injection (epsilon=0.3) cuts extremism to 1.2% at a bounded engagement cost. Our prior revision showed individual open-mindedness (bounded-confidence tolerance) is inert under dense content pools and only binds once the pool shrinks to the order of the per-round slot count. This revision runs three further stress tests a reviewer asked for. (1) **Homophily**: replacing our earlier *random*-following network with a *homophilous* one (agents follow the 20 others closest to their own initial opinion) does not restore the recommender-driven ranking — instead it pushes extremism to ~30% under *all three* policies alike (random, engagement-max, diversity all land at 0.30–0.31), because network segregation alone, independent of recommender choice, is enough to trap agents in like-minded content. (2) **Backfire strength**: tripling the reactance parameters at the pool sizes where the confidence bound becomes causally active does not reverse the sign of "wider tolerance lowers variance" — it only amplifies the low-tolerance variance, confirming the sign is a robust feature of the assimilation/backfire asymmetry, not an artifact of a fragile parameter choice. (3) **Engagement-function shape**: the extremism ranking we previously called robust across proximity- and novelty-seeking engagement functions in fact *inverts* under a third, more extreme shape — flat (near distance-independent) engagement — where engagement-max becomes the *least* polarizing policy (30% extremism) while random and diversity policies produce 99.7% and 85.4% extremism respectively, because uniform-high engagement combined with wide-distance recommendations triggers pervasive backfire. We release all code, logs, and numeric results, including all new robustness experiments.

## 1. Introduction

Recommender systems that optimize for engagement can amplify content homogeneity and shift opinions over repeated exposure (Pariser's "filter bubble" hypothesis; Bakshy et al. 2015; Cinelli et al. 2021). A related literature on LLM sycophancy studies how AI systems that adapt to a user's expressed views affect trust and decisions in single- or few-turn conversational settings. Both share a structural concern: an AI system that adapts to what a user already believes can entrench rather than inform. We treat sycophancy only as motivating analogy here — our simulation has no LLM or conversational mechanism, and we do not claim to test it directly (see Limitations).

**Gap.** Human-subject studies of these feedback loops are typically short-horizon; observational platform studies cannot manipulate the recommender's policy or cleanly decompose *which* mechanism — selection policy vs. individual openness to disagreement — drives observed polarization. Agent-based simulation allows both, run long enough to observe compounding effects.

**Contribution.** We implement a minimal closed-loop simulation (agents, a self-replenishing content pool, three recommender policies, a bounded-confidence update rule with reactance) to ask how much filter-bubble formation is attributable to the recommender's exposure policy versus individual open-mindedness. This revision goes further than reporting a robust ranking: we actively try to break it along three axes reviewers identified — network homophily, reactance strength, and engagement-function shape — and report where it holds and where it does not.

### Related Work

- **Filter bubbles and echo chambers.** Pariser (2011); Bakshy et al. (2015, *Science*) found algorithmic ranking modestly reduces cross-cutting exposure, smaller than individual choice effects; Cinelli et al. (2021, *PNAS*) compared echo-chamber structure across platforms. Our simulation manipulates the recommender directly, which these observational studies cannot.
- **Opinion dynamics.** Hegselmann & Krause (2002) and Deffuant et al. (2000) introduced bounded-confidence models — the core mechanism we adopt, here under recommender-mediated rather than random pairwise selection.
- **LLM sycophancy.** CHI 2026 work and arXiv work (2025–2026) study stance-adaptive AI in short interactions; we flag this as a structurally related long-horizon complement, not a mechanism we implement.
- **Overreliance/calibration.** "Measuring and mitigating overreliance..." (2025) studies trust miscalibration; distinct from our population-level selection question.

## 2. Method

**Population and content.** N=300 agents each have opinion $o_i \in [-1,1]$, initialized i.i.d. Uniform(-1,1). A content pool (capped at 3000 items) starts with $3N$ items drawn from the same distribution.

**Round structure (T=150 rounds).** Each round: (1) *Recommendation* — select $k=5$ items per agent under **random**, **engagement-maximizing** (nearest opinion-distance), or **diversity-aware epsilon-greedy** ($(1-\epsilon)$ nearest + $\epsilon$ random; main results use $\epsilon=0.3$). (2) *Engagement* — item at distance $d$ engaged with probability $p=0.9\exp(-d^2/(2\sigma^2))$, $\sigma=0.4$ by default ("proximity"). (3) *Opinion update* — if $|d|\le$ confidence bound (default 0.5), assimilate $o_i \mathrel{+}= 0.15d$; else with probability 0.15 (backfire_prob), backfire $o_i \mathrel{-}= 0.10d$ (backfire_rate); clip to $[-1,1]$. (4) *Content generation* — 5% of agents post at their current opinion, closing the loop.

**Metrics** (mean over final 20% of rounds): opinion variance, extremism share ($|o_i|>0.7$), engagement rate, diversity consumed.

**Experiments carried over:** (1) policy comparison, 8 seeds; (2) confidence-bound ablation, 5 seeds; (3) epsilon sweep, 5 seeds; (4) pool-density × confidence-bound sweep tracking nearest-recommended-item distance, 4 seeds; (5) novelty-seeking engagement function ($p=0.9\exp(-(d-0.5)^2/2\sigma^2)$), 5 seeds; (6) random-following network (degree 20, local history), 4 seeds.

**New this revision**, all reusing the existing simulation core: (7) `sim_homophily.py` — identical to the network variant except each agent follows the 20 *closest-initial-opinion* others instead of a uniformly random 20, 4 seeds. (8) `sim_backfire.py` — re-runs the pool-size-8/20 confidence-bound ablation (where the bound was shown to bind) under baseline backfire (prob=0.15, rate=0.10) vs. tripled backfire (prob=0.45, rate=0.35), 4 seeds. (9) `sim_flat_engagement.py` — re-runs the full policy comparison with engagement_sigma=5.0 (engagement probability nearly flat over the entire $[-1,1]$ opinion range), 5 seeds. Combined new-experiment runtime this session: under one minute.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Policy comparison (mean ± std, 8 seeds)

| Policy | Variance | Extremism | Engagement rate | Diversity consumed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Random | 0.162 ± 0.074 | 0.003 ± 0.005 | 0.493 ± 0.061 | 0.206 ± 0.019 |
| Engagement-max | 0.330 ± 0.008 | 0.291 ± 0.018 | 0.901 ± 0.001 | 0.000 ± 0.000 |
| Diversity (ε=0.3) | 0.212 ± 0.016 | 0.012 ± 0.005 | 0.807 ± 0.002 | 0.028 ± 0.001 |

Engagement-maximization produces ~2x the variance of random exposure and pushes ~29% of the population to extremes vs. 0.3%, while also achieving the highest raw engagement rate. The diversity policy recovers most of the reduction (1.2% extremism) at ~10 points of engagement cost.

### 3.2 Confidence-bound ablation and pool-density dependence

The bound is inert (var=0.333 regardless of tolerance) once the pool is dense (≥100 items); it only becomes causally active once pool size shrinks to roughly the recommendation slot count ($k=5$, transition between pool sizes 100 and 20), at which point wider tolerance *lowers* variance (assimilation dominates over backfire):

| Initial pool size | Nearest dist (cb=0.2) | Variance, cb=0.2 | Variance, cb=0.5 | Variance, cb=0.8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 900 | 0.0006 | 0.332 | 0.332 | 0.332 |
| 100 | 0.0050 | 0.333 | 0.333 | 0.333 |
| 20 | 0.030 | 0.360 | 0.261 | 0.261 |
| 8 | 0.081 | 0.423 | 0.342 | 0.277 |
| 5 | 0.175 | 0.391 | 0.418 | 0.387 |

**New — is this sign robust to backfire strength?** A reviewer asked whether stronger reactance could flip "wider tolerance lowers variance" into the opposite. We tripled both backfire parameters (prob 0.15→0.45, rate 0.10→0.35) at pool sizes 20 and 8:

| Pool size | Backfire setting | cb=0.2 | cb=0.5 | cb=0.8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 20 | baseline | 0.360 | 0.261 | 0.261 |
| 20 | tripled | 0.403 | 0.261 | 0.261 |
| 8 | baseline | 0.423 | 0.342 | 0.277 |
| 8 | tripled | 0.753 | 0.449 | 0.287 |

The monotone-decreasing shape (wider tolerance → lower variance) holds under both settings; tripling backfire only raises the low-tolerance variance further (0.42→0.75 at pool=8, cb=0.2) and leaves cb=0.8 nearly unchanged (backfire rarely triggers once almost everything falls inside a wide bound). **The sign is not a fragile artifact of the specific 0.15/0.10 parameters** — it reflects the assimilation/backfire asymmetry itself, and stronger reactance amplifies rather than reverses it.

### 3.3 Epsilon sweep (diversity policy, 5 seeds)

| ε | Variance | Engagement rate |
|---|---|---|
| 0.0 (=engagement) | 0.333 ± 0.008 | 0.901 ± 0.001 |
| 0.2 | 0.211 ± 0.020 | 0.807 ± 0.002 |
| 0.5 | 0.178 ± 0.046 | 0.639 ± 0.009 |
| 1.0 (=random) | 0.153 ± 0.090 | 0.577 ± 0.045 |

A small diversity budget (ε=0.2) already cuts variance 37% relative to ε=0 at modest engagement cost — a steep, front-loaded dose-response with diminishing returns thereafter.

### 3.4 Robustness to the engagement-function assumption — including a flip

We previously reported that a *novelty-seeking* engagement function (peak at nonzero distance, $d_0=0.5$) preserves the extremism ranking (engagement-max worst, diversity best) while reversing the variance ranking:

| Policy | Variance (novelty) | Extremism (novelty) |
|---|---|---|
| Random | 0.360 ± 0.040 | 0.165 ± 0.067 |
| Engagement-max | 0.333 ± 0.008 | 0.301 ± 0.012 |
| Diversity | 0.327 ± 0.019 | 0.125 ± 0.027 |

A reviewer then asked whether *any* engagement-function shape could flip the extremism ranking itself, not just variance. We tested an extreme case — **flat engagement** (probability nearly independent of distance, engagement_sigma=5.0 vs. the 0.4 baseline, so engagement is ≈0.85–0.9 at essentially every distance in $[-1,1]$):

| Policy | Variance (flat) | Extremism (flat) |
|---|---|---|
| Random | 0.869 ± 0.021 | 0.997 ± 0.003 |
| Engagement-max | 0.333 ± 0.008 | 0.300 ± 0.012 |
| Diversity | 0.711 ± 0.025 | 0.854 ± 0.022 |

**It flips completely.** Engagement-max is now the *least* polarizing policy by a wide margin (30% extremism vs. 85–99.7%). The mechanism: once engagement no longer depends on distance, random and diversity policies expose agents to far-away content at the same high engagement rate as near content, and every such exposure has a 15% chance of backfire — pushing agents outward almost every round. Engagement-max, by construction, still only ever recommends near items, so it is largely insulated from backfire regardless of the engagement function driving whether those near items get engaged. **The extremism ranking we called "the more robust indicator" in the prior revision is therefore not universally robust — it holds under engagement functions that are monotone-decreasing or unimodal-away-from-zero in distance, but inverts once engagement becomes distance-*insensitive*, because insensitivity is exactly what erases engagement-max's structural advantage (never showing far content) while exposing the other policies' occasional far picks to backfire at full rate.** We revise our recommendation: neither variance nor extremism is a shape-invariant metric; both must be reported alongside the assumed engagement function.

### 3.5 Robustness to network structure — random vs. homophilous following

Our previous revision showed a *random* degree-20 following network shrinks the policy effect by roughly an order of magnitude and destabilizes it (large cross-seed variance):

| Policy | Variance (random-follow) | Extremism (random-follow) |
|---|---|---|
| Random | 0.245 ± 0.017 | 0.004 ± 0.005 |
| Engagement-max | 0.225 ± 0.060 | 0.044 ± 0.043 |
| Diversity | 0.235 ± 0.030 | 0.001 ± 0.001 |

A reviewer asked whether the recommender-driven ranking would return under a more realistic *homophilous* following graph (agents follow others who already agree with them), since real platforms' social graphs are homophilous, not random. We re-ran the network variant with each agent following the 20 agents closest to it in *initial* opinion:

| Policy | Variance (homophily) | Extremism (homophily) |
|---|---|---|
| Random | 0.309 ± 0.011 | 0.303 ± 0.018 |
| Engagement-max | 0.329 ± 0.009 | 0.308 ± 0.020 |
| Diversity | 0.318 ± 0.010 | 0.315 ± 0.023 |

**Homophily does not restore the recommender-driven ranking — it swamps it.** Extremism jumps to ~30% under *all three* policies alike, and the gap between policies (0.5–1.2 pp in extremism) is now far smaller than the cross-seed noise band, essentially collapsing the ranking rather than reviving it. The mechanism is structural, not algorithmic: once an agent's entire visible world is 20 people who already agreed with it at t=0, the network itself performs the sorting a biased recommender would have done, and the recommender's marginal choice among those 20 already-similar neighbors barely matters. This is a different, and in some ways more concerning, failure of our original ranking claim than the random-network result: there, the recommender's advantage was diluted toward zero; here, extremism is high regardless of recommender policy, meaning **network structure alone can be the dominant driver**, with recommender policy a second-order effect once neighborhoods are opinion-homogeneous to begin with.

### 3.6 Limitations

This remains a stylized, single-dimension model without validated behavioral calibration; the policy ranking and epsilon dose-response, not the specific numeric values, are the intended contribution. Both network experiments use small degree (20), few seeds (4), and simplified following rules (uniformly random or similarity-at-t=0 only, no dynamic re-following); real platforms' graphs are far richer. The flat-engagement condition is a deliberately extreme probe, not a claim that real engagement functions are distance-independent — it establishes an existence result (the ranking is not shape-invariant) rather than an empirical estimate of how likely a flip is in practice. We had no LLM API access in this compute-constrained setting, so the sycophancy connection remains an analogy, not a tested mechanism.

## 4. Conclusion

Engagement-maximizing recommendation substantially increases polarization relative to random exposure in our baseline closed-loop model, and a lightweight diversity injection recovers most of the lost diversity cheaply. But this revision's three new stress tests each qualify that headline further: the "wider tolerance lowers variance" sign is robust to backfire strength (a genuine, non-fragile finding), while both the network structure and the engagement-function shape can overturn or swamp the core policy ranking — homophilous following collapses the ranking by making network position the dominant driver instead of recommender choice, and flat engagement inverts the extremism ranking entirely by removing engagement-max's structural insulation from backfire. We no longer state "recommender policy drives filter-bubble formation" as a general claim; we state it as conditional on a dense global pool (or non-homophilous network) and an engagement function with meaningful distance-dependence — conditions we believe hold for many but not all real platform/audience combinations, and which future empirical work should check directly rather than assume. Future work should measure real engagement-distance curves and real following-graph homophily to locate actual platforms within this parameter space, rather than treating the stylized baseline as generalizable by default.
