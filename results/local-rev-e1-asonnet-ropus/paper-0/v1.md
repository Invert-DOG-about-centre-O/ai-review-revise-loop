# Recommender Policy, Not Individual Open-Mindedness, Drives Filter-Bubble Formation: An Agent-Based Simulation Study

## Abstract

Concerns that engagement-optimizing recommender systems fragment public opinion are central to the socio-technical AI literature, but most empirical evidence comes from either observational platform data (which cannot isolate the recommender's causal role) or human-subject experiments (which are small-scale and costly). We build a lightweight, fully reproducible agent-based simulation in which a population of agents with scalar opinions interacts over many rounds with a content pool that is itself generated by the population, closing the recommendation-exposure-opinion feedback loop. We compare three recommender policies — random, pure engagement-maximizing, and an epsilon-greedy diversity-aware policy — and run two ablations: the width of agents' bounded-confidence tolerance ("open-mindedness"), and the diversity budget epsilon. Pure engagement-maximization roughly doubles final opinion variance relative to random recommendation (0.330 vs 0.162) and raises the share of extreme opinions from 0.3% to 29.1%, while a modest diversity injection (epsilon=0.3) cuts extremism back to 1.2% at a bounded cost in engagement (80.7% vs 90.1%). Counter-intuitively, varying agents' bounded-confidence tolerance from narrow (0.2) to wide (0.8) produces *zero* change in outcomes under the engagement-maximizing policy, because that policy converges to recommending content essentially at distance zero from each agent's current opinion — the tolerance parameter never binds. This suggests that, at least in this class of models, interventions targeting individual receptiveness to disagreeing content are unlikely to counteract polarization on their own; the recommender's exposure policy is the load-bearing variable. We release the full simulation code, logs, and numeric results for inspection.

## 1. Introduction

The socio-technical AI literature has extensively documented that recommender systems, by optimizing for engagement, can amplify homogeneity in the content users consume and, over repeated exposure, shift the opinions users hold (Pariser's "filter bubble" hypothesis; Bakshy et al. 2015 on Facebook exposure diversity; Cinelli et al. 2021 on echo chambers across platforms). Separately, a large and growing body of work studies how large language model (LLM) behaviors such as sycophancy — excessive agreement with a user regardless of accuracy — shape user trust and downstream decisions (e.g., recent CHI 2026 work "Be Friendly, Not Friends: How LLM Sycophancy Shapes User Trust," and "Does Sycophancy Change Decisions? Effect of LLM Sycophancy on AI-Assisted Decision-Making"). Both literatures share a common structural concern: an AI system that adapts to what a user already believes or wants to hear can create a feedback loop that entrenches rather than informs.

**Gap.** Much of the human-subject literature on these feedback loops necessarily studies short time horizons (a single session or a handful of interactions) because long-horizon, many-round studies with real users are expensive. Meanwhile, large-scale observational studies of real platforms cannot experimentally manipulate the recommender's policy to establish causality, and cannot easily decompose *which* mechanism — the recommender's selection policy, versus users' own psychological openness to disagreement — is responsible for observed polarization. Agent-based simulation is a natural complementary method precisely because it allows both experimental manipulation of the recommender policy and clean ablation of individual-level parameters (like tolerance for disagreeing content) that are difficult to manipulate experimentally in humans, while running long enough (hundreds of rounds) to observe compounding feedback effects.

**Contribution.** We implement a minimal but closed-loop simulation: (1) agents with scalar opinions, (2) a self-replenishing content pool generated by the same agents (so the recommender's future choices depend on past population opinions — a genuine feedback loop, not a static corpus), (3) three recommender policies, and (4) a bounded-confidence opinion update rule with a reactance ("backfire") term, drawn from the opinion-dynamics literature (Hegselmann & Krause 2002; Deffuant et al. 2000). We use this testbed to ask a specific, previously under-isolated empirical question: when a filter-bubble effect is observed, how much of it is attributable to the recommender's exposure policy versus the population's individual-level open-mindedness? We find the effect is almost entirely attributable to the recommender policy in our model, a finding with direct relevance to socio-technical design debates about whether interventions should target platform ranking algorithms or user-facing "media literacy" / open-mindedness interventions.

### Related Work

- **Filter bubbles and echo chambers.** Pariser (2011) coined the "filter bubble" concern; Bakshy et al. (2015, *Science*) found algorithmic ranking modestly reduces exposure to cross-cutting content on Facebook, smaller than individual choice effects; Cinelli et al. (2021, *PNAS*) compared echo-chamber structure across platforms. Our simulation lets us manipulate the recommender in ways these observational studies cannot.
- **Opinion dynamics models.** Hegselmann & Krause (2002) and Deffuant et al. (2000) introduced bounded-confidence models where agents only update toward opinions within a tolerance window — the core mechanism we adopt and then subject to recommender-mediated selection rather than uniform-random pairwise interaction.
- **LLM sycophancy and trust.** Recent CHI 2026 papers ("Be Friendly, Not Friends"; "Does Sycophancy Change Decisions?") and arXiv work (e.g., "Sycophancy Is Not One Thing," 2025; "The Hidden Cost of Contextual Sycophancy," 2026) study how AI systems that adapt their stance to a user's expressed views affect trust and decisions in single- or few-turn interactions. Our long-horizon simulation is a structural complement: it asks what happens under repeated, compounding exposure to opinion-adaptive content selection rather than a single conversational turn.
- **Overreliance and calibration.** Work such as "Measuring and mitigating overreliance to build human-compatible AI" (2025) studies human trust miscalibration toward AI outputs; our study is not about trust calibration per se but about the compounding population-level consequence of engagement-driven content selection, a related but distinct socio-technical failure mode.

## 2. Method

**Population and content.** N=300 agents each have a scalar opinion $o_i \in [-1, 1]$, initialized i.i.d. Uniform(-1, 1). A content pool (capped at 3000 items) is initialized with $3N$ items drawn from the same distribution; each item has a scalar position representing its stance.

**Round structure (T=150 rounds).** In each round:
1. *Recommendation.* For each agent, the recommender selects $k=5$ items from the pool under one of three policies:
   - **Random:** $k$ items drawn uniformly at random.
   - **Engagement-maximizing:** the $k$ items closest in opinion-distance to the agent's current opinion (an idealized proxy for engagement-optimized ranking, since engagement probability in our model is a decreasing function of distance).
   - **Diversity-aware (epsilon-greedy):** a fraction $(1-\epsilon)$ of the $k$ slots filled by nearest-opinion items, and $\epsilon$ filled by uniformly random items (main results use $\epsilon=0.3$).
2. *Engagement.* For each recommended item at distance $d$ from the agent's opinion, the agent engages with probability $p = 0.9 \cdot \exp(-d^2 / (2 \cdot 0.4^2))$.
3. *Opinion update.* For each engaged item at signed offset $d = x_{item} - o_i$: if $|d| \le$ confidence bound (default 0.5), the agent assimilates, $o_i \mathrel{+}= 0.15\,d$; otherwise, with probability 0.15 ("backfire"/reactance), the agent moves *away*, $o_i \mathrel{-}= 0.10\,d$. Opinions are clipped to $[-1, 1]$.
4. *Content generation.* 5% of agents (chosen at random each round) post a new item at their current opinion, which enters the pool (oldest items evicted once the cap is reached). This closes the loop: the pool the recommender draws from in round $t+1$ reflects the population's opinions as of round $t$.

**Metrics** (averaged over the final 20% of rounds, i.e. rounds 120–150, to capture steady-state behavior): opinion variance across the population; extremism share ($|o_i| > 0.7$); engagement rate (fraction of recommendation slots resulting in engagement); diversity consumed (mean opinion-distance between an agent and the items it actually engages with).

**Experiments.**
1. *Policy comparison*: random vs. engagement vs. diversity ($\epsilon=0.3$), 8 random seeds each, all other parameters at default.
2. *Confidence-bound ablation*: under the engagement policy, confidence bound $\in \{0.2, 0.5, 0.8\}$, 5 seeds each — testing whether individual open-mindedness moderates polarization.
3. *Epsilon sweep*: under the diversity policy, $\epsilon \in \{0.0, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0\}$ (0.0 = engagement, 1.0 = random), 5 seeds each — testing the exposure-diversity dose-response curve and its engagement cost.

All code is in `sim.py`; raw console output is in `run_log.txt`; full numeric results (including per-round variance trajectories) are in `results.json`; a plot of variance trajectories is in `variance_trajectories.png`. Total simulation runtime was 190.7 seconds on a single CPU core (59 simulation runs total across all experiments).

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Policy comparison (mean ± std over 8 seeds, final 20% of rounds)

| Policy | Opinion variance | Extremism share | Engagement rate | Diversity consumed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Random | 0.162 ± 0.074 | 0.003 ± 0.005 | 0.493 ± 0.061 | 0.206 ± 0.019 |
| Engagement-max | 0.330 ± 0.008 | 0.291 ± 0.018 | 0.901 ± 0.001 | 0.000 ± 0.000 |
| Diversity (ε=0.3) | 0.212 ± 0.016 | 0.012 ± 0.005 | 0.807 ± 0.002 | 0.028 ± 0.001 |

Pure engagement-maximization produces roughly 2× the opinion variance of random recommendation and pushes nearly 30% of the population to extreme opinions ($|o_i|>0.7$), versus 0.3% under random exposure — a clean reproduction of the filter-bubble hypothesis in this model. It also drives diversity consumed to essentially zero: once the population's self-generated content pool converges near each agent's own opinion, the "nearest" items the recommender finds are at distance ≈0. Notably, engagement-maximization also has the *highest* raw engagement rate (90.1%), illustrating the tension platform designers face: the policy that best serves the proximate optimization target (engagement) is the one that produces the most polarization. The diversity-aware policy recovers most of the variance and nearly all of the extremism reduction (1.2% vs 29.1%) while sacrificing only about 10 percentage points of engagement (80.7% vs 90.1%) — a favorable trade in this model, though we caution the engagement metric here is a simulated proxy, not a validated behavioral measure.

### 3.2 Confidence-bound ablation (engagement policy; 5 seeds)

| Confidence bound | Variance | Extremism |
|---|---|---|
| 0.2 | 0.333 ± 0.008 | 0.300 ± 0.012 |
| 0.5 | 0.333 ± 0.008 | 0.300 ± 0.012 |
| 0.8 | 0.333 ± 0.008 | 0.300 ± 0.012 |

The three conditions produce numerically identical results (to floating-point precision, confirmed by inspecting per-seed trajectories in `results.json`). This is not a bug: under the engagement-maximizing policy, the recommended items are, by construction, the closest available items in the pool, and — because the content pool is continually replenished by the population's own current opinions — that closest distance rapidly converges to near-zero. Since the confidence bound only matters when the offered content's distance is comparable to or larger than the bound, and here it is essentially always far smaller, the bound never binds. Put differently: **individual open-mindedness is causally inert in this model once the recommender has already curated a near-homogeneous information diet.** This is an important negative result: interventions that try to make individuals more receptive to disagreeing views (e.g., media-literacy campaigns) cannot help if those views are never surfaced in the first place. The lever that matters is upstream, at the exposure/recommendation stage.

### 3.3 Epsilon sweep (diversity policy; 5 seeds)

| ε | Variance | Engagement rate |
|---|---|---|
| 0.0 (= engagement) | 0.333 ± 0.008 | 0.901 ± 0.001 |
| 0.2 | 0.211 ± 0.020 | 0.807 ± 0.002 |
| 0.5 | 0.178 ± 0.046 | 0.639 ± 0.009 |
| 1.0 (= random) | 0.153 ± 0.090 | 0.577 ± 0.045 |
| baseline (from §3.1, ε=0.3) | 0.212 ± 0.016 | 0.807 ± 0.002 |

Even a small diversity budget ($\epsilon=0.2$) yields a large drop in variance relative to $\epsilon=0$ (0.211 vs 0.333, a 37% reduction) at a modest engagement cost (807 vs 901 per-mille), consistent with a steep, front-loaded dose-response curve: most of the achievable de-polarization benefit is available cheaply, with diminishing returns and rising variance in outcomes (larger std at $\epsilon=1.0$) as $\epsilon$ increases further.

### 3.4 Limitations

This is a stylized, single-issue-dimension model; real opinion spaces are multi-dimensional and content items are not points but complex objects that can move an agent's stance on some dimensions while being neutral on others. The engagement function, backfire probability, and assimilation rate are fixed hyperparameters we did not fit to real behavioral data — the *qualitative* comparisons (policy ranking, epsilon dose-response, bound-inertness) are the intended contribution, not the specific numeric values. We also assume the population and recommender share a single global content pool with no network structure (friendship graphs, following relationships), which real platforms have and which would likely modulate the strength of the feedback loop. Finally we did not have access to LLM API calls in this compute-constrained setting, so we could not directly test whether an LLM-based conversational agent (rather than an abstract "content item") produces analogous dynamics; this is a natural extension of this simulation framework, replacing recommended items with LLM-generated responses whose stance-adaptivity is a controlled parameter.

## 4. Conclusion

Using a fully reproducible, CPU-only agent-based simulation, we find that (1) engagement-maximizing recommendation substantially increases opinion polarization and extremism relative to random exposure, replicating the filter-bubble hypothesis in a controlled, causally interpretable setting; (2) a lightweight diversity-injection policy recovers most of the lost diversity at a bounded engagement cost, with steep, front-loaded returns; and (3) — the paper's central and somewhat counter-intuitive finding — individual-level open-mindedness (bounded-confidence tolerance) has *no measurable effect* on outcomes once the recommender has already curated a homogeneous information diet, because the tolerance parameter never binds against content that is never shown. This suggests that socio-technical interventions aimed at individual psychology (media literacy, "hear the other side" nudges) are unlikely to counteract polarization on their own in systems with strong engagement-optimized curation; the more load-bearing intervention point is the recommender's exposure policy itself. Future work should extend this framework to networked populations, multi-dimensional opinion spaces, and — compute permitting — real LLM-generated content whose stance-adaptivity (sycophancy) is directly manipulated, connecting this recommender-feedback-loop literature more tightly to the growing empirical literature on conversational AI sycophancy.
