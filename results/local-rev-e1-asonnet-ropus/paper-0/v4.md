# Recommender Policy, Not Individual Open-Mindedness, Drives Filter-Bubble Formation — Under What Conditions? An Agent-Based Simulation Study

## Abstract

Concerns that engagement-optimizing recommender systems fragment public opinion rest on observational data or short human-subject studies. We build a closed-loop agent-based simulation — agents with scalar opinions, a self-replenishing content pool, three recommender policies (random, engagement-maximizing, epsilon-greedy diversity-aware) — and stress-test a headline finding (engagement-max roughly doubles final opinion variance vs. random: 0.330 vs 0.162; extremism 29.1% vs 0.3%) against reviewer-proposed failure modes. This revision runs two further probes the previous review asked for, on top of three already reported. (1) **Does dynamic re-following rescue the recommender ranking under homophily?** No: letting agents periodically rewire toward their *current* (not just initial) opinion neighbors produces the same ~30–33% extremism under all three policies, entrenching rather than reversing the collapse we reported under static homophily. (2) **Is the flat-engagement extremism-inversion an artifact of the specific backfire probability?** Only partly: halving backfire_prob (0.15→0.075) still leaves engagement-max least polarizing (30.0% extremism) vs. random (54.3%), but diversity's extremism (30.3%) is now statistically indistinguishable from engagement-max's — the *inversion* survives, but the *diversity-is-safer* sub-claim does not survive at lower backfire strength. We revise our claims accordingly: the recommender-driven ranking is conditional on network homogeneity being low (static or dynamic homophily both defeat it) and on backfire being strong enough to differentiate policies once engagement is distance-insensitive. All code, logs, and numeric results are released.

## 1. Introduction

Recommender systems optimizing for engagement can amplify content homogeneity and shift opinions over repeated exposure (Pariser; Bakshy et al. 2015; Cinelli et al. 2021). A related literature on LLM sycophancy studies how AI systems that adapt to a user's expressed views affect trust in single- or few-turn conversation. Both share a structural concern: an adaptive AI system can entrench rather than inform. We treat sycophancy only as motivating analogy — our simulation has no LLM or conversational mechanism (see Limitations).

**Gap.** Human-subject studies of these loops are short-horizon; observational platform studies cannot manipulate recommender policy or decompose *which* mechanism — selection policy vs. individual openness — drives polarization. Agent-based simulation allows both, run long enough for compounding effects, and allows deliberately adversarial stress-testing of any resulting ranking.

**Contribution.** A minimal closed-loop simulation (agents, self-replenishing pool, three recommender policies, bounded-confidence update with reactance) isolates how much filter-bubble formation is attributable to recommender policy vs. individual open-mindedness. Each revision has tried to break the resulting ranking along a reviewer-identified axis; this one asks whether two of our three prior "breaks" (homophily, flat engagement) are themselves robust to a natural follow-up perturbation, rather than accepting them as final.

### Related Work

- **Filter bubbles.** Pariser (2011); Bakshy et al. (2015, *Science*): algorithmic ranking modestly reduces cross-cutting exposure, less than individual choice; Cinelli et al. (2021, *PNAS*) compared echo-chamber structure across platforms. We manipulate the recommender directly, which these cannot.
- **Opinion dynamics.** Hegselmann & Krause (2002), Deffuant et al. (2000): bounded-confidence models, the core mechanism here, under recommender-mediated rather than random pairwise selection.
- **LLM sycophancy.** CHI 2026 and arXiv work (2025–2026) study stance-adaptive AI in short interactions — a structurally related long-horizon complement, not a mechanism we implement.

## 2. Method

**Population and content.** N=300 agents, opinion $o_i \in [-1,1]$, i.i.d. Uniform(-1,1). Content pool (cap 3000) starts with $3N$ items from the same distribution.

**Round structure (T=150).** (1) *Recommendation*: $k=5$ items/agent under random, engagement-maximizing (nearest opinion-distance), or diversity-aware epsilon-greedy ($(1-\epsilon)$ nearest + $\epsilon$ random; main results $\epsilon=0.3$). (2) *Engagement*: item at distance $d$ engaged with $p=0.9\exp(-d^2/2\sigma^2)$, $\sigma=0.4$ default ("proximity"; flat variant $\sigma=5.0$). (3) *Update*: if $|d|\le$ confidence bound (0.5), assimilate $o_i \mathrel{+}= 0.15d$; else with prob. 0.15 (backfire_prob), backfire $o_i \mathrel{-}= 0.10d$ (backfire_rate); clip to $[-1,1]$. (4) *Content generation*: 5% of agents post at current opinion.

**Metrics** (mean, final 20% of rounds): variance, extremism share ($|o_i|>0.7$), engagement rate, diversity consumed.

**Experiments carried over:** policy comparison (8 seeds); confidence-bound ablation (5 seeds); epsilon sweep (5 seeds); pool-density × confidence-bound sweep (4 seeds); novelty-seeking engagement (5 seeds); random-following network, degree 20 (4 seeds); homophilous following fixed at t=0 (4 seeds); tripled-backfire ablation (4 seeds); flat-engagement policy comparison (5 seeds).

**New this revision:** (10) `sim_homophily_dynamic.py` — identical to the static homophily variant, but every 10 rounds each agent *rewires* to the 20 agents closest in *current* opinion (not just t=0), 4 seeds. (11) `sim_flat_engagement_halfbackfire.py` — re-runs the flat-engagement policy comparison with backfire_prob halved (0.15→0.075), 5 seeds. Combined new runtime: ~39s.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Policy comparison (mean ± std, 8 seeds)

| Policy | Variance | Extremism | Engagement rate | Diversity consumed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Random | 0.162 ± 0.074 | 0.003 ± 0.005 | 0.493 ± 0.061 | 0.206 ± 0.019 |
| Engagement-max | 0.330 ± 0.008 | 0.291 ± 0.018 | 0.901 ± 0.001 | 0.000 ± 0.000 |
| Diversity (ε=0.3) | 0.212 ± 0.016 | 0.012 ± 0.005 | 0.807 ± 0.002 | 0.028 ± 0.001 |

Engagement-maximization produces ~2x the variance of random exposure and pushes ~29% of agents to extremes vs. 0.3%. Diversity recovers most of the reduction (1.2% extremism) at ~10 points of engagement cost.

### 3.2 Confidence bound, pool density, and backfire strength

The bound is inert (var≈0.33) once the pool is dense (≥100 items); it activates once pool size shrinks toward $k=5$, where wider tolerance *lowers* variance. Tripling backfire (prob 0.15→0.45, rate 0.10→0.35) at the pool sizes where the bound binds amplifies but never reverses this sign (e.g. pool=8, cb=0.2: 0.423→0.753), confirming the monotone shape reflects the assimilation/backfire asymmetry rather than a fragile parameter choice.

### 3.3 Epsilon sweep (diversity policy, 5 seeds)

| ε | Variance | Engagement rate |
|---|---|---|
| 0.0 (=engagement) | 0.333 ± 0.008 | 0.901 ± 0.001 |
| 0.2 | 0.211 ± 0.020 | 0.807 ± 0.002 |
| 0.5 | 0.178 ± 0.046 | 0.639 ± 0.009 |
| 1.0 (=random) | 0.153 ± 0.090 | 0.577 ± 0.045 |

A small diversity budget (ε=0.2) already cuts variance 37% relative to ε=0 — a steep, front-loaded dose-response.

### 3.4 Engagement-function shape, and whether the flip is a backfire artifact

A novelty-seeking engagement function (peak at $d_0=0.5$) preserves the extremism ranking while reversing the variance ranking. A flat (distance-insensitive, $\sigma=5.0$) engagement function fully **inverts** the extremism ranking: engagement-max becomes *least* polarizing (30.0%) while random and diversity reach 99.7% and 85.4%, because engagement-max still only ever recommends near items (structurally insulated from backfire), while the other two expose agents to far content at full engagement rate, and every such exposure risks backfire.

**New — is this inversion just a backfire-rate artifact (reviewer Q2)?** We halved backfire_prob (0.15→0.075) under flat engagement:

| Policy | Variance (flat, half-backfire) | Extremism (flat, half-backfire) |
|---|---|---|
| Random | 0.523 ± 0.079 | 0.543 ± 0.186 |
| Engagement-max | 0.333 ± 0.008 | 0.300 ± 0.012 |
| Diversity | 0.405 ± 0.031 | 0.303 ± 0.062 |

The inversion **partly survives**: engagement-max (30.0%) is still far below random (54.3%). But diversity's extremism (30.3% ± 0.062) is now statistically indistinguishable from engagement-max's — at full backfire strength diversity was clearly worse (85.4%), at half it collapses onto engagement-max. So halving backfire strength does not restore the original ranking, but it does erase the specific sub-claim that engagement-max is uniquely best under flat engagement — it is now tied with diversity, both far better than random. The qualitative flip (engagement-max no longer worst) is robust to backfire strength; the specific ordinal ranking among the non-random policies is not.

### 3.5 Network structure: static homophily, and whether dynamic re-following rescues the ranking

A random degree-20 following network shrinks the policy effect by roughly an order of magnitude (extremism 0.001–0.044 across policies). A homophilous network — each agent follows the 20 closest others in *initial* opinion — instead swamps the ranking: all three policies land at 0.303–0.315 extremism, because the network itself performs the sorting a biased recommender would have, regardless of which policy operates within it.

**New — does dynamic re-following change this (reviewer Q1)?** We let agents rewire every 10 rounds toward their 20 closest neighbors in *current* opinion, rather than freezing the graph at t=0:

| Policy | Variance (dynamic homophily) | Extremism (dynamic homophily) |
|---|---|---|
| Random | 0.330 ± 0.014 | 0.333 ± 0.040 |
| Engagement-max | 0.330 ± 0.008 | 0.303 ± 0.014 |
| Diversity | 0.327 ± 0.015 | 0.305 ± 0.026 |

Dynamic re-following does **not** restore the recommender's leverage — extremism (30.3–33.3%) and the spread across policies are essentially identical to the static-homophily result. If anything, letting the graph track current opinion should tighten neighborhoods further as agents drift, and empirically it does not loosen the collapse at all. This answers the reviewer's question directly: the recommender's marginal choice does not regain leverage under more realistic, evolving homophily; the network's self-sorting dominates whether it is fixed or adaptive.

### 3.6 Limitations

Stylized, single-dimension model without validated behavioral calibration; the *ranking* and dose-response, not specific numeric values, are the intended contribution. Network experiments use small degree (20) and few seeds (4); real graphs are richer, and our dynamic re-following rule (periodic full rewire toward current opinion) is one plausible mechanism among several (e.g. gradual edge-by-edge rewiring) that we have not exhaustively compared. The flat-engagement condition is a deliberate extreme probe establishing an existence result, not an empirical estimate of a real engagement function. On the reviewer's third question — whether a real platform's engagement-distance curve or following-graph homophily can be estimated to locate it within this parameter space — that requires external behavioral/log data our simulation cannot supply; we can only report which of our internal manipulations would matter if such data showed distance-insensitive engagement or strong homophily, not whether either actually holds on a given platform. We had no LLM API access in this compute-constrained setting, so the sycophancy connection remains an analogy.

## 4. Conclusion

Engagement-maximizing recommendation substantially increases polarization relative to random exposure in our baseline model, and a lightweight diversity injection recovers most of the lost diversity cheaply. But the ranking is conditional, and this revision's two new tests sharpen rather than soften that conditionality: dynamic re-following does not rescue the ranking from homophily's collapse (network position dominates whether the graph is static or evolving), and halving backfire strength shows the flat-engagement inversion is partly but not fully robust — engagement-max stays least polarizing, but its advantage over diversity specifically (not over random) is backfire-rate-dependent. We state the claim as conditional on a dense global pool or non-homophilous network (static or dynamic) and a meaningfully distance-dependent engagement function; within flat-engagement regimes, only the "engagement-max is not worst" qualitative claim is robust, not the full ordinal ranking. Future empirical work should measure real engagement-distance curves, real following-graph homophily (static and dynamic), and real backfire/reactance rates to locate actual platforms within this parameter space, rather than treating any stylized baseline as generalizable by default.
