# Recommender Policy, Not Individual Open-Mindedness, Drives Filter-Bubble Formation — Under What Conditions? An Agent-Based Simulation Study

## Abstract

Concerns that engagement-optimizing recommender systems fragment public opinion rest on observational data (no causal isolation) or small human-subject studies. We build a lightweight, closed-loop agent-based simulation — agents with scalar opinions interacting over many rounds with a content pool the population itself generates — and compare three recommender policies (random, engagement-maximizing, epsilon-greedy diversity-aware). Engagement-maximization roughly doubles final opinion variance vs. random (0.330 vs 0.162) and raises extreme-opinion share from 0.3% to 29.1%; a modest diversity injection (epsilon=0.3) cuts extremism to 1.2% at a bounded engagement cost (80.7% vs 90.1%). We previously reported that agents' bounded-confidence tolerance (0.2 to 0.8) produces *zero* change under the engagement policy. This revision shows that "open-mindedness is inert" result is a near-tautological consequence of pool density: with ~100+ pool items, nearest-item distance collapses to near zero **even with no content replenishment**, so no tested tolerance can bind; it only starts to matter once the pool shrinks to roughly the same order as the number of recommendation slots (transition between pool sizes 100 and 20, N=300, k=5). We also stress-test the policy ranking along two axes the original submission did not: (1) a "novelty-seeking" engagement function (peak at nonzero distance) preserves the extremism ranking but *reverses* the variance ranking, showing the two metrics are not equally robust; (2) a simple random "following" network (degree 20) shrinks and de-stabilizes the effect (high cross-seed variance), showing the global-pool result does not straightforwardly generalize to networked platforms. We release all code, logs, and numeric results, including the new robustness experiments.

## 1. Introduction

Recommender systems that optimize for engagement can amplify content homogeneity and shift opinions over repeated exposure (Pariser's "filter bubble" hypothesis; Bakshy et al. 2015; Cinelli et al. 2021). A related literature on LLM sycophancy studies how AI systems that adapt to a user's expressed views affect trust and decisions in single- or few-turn conversational settings. Both share a structural concern: an AI system that adapts to what a user already believes can entrench rather than inform. We treat sycophancy only as motivating analogy here — our simulation has no LLM or conversational mechanism, and we do not claim to test it directly (see Limitations).

**Gap.** Human-subject studies of these feedback loops are typically short-horizon; observational platform studies cannot manipulate the recommender's policy or cleanly decompose *which* mechanism — selection policy vs. individual openness to disagreement — drives observed polarization. Agent-based simulation allows both, run long enough to observe compounding effects.

**Contribution.** We implement a minimal closed-loop simulation (agents, a self-replenishing content pool, three recommender policies, a bounded-confidence update rule with reactance) to ask how much filter-bubble formation is attributable to the recommender's exposure policy versus individual open-mindedness. Unlike our original submission, we do not stop at reporting that open-mindedness is inert — we test *why*, at what parameter values that inertness would break, and how sensitive the whole policy ranking is to two modeling choices a reviewer flagged: the shape of the engagement function and the presence of network structure.

### Related Work

- **Filter bubbles and echo chambers.** Pariser (2011); Bakshy et al. (2015, *Science*) found algorithmic ranking modestly reduces cross-cutting exposure, smaller than individual choice effects; Cinelli et al. (2021, *PNAS*) compared echo-chamber structure across platforms. Our simulation manipulates the recommender directly, which these observational studies cannot.
- **Opinion dynamics.** Hegselmann & Krause (2002) and Deffuant et al. (2000) introduced bounded-confidence models — the core mechanism we adopt, here under recommender-mediated rather than random pairwise selection.
- **LLM sycophancy.** CHI 2026 work and arXiv work (2025–2026) study stance-adaptive AI in short interactions; we flag this as a structurally related long-horizon complement, not a mechanism we implement.
- **Overreliance/calibration.** "Measuring and mitigating overreliance..." (2025) studies trust miscalibration; distinct from our population-level selection question.

## 2. Method

**Population and content.** N=300 agents each have opinion $o_i \in [-1,1]$, initialized i.i.d. Uniform(-1,1). A content pool (capped at 3000 items) starts with $3N$ items drawn from the same distribution.

**Round structure (T=150 rounds).** Each round: (1) *Recommendation* — select $k=5$ items per agent under **random** (uniform), **engagement-maximizing** (nearest opinion-distance items, an idealized proxy for engagement-optimized ranking), or **diversity-aware epsilon-greedy** ($(1-\epsilon)$ nearest + $\epsilon$ random; main results use $\epsilon=0.3$). (2) *Engagement* — item at distance $d$ engaged with probability $p=0.9\exp(-d^2/(2\cdot0.4^2))$ ("proximity"; see §3.4 for a "novelty-seeking" alternative). (3) *Opinion update* — engaged item at signed offset $d$: if $|d|\le$ confidence bound (default 0.5), assimilate $o_i \mathrel{+}= 0.15d$; else with probability 0.15, backfire $o_i \mathrel{-}= 0.10d$; clip to $[-1,1]$. (4) *Content generation* — 5% of agents post at their current opinion (oldest evicted at cap), closing the loop.

**Metrics** (mean over final 20% of rounds): opinion variance, extremism share ($|o_i|>0.7$), engagement rate, diversity consumed.

**Experiments.** (1) Policy comparison, 8 seeds. (2) Confidence-bound ablation under engagement policy, $\in\{0.2,0.5,0.8\}$, 5 seeds — replicated from the original submission. (3) Epsilon sweep under diversity policy, 5 seeds. New in this revision: (4) a **pool-density x confidence-bound sweep** — the same ablation re-run at post_frac $\in\{0.05,0.01,0.002,0\}$ and, holding post_frac=0, at initial pool sizes $\in\{900,100,20,8,5\}$, 4 seeds each, tracking mean nearest-recommended-item distance. (5) a **novelty-seeking engagement function** ($p=0.9\exp(-(d-0.5)^2/(2\cdot0.4^2))$), full policy comparison, 5 seeds. (6) a **networked variant**: each agent follows a fixed random set of 20 others; the recommender draws only from content posted by that set (local history, last 30 items), 4 seeds. New code: `sim_robustness.py`, `sim_sparsity.py`, `sim_network.py`; results in the matching `results_*.json` files, run this session (combined runtime 173s).

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Policy comparison (mean ± std, 8 seeds)

| Policy | Variance | Extremism | Engagement rate | Diversity consumed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Random | 0.162 ± 0.074 | 0.003 ± 0.005 | 0.493 ± 0.061 | 0.206 ± 0.019 |
| Engagement-max | 0.330 ± 0.008 | 0.291 ± 0.018 | 0.901 ± 0.001 | 0.000 ± 0.000 |
| Diversity (ε=0.3) | 0.212 ± 0.016 | 0.012 ± 0.005 | 0.807 ± 0.002 | 0.028 ± 0.001 |

Engagement-maximization produces ~2x the variance of random exposure and pushes ~29% of the population to extremes vs. 0.3%, while also achieving the *highest* raw engagement rate — the tension platform designers face. The diversity policy recovers most of the reduction (1.2% extremism) at ~10 points of engagement cost.

### 3.2 Confidence-bound ablation and its pool-density dependence

The original ablation found variance and extremism numerically identical across confidence bounds 0.2/0.5/0.8 (var=0.333, ext=0.300), because the nearest recommendable item is at distance ≈0 once the pool is dense. A reviewer noted this looks tautological and asked whether it survives at lower pool density. We tested this by (a) sweeping the content-generation rate to 0 (no replenishment ever) and (b) shrinking the initial pool size while holding replenishment at 0, tracking mean nearest-recommended-item distance.

**It is even more tautological than we realized.** Turning off replenishment barely moves nearest-item distance (post_frac=0.05: 8.7e-6; post_frac=0: 6.4e-4) — the effect comes not from the feedback loop refreshing content, but simply from ~900 points scattered densely in a 1-D interval of width 2. Shrinking the *pool size itself* is what matters:

| Initial pool size | Nearest dist (cb=0.2) | Variance, cb=0.2 | Variance, cb=0.5 | Variance, cb=0.8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 900 | 0.0006 | 0.332 | 0.332 | 0.332 |
| 100 | 0.0050 | 0.333 | 0.333 | 0.333 |
| 20 | 0.030 | 0.360 | 0.261 | 0.261 |
| 8 | 0.081 | 0.423 | 0.342 | 0.277 |
| 5 | 0.175 | 0.391 | 0.418 | 0.387 |

The bound becomes causally active only once the pool shrinks to roughly the order of magnitude of the number of slots recommended per round ($k=5$; transition between pool sizes 100 and 20). At that point wider tolerance mostly *lowers* variance (more items fall within the bound, pulling agents toward assimilation rather than backfire) — the opposite sign a naive "openness only reduces extremism" intuition predicts — though at pool=5 the pattern is noisier and non-monotonic. **Revised interpretation:** open-mindedness is inert whenever effective content supply is large relative to per-round recommendations, plausibly true on real large platforms — but the finding is best read as "the bound doesn't bind under realistic pool/slot ratios," not as a general claim that open-mindedness never matters.

### 3.3 Epsilon sweep (diversity policy, 5 seeds)

| ε | Variance | Engagement rate |
|---|---|---|
| 0.0 (=engagement) | 0.333 ± 0.008 | 0.901 ± 0.001 |
| 0.2 | 0.211 ± 0.020 | 0.807 ± 0.002 |
| 0.5 | 0.178 ± 0.046 | 0.639 ± 0.009 |
| 1.0 (=random) | 0.153 ± 0.090 | 0.577 ± 0.045 |

A small diversity budget (ε=0.2) already cuts variance 37% relative to ε=0 at modest engagement cost — a steep, front-loaded dose-response curve with diminishing returns thereafter.

### 3.4 Robustness to the engagement-function assumption

A reviewer asked whether the policy ranking depends on engagement probability being monotone-decreasing in distance. We re-ran the policy comparison with engagement instead peaking at a nonzero distance (novelty-seeking, peak at d=0.5), leaving the recommender's proximity-based "engagement-maximizing" policy unchanged (the recommender still assumes proximity maximizes engagement, but the population no longer behaves that way):

| Policy | Variance | Extremism |
|---|---|---|
| Random | 0.360 ± 0.040 | 0.165 ± 0.067 |
| Engagement-max | 0.333 ± 0.008 | 0.301 ± 0.012 |
| Diversity | 0.327 ± 0.019 | 0.125 ± 0.027 |

The extremism ranking is preserved (engagement-max still worst, diversity still best), but the *variance* ranking reverses (random now has the highest variance). Extremism share is thus the more robust indicator of the mechanism we care about; variance conflates "spread" with "extremity" in ways that depend on the engagement function's shape. We recommend extremism share as the primary metric in future work with this framework.

### 3.5 Robustness to network structure

A reviewer asked whether the policy>open-mindedness ordering survives a simple following-graph, since our main model uses one global pool. We implemented a lightweight variant: each agent follows a fixed random set of 20 others and only sees content posted by that set (local history, last 30 items), otherwise identical mechanics.

| Policy | Variance | Extremism |
|---|---|---|
| Random | 0.245 ± 0.017 | 0.004 ± 0.005 |
| Engagement-max | 0.225 ± 0.060 | 0.044 ± 0.043 |
| Diversity | 0.235 ± 0.030 | 0.001 ± 0.001 |

With a degree-20 following network, the effect **shrinks by roughly an order of magnitude in absolute extremism and becomes noisy**: cross-seed standard deviations are large relative to the differences between policies, and the variance ordering no longer clearly favors engagement-max. Restricting each agent to a small, fixed neighborhood dilutes the recommender's ability to concentrate exposure the way a global, fully personalized pool does. This qualifies our original generalization claims: the global-pool result should not be assumed to transfer directly to networked platforms without further study (larger networks, more seeds, homophilous rather than random following).

### 3.6 Limitations

This remains a stylized, single-dimension model without validated behavioral calibration; the policy ranking and epsilon dose-response, not the specific numeric values, are the intended contribution. The network experiment is a first, coarse probe (random rather than homophilous following, small degree, fewer seeds) and should be read as a robustness flag, not a full treatment. We had no LLM API access in this compute-constrained setting, so the sycophancy connection remains an analogy, not a tested mechanism; replacing content items with LLM-generated, stance-adaptive responses is the natural next extension.

## 4. Conclusion

Engagement-maximizing recommendation substantially increases polarization relative to random exposure in this model, and a lightweight diversity injection recovers most of the lost diversity cheaply. Our original claim that individual open-mindedness is causally inert holds, but this revision shows it is contingent on realistic pool/slot ratios rather than a deep structural fact, and is more robust on the extremism metric than the variance metric. It is also substantially weaker — noisy and non-dominant — once a simple following-network restricts exposure, tempering any claim that platform-level interventions alone generalize straightforwardly to networked settings. We still think the recommender's exposure policy is the more load-bearing lever relative to individual psychology in this class of models, but we now state that conclusion with the boundary conditions under which it holds (dense global pool, proximity-shaped engagement, no or weak network structure) rather than unconditionally. Future work should extend to homophilous networks at scale, multi-dimensional opinion spaces, and, compute permitting, real LLM-generated content with a directly manipulated stance-adaptivity parameter.
