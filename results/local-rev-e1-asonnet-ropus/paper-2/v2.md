# Engagement Bias Fragments Less, Bridging Bias Fragments More: An Agent-Based Study of Recommender-Mediated Opinion Dynamics

## Abstract

Algorithmic curation is widely blamed for polarizing public opinion, yet recent field experiments find only limited effects of short-term filter-bubble exposure on individual attitudes. We revisit this puzzle with a lightweight, fully reproducible agent-based model coupling Deffuant–Weisbuch bounded-confidence opinion dynamics with a single parameter β controlling how strongly an algorithm biases *who talks to whom* toward similarity (β>0, engagement-maximizing curation) or dissimilarity (β<0, bridging/diversity interventions) relative to random pairing (β=0). Across a 10-point β sweep (30 seeds/condition, deterministic integer seeding) and ablations of confidence threshold ε and population size N, with Mann-Whitney U tests against baseline for every comparison, we find: **(1)** final opinion variance is not reliably moved by β — where differences reach significance (β∈{−10,−5,−1,20}) the effect size is tiny (≤0.012 on a 0.067–0.079 range) and non-monotonic, consistent with muted field-experiment effects; **(2)** the "modest cluster reduction" under engagement bias reported in an earlier draft of this work is **not statistically significant at any tested β≥1** (p=0.10–0.65) and we retract it as noise; **(3)** bridging interventions (β≤−2) produce a large, highly significant fragmentation increase (p<0.001), from 3.70 clusters at baseline to 13.97 at β=−10, because pairing agents beyond the confidence threshold ε produces contact without the belief updates needed for consensus. We also show "engagement" (mean similarity of realized pairs) rises with β by construction, not as an independent finding. The revised contribution is narrower and better supported: exactly reproducible numbers (verified via byte-identical reruns), significance-tested throughout, showing a real but *one-sided* fragmentation risk from bridging bias that a variance-only report would miss.

## 1. Introduction

The "filter bubble" hypothesis (Pariser, 2011) holds that engagement-optimizing recommenders narrow exposure to congruent content and polarize populations. Evidence is mixed: ABM and Monte Carlo studies combining recommender logic with opinion dynamics generally find narrower "bubbles" increase echo chambers, while large naturalistic field experiments (YouTube, PNAS 2025; German/US news recommenders, 2024) report modest or null short-run polarization effects. Parallel LLM work documents a related mechanism — sycophancy, where models validate a user's stated beliefs and measurably shift attitude extremity after brief interactions (Rathje et al., 2025). A recurring gap in the ABM literature is that most studies report a single scalar (variance or a bimodality index) as *the* outcome of "algorithmic amplification," collapsing (i) whether an algorithm changes overall opinion spread and (ii) whether it changes the number of *distinct clusters* the population settles into. This collapse can make an intervention look "ineffective" (unchanged variance) while reshaping social structure — but demonstrating that credibly requires significance testing and exact reproducibility, both missing from our first draft, which a review found reported seed-dependent numbers from an unsalted-hash bug and an untested "modest reduction" claim that did not survive scrutiny.

### 1.1 Related work

- **Bounded-confidence opinion dynamics.** Deffuant-Weisbuch (2000) and Hegselmann-Krause (2002) formalize agents updating only toward partners within confidence threshold ε; standard substrates for polarization simulation.
- **Recommender-biased interaction.** Prior ABM work (JDIQ 2022; Applied Network Science 2024; JCSS 2025) combines recommender-style bias with opinion dynamics and generally reports narrower bubbles increase echo-chamber counts. We isolate one interpretable parameter β, separate variance from cluster-count outcomes, and — unlike prior summaries — significance-test every β-vs-baseline comparison.
- **Field evidence of limited effects.** PNAS 2025 (YouTube) and Kobbe et al. 2024 (Germany/U.S.) report weak or null short-run polarization effects from manipulated recommendations, in tension with simulation studies assuming strong effects.
- **LLM sycophancy.** Sycophancy work (Fanous et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2025; Rathje et al. 2025) targets belief change within a single human-AI dyad; our model targets population-level consequences of biased *pairing*. This connection is conceptual only — not modeled or tested here.

Our contribution, after revision: a minimal, fast, **exactly reproducible** model with every headline comparison **significance-tested**, showing (a) bridging bias produces a large, statistically robust fragmentation increase with no accompanying variance change, and (b) the previously claimed complementary benefit of engagement bias — modestly *reducing* fragmentation — does not survive testing at a matched interaction budget and should not be reported as a finding. The asymmetry is real; the mechanism is a corollary of the update rule; the value added is careful measurement and honest negative results.

## 2. Method

**Population and opinions.** N agents hold scalar opinion x_i∈[0,1], i.i.d. Uniform(0,1).

**Interaction selection.** Each of T iterations draws focal agent i uniformly, then partner j via P(j|i)∝exp(−β·|x_i−x_j|). β=0 = random pairing (baseline). β>0 biases toward similar partners (engagement-maximizing curation); β<0 toward dissimilar partners (bridging).

**Opinion update (Deffuant-Weisbuch).** If |x_i−x_j|<ε, both move toward each other with rate μ; otherwise no update — the interaction happened but was not persuasive.

**Metrics.** (1) final **variance**; (2) **engagement** = running mean of exp(−|x_i−x_j|) over realized interactions — since β directly reweights pairing by this same distance, its rise with β is a **definitional consequence of the sampling rule, not an independent finding**; we report it only as a manipulation check; (3) **# clusters**, gaps>2/N in sorted opinions; (4) Sarle's **bimodality coefficient**.

**Statistical testing (new).** For every β (and ε/N ablation cell) we run a two-sided Mann-Whitney U test on per-seed variance and cluster-count against the β=0 baseline, reporting p alongside every mean; we call a difference a "finding" only if p<0.05.

**Reproducibility fix (new).** The first draft seeded runs via `hash((tag,beta,N,eps,s))`, and Python's `hash()` on tuples/strings is salted per-process, so identical reruns gave different table values — exactly as the review demonstrated. We replaced this with pure integer arithmetic, `seed = RNG_SEED_BASE + cond_index*n_seeds + s`, fed to `numpy.random.default_rng`. We verified the fix by running `experiment.py` twice this session and diffing the summary JSON: **byte-identical output** both times.

**Baselines and ablations.** β∈{−10,−5,−2,−1,0,1,2,5,10,20} (30 seeds/condition, up from 12, to sharpen significance tests). Ablations: ε∈{0.05,0.1,0.15,0.2,0.3,0.5} and N∈{50,100,200,400}, each at β∈{0,5}.

**Implementation.** Pure NumPy, N=100, T=4000, μ=0.3, ε=0.15 default, deterministic seeding. Code in `experiment.py`; raw results in `results_beta_sweep.csv`, `results_epsilon_ablation.csv`, `results_N_ablation.csv`; log in `run_log.txt`. Wall-clock for all sweeps: **≈70s** on one CPU core (up from 28.9s in the first draft since seeds/condition tripled).

**(i) Variance is not reliably moved by β.** Four of nine comparisons reach p<0.05, but the largest shift (0.0122) is small relative to baseline sd (0.0085) and non-monotonic in |β| (β=−1, 20 significant; β=−2,1,2,5,10 not) — consistent with noise around a flat curve, and with muted field-experiment variance effects. **(ii) Cluster count is asymmetric, and only the bridging side is robust.** β=−10,−5,−2 all show p<0.001 with large effects (3.6–3.8× baseline). β=−1 is not significant (p=0.446) — fragmentation only becomes reliable once bias pushes most pairs' gap above ε. **No β≥1 reaches significance** (p=0.10–0.65): the earlier "modest reduction to 3.3–3.6 clusters" is real numbers but not a real effect, and we retract it. **(iii)** Bimodality stays at 0.207–0.231 throughout, far below the ≈0.555 threshold: bridging produces many small clusters, not two large camps.

Larger ε monotonically drives the system toward one cluster regardless of β — ε, not recommender bias, is the dominant lever on whether consensus is reachable. β=5 clusters stay close to β=0 at every ε, consistent with §3.1: engagement bias does not reliably fragment more (or less).

### 3.3 Ablation: population size N (β∈{0,5}, T=4000, 30 seeds)

| N | Clusters β=0 | Clusters β=5 | Engagement β=0 | Engagement β=5 |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 50 | 3.63 | 3.60 | 0.7624 | 0.9228 |
| 100 | 3.63 | 3.30 | 0.7578 | 0.9180 |
| 200 | 14.43 | 4.90 | 0.7501 | 0.9009 |
| 400 | 47.83 | 32.07 | 0.7423 | 0.8806 |

At fixed T, cluster count grows sharply with N (interactions-per-agent T/N falls) — larger populations are under-mixed, so cross-N comparisons must control for that. β=5 shows notably fewer clusters at N=200,400 (4.90 vs 14.43; 32.07 vs 47.83), a gap large enough to be unlikely pure noise, unlike the flat N=100 case in §3.1 — likely a budget-scarcity effect (homophily concentrates a scarce budget on already-close pairs) rather than evidence that engagement bias fragments less at ample, matched budgets. We did not formally test this ablation (only 2×4 cells) and flag it as a direction for confirmation, not a settled result.

## 4. Discussion and Limitations

The model is deliberately minimal: one well-mixed population, one scalar opinion, one global β; it omits network topology, stubborn agents, and multi-topic coupling. The engagement metric is a manipulation check, not a discovery. This revision's main contribution is process: we fixed a real reproducibility bug (unsalted-hash seeding) and significance-tested every headline comparison, which changed our own conclusions — the original "engagement bias reduces fragmentation" claim does not survive testing at a matched budget, while "bridging bias sharply fragments without moving variance" does (p<0.001 for β≤−2) and is now the paper's defensible center. The mechanism itself — pairing beyond ε produces contact without persuasion — is an immediate corollary of the Deffuant-Weisbuch rule; the value added is a carefully tested measurement of its size and asymmetry, not a new mechanism.

## 5. Conclusion

Using an exactly reproducible, significance-tested simulation, we show recommender pairing-bias (β) has a real but asymmetric effect on two commonly conflated outcomes. Overall variance is not reliably moved by β either direction. Cluster count is strongly and significantly increased by bridging bias (3.6–3.8× baseline at β≤−2, p<0.001) because pairing beyond ε produces contact without belief change, while the previously claimed reduction in clusters under engagement-maximizing bias does not hold up at a fixed interaction budget and should not be relied upon. Designers of diversity-boosting recommenders should evaluate fragmentation explicitly and with significance testing — an intervention can look inert on variance while robustly reshaping community structure, and not every eye-catching mean difference in a small ABM survives a nonparametric test. Future work: networked topologies, per-agent β personalization, empirically calibrated ε distributions, and a properly powered test of the N-ablation's budget-scarcity effect.
