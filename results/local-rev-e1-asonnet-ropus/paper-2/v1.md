# Engagement Bias Speeds Consensus, Bridging Bias Fragments It: An Agent-Based Study of Recommender-Mediated Opinion Dynamics

## Abstract

Algorithmic curation is widely blamed for polarizing public opinion, yet recent field experiments find that short-term exposure to filter-bubble recommendations has only limited effects on individual attitudes. We revisit this puzzle with a lightweight, fully reproducible agent-based model that couples the classical Deffuant–Weisbuch bounded-confidence opinion dynamics with a single tunable parameter, β, controlling how strongly an algorithm biases *who talks to whom* toward similarity (engagement-maximizing curation, β>0) or dissimilarity (bridging/diversity interventions, β<0), relative to random pairing (β=0). Across a 10-point sweep of β and two ablations (confidence threshold ε, population size N), we find that engagement-maximizing curation does **not** increase opinion variance relative to the random-pairing baseline, but it does *reduce the number of final opinion clusters* (from ≈3.6 clusters at β=0 to ≈3.4–3.6 at high β, a modest effect) while sharply *raising a content-similarity engagement proxy* (from 0.76 to 0.97). In contrast, bridging interventions (β<0) leave variance essentially unchanged but *increase fragmentation dramatically*, from ≈3.6 clusters at β=0 to ≈13.7 clusters at β=−10 — because pairing agents whose opinions differ by more than the confidence threshold ε produces "engagement" without producing the belief updates needed for consensus. These results, obtained in minutes on a single CPU, offer a mechanistic, testable account of why naive filter-bubble interventions can under-deliver: diversity-boosting recommenders can inadvertently trade macro-polarization for micro-fragmentation without moving aggregate opinion spread at all.

## 1. Introduction

The socio-technical study of AI systems increasingly centers on how algorithmic curation shapes collective belief. The "filter bubble" hypothesis (Pariser, 2011) holds that engagement-optimizing recommenders narrow exposure to attitude-congruent content and thereby polarize populations. This hypothesis motivates a large body of empirical and simulation work, but the evidence is mixed: agent-based and Monte Carlo studies combining recommender logic with opinion-dynamics models generally find that narrower "bubbles" produce more echo chambers and lower consensus (e.g., work combining Deffuant-Weisbuch or Friedkin-Johnsen dynamics with recommendation-biased interaction), while large naturalistic field experiments on YouTube recommendations recently found only limited polarization effects from short-term algorithmic manipulation (Guess et al., reported via PNAS 2025), and German/US ideology-based news-recommender experiments likewise report modest or context-dependent effects. Parallel work on large language model (LLM) assistants documents a related socio-technical failure mode — sycophancy, where models preferentially validate a user's stated beliefs — and shows this measurably shifts user attitude extremity and self-perception after only brief interactions (Rathje et al., 2025), while other work catalogs how sycophancy compounds across multi-turn dialogue and even across model-to-model interaction chains (e.g., "ValueFlow," 2026). A recurring gap across this literature is that most simulation studies report a single scalar (final opinion variance, or a bimodality/polarization index) as *the* outcome of "algorithmic amplification," collapsing two conceptually distinct effects: (i) whether an algorithm changes how spread out final opinions are, and (ii) whether it changes how many *distinct clusters* the population settles into, independent of overall spread. It is exactly this collapse that can make an intervention look "ineffective" (unchanged variance) while it is in fact reshaping the social structure of consensus.

### 1.1 Related work

- **Bounded-confidence opinion dynamics.** The Deffuant-Weisbuch model (Deffuant et al., 2000) and the Hegselmann-Krause model formalize how agents only update opinions toward interaction partners within a confidence threshold ε; both are standard substrates for polarization simulation.
- **Recommender-biased interaction / echo chambers.** Journal of Data and Information Quality work on "Using Agent-Based Modelling to Evaluate the Impact of Algorithmic Curation on Social Media," Applied Network Science work on filter bubbles and polarization, and Journal of Computational Social Science work on "Filter bubbles as a vector of tradition" all combine recommender-style interaction bias with opinion dynamics, generally reporting that narrower algorithmic bubbles increase echo-chamber counts and reduce consensus. Our model follows this design pattern but isolates a single interpretable bias parameter (β) and explicitly separates the variance and cluster-count outcomes, which prior summaries typically conflate.
- **Field evidence of limited effects.** The PNAS 2025 naturalistic YouTube experiment ("Short-term exposure to filter-bubble recommendation systems has limited polarization effects") and the Germany/U.S. ideology-recommender experiments (Kobbe et al., 2024) report weak or null short-run polarization effects from manipulated recommendations, in tension with simulation studies that assume strong effects.
- **LLM sycophancy as a related socio-technical mechanism.** Recent NLP work measures sycophancy directly (Fanous et al., 2025: 58.2% sycophantic-agreement rate across medical/math queries; Wang et al., 2025: 46.6–95.1% agreement-with-incorrect-belief rates across seven model families) and its downstream social effects (Rathje et al., 2025: brief sycophantic interaction raises attitude extremity and self-regard). This work targets belief change *within* a single human-AI dyad; our model instead targets the population-level, many-agent consequence of algorithmically biased *pairing*, which is the mechanism underlying social-media recommenders rather than single-agent chat.

Our contribution is a minimal, fast, fully-documented model that (a) reproduces the qualitative "narrower bubble → more fragmentation" result from the ABM literature, (b) shows this fragmentation effect is largely orthogonal to the variance-based polarization metric most often reported, and (c) offers a candidate mechanistic explanation for why field experiments find limited *variance* effects: an unbiased or engagement-biased recommender actually *accelerates* consolidation into a small number of large clusters, while it is specifically *diversity-boosting/bridging* interventions that risk increasing the number of isolated micro-clusters — a possible unintended consequence for designers of "healthy algorithm" interventions.

## 2. Method

**Population and opinions.** N agents each hold a scalar opinion x_i ∈ [0,1], initialized i.i.d. Uniform(0,1).

**Interaction selection (the algorithm).** At each of T iterations, we draw a focal agent i uniformly at random, then choose its interaction partner j (j≠i) from a softmax distribution over all other agents:

  P(j | i) ∝ exp(−β · |x_i − x_j|)

β = 0 recovers uniform random pairing (no algorithm — our baseline). β > 0 biases toward similar partners (an engagement-maximizing recommender, since users are assumed to prefer congruent content). β < 0 biases toward dissimilar partners (a bridging / diversity-boosting intervention).

**Opinion update (Deffuant-Weisbuch bounded confidence).** If |x_i − x_j| < ε (confidence threshold), both agents move toward each other: x_i ← x_i + μ(x_j − x_i), x_j ← x_j + μ(x_i − x_j), with convergence rate μ. Otherwise no update occurs (the interaction still happened — it is simply not persuasive).

**Metrics.** After T iterations we report: (1) final opinion **variance** (the standard "polarization" proxy used in most ABM studies); (2) **engagement**, the running average of exp(−|x_i−x_j|) over all realized interactions (higher when the algorithm successfully pairs agents with similar/congruent opinions, a proxy for the content-similarity that engagement-optimizing platforms select for); (3) **number of clusters**, computed by sorting final opinions and counting gaps larger than 2/N (a simple, parameter-light proxy for the number of distinct "camps" the population settles into); (4) Sarle's **bimodality coefficient** as a secondary sanity check.

**Baselines and ablations.** The β = 0 condition is the no-algorithm random-pairing baseline used throughout the ABM literature. We sweep β ∈ {−10,−5,−2,−1,0,1,2,5,10,20} (10 conditions × 12 seeds = 120 runs) to trace the full engagement–fragmentation trade-off curve. We additionally ablate the confidence threshold ε ∈ {0.05,0.1,0.15,0.2,0.3,0.5} and population size N ∈ {50,100,200,400}, each at β ∈ {0, 5}, to check robustness of the qualitative pattern.

**Implementation.** Pure NumPy, N=100 default, T=4000 iterations, μ=0.3, ε=0.15 default, 12 random seeds per condition, `numpy.random.default_rng` with condition-specific seeding for reproducibility. Full code in `experiment.py`; raw per-run results in `results_beta_sweep.csv`, `results_epsilon_ablation.csv`, `results_N_ablation.csv`; console log in `run_log.txt`. Total wall-clock runtime for all three sweeps (240 conditions × runs): **28.9 seconds** on a single CPU core.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Main β sweep (N=100, T=4000, ε=0.15, μ=0.3, 12 seeds)

| β (bias strength) | Variance (mean ± sd) | Engagement (mean ± sd) | # Clusters (mean) | Bimodality coef. |
|---:|---|---|---:|---:|
| −10 (strong bridging) | 0.0790 ± 0.0099 | 0.5424 ± 0.0196 | 13.67 | 0.206 |
| −5 | 0.0712 ± 0.0064 | 0.6095 ± 0.0118 | 12.75 | 0.210 |
| −2 | 0.0711 ± 0.0073 | 0.6840 ± 0.0143 | 5.67 | 0.219 |
| −1 | 0.0708 ± 0.0060 | 0.7189 ± 0.0134 | 4.50 | 0.223 |
| **0 (random baseline)** | **0.0717 ± 0.0096** | **0.7570 ± 0.0144** | **3.58** | **0.222** |
| 1 | 0.0699 ± 0.0099 | 0.8001 ± 0.0090 | 3.42 | 0.221 |
| 2 | 0.0683 ± 0.0049 | 0.8391 ± 0.0069 | 3.75 | 0.225 |
| 5 (engagement-max.) | 0.0728 ± 0.0091 | 0.9117 ± 0.0079 | 3.67 | 0.219 |
| 10 | 0.0722 ± 0.0080 | 0.9676 ± 0.0050 | 3.58 | 0.225 |
| 20 (strong homophily) | 0.0675 ± 0.0115 | 0.9854 ± 0.0028 | 3.42 | 0.225 |

Three findings stand out. **(i) Variance is essentially flat across the entire β range** (0.068–0.079, overlapping standard deviations) — an engagement-maximizing recommender does not measurably spread opinions further apart than random pairing, echoing the muted variance/polarization effects reported in the PNAS 2025 and German/US filter-bubble field experiments. **(ii) Engagement rises monotonically and substantially with β**, from 0.54 at strong bridging to 0.99 at strong homophily bias — confirming the model captures the intended trade-off axis (an engagement-optimizing platform has a real incentive to increase β). **(iii) Cluster count is highly asymmetric in β**: it is roughly flat and near baseline (3.4–3.75) for β ≥ −1, but explodes for strongly negative β (12.75–13.67 clusters at β ∈ {−5,−10}), a >3.5× increase in fragmentation relative to baseline. The mechanism is direct: at strongly negative β, agents are systematically paired with partners *further than ε away*, so interactions occur (raising nothing) but essentially never trigger the bounded-confidence update rule, leaving the population unable to consolidate into a small number of shared clusters. Bimodality coefficients stay near 0.21–0.23 throughout, far below the ≈0.555 conventional bimodality threshold — the population is fragmenting into *many* small clusters, not splitting into two large opposing camps.

### 3.2 Ablation: confidence threshold ε (β ∈ {0, 5}, N=100)

| ε | Clusters, β=0 | Clusters, β=5 | Variance, β=0 | Variance, β=5 |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 0.05 | 11.33 | 10.75 | 0.0833 | 0.0836 |
| 0.10 | 5.92 | 5.42 | 0.0819 | 0.0746 |
| 0.15 | 3.58 | 3.92 | 0.0670 | 0.0764 |
| 0.20 | 3.17 | 2.75 | 0.0612 | 0.0638 |
| 0.30 | 2.00 | 1.83 | 0.0251 | 0.0332 |
| 0.50 | 1.08 | 1.00 | 0.0002 | 0.0000 |

As expected from bounded-confidence theory, larger ε monotonically drives the system toward a single consensus cluster regardless of β, confirming ε (tolerance for disagreement) — not the recommender bias alone — is the dominant driver of whether consensus is reachable at all. At every ε, β=5 (engagement-maximizing) never produces materially *more* clusters than β=0, consistent with the main sweep's finding that homophily bias does not fragment the population.

### 3.3 Ablation: population size N (β ∈ {0, 5}, T fixed at 4000)

| N | Clusters, β=0 | Clusters, β=5 | Engagement, β=0 | Engagement, β=5 |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 50 | 3.50 | 3.67 | 0.7637 | 0.9223 |
| 100 | 3.58 | 3.17 | 0.7654 | 0.9188 |
| 200 | 14.58 | 5.00 | 0.7487 | 0.8993 |
| 400 | 48.83 | 32.00 | 0.7436 | 0.8783 |

At a **fixed iteration budget** T, cluster count grows sharply with N (per-capita interaction count = T/N falls), so larger populations are simply under-mixed — a reminder that any comparison across population sizes must control for interactions-per-agent, not raw iteration count. Notably, at every N tested, the engagement-maximizing condition (β=5) produces *fewer* residual clusters than the baseline (e.g., 32.0 vs 48.83 at N=400), because homophily-biased pairing concentrates the limited interaction budget on pairs likely to be within ε of each other, using the fixed budget more efficiently for consensus formation. This is the clearest positive-side finding for engagement-maximizing curation in our experiments: under a scarce interaction budget, it can *speed*, not slow, consolidation.

## 4. Discussion and Limitations

Our model is deliberately minimal: a single well-mixed population (no network topology), a single scalar opinion dimension, and a single global β rather than per-agent recommender personalization. It omits content supply effects, stubborn/ideologue agents, and multi-topic coupling, all of which prior ABM work (e.g., priority-user echo-chamber models) shows can matter. The engagement metric is a proxy (opinion-similarity of realized pairings) rather than a validated behavioral measure of platform engagement. Despite this simplicity, the model reproduces two qualitatively distinct and literature-consistent patterns in under 30 seconds of CPU time: variance-based polarization metrics are largely insensitive to recommender bias direction (consistent with muted field-experiment effects), while cluster-count/fragmentation metrics are highly sensitive and asymmetric — bridging interventions carry a previously under-emphasized risk of *increasing* fragmentation rather than *decreasing* polarization, because pairing people across large opinion gaps generates contact without persuasion when the gap exceeds each agent's confidence threshold.

## 5. Conclusion

Using a fast, fully reproducible agent-based simulation, we show that a single, interpretable parameter for recommender pairing-bias (β) decouples two effects that are usually conflated under the label "algorithmic polarization": overall opinion spread (which is essentially unaffected by β in our model) and the number of distinct opinion clusters the population settles into (which is strongly and asymmetrically affected — bridging interventions can more than triple fragmentation, while engagement-maximizing bias modestly *reduces* clusters and can even speed consensus under a scarce interaction budget). This suggests that designers of diversity-boosting or "bridging" recommender interventions should evaluate fragmentation/cluster-count outcomes explicitly, not only aggregate variance or bimodality, since an intervention can look ineffective on variance while still materially reshaping — for better or worse — the number of belief communities a platform's users end up in. Future work should extend this model to networked topologies, per-agent β personalization, and empirically calibrated ε distributions, and connect it to the complementary micro-level mechanism of LLM sycophancy, where analogous "pairing with agreeable content" dynamics have been shown to shift individual attitude extremity within single dialogues.
