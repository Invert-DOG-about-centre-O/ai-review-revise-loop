# Engagement Bias Fragments Less, Bridging Bias Fragments More: An Agent-Based Study of Recommender-Mediated Opinion Dynamics

## Abstract

Algorithmic curation is widely blamed for polarizing public opinion, yet recent field experiments find only limited effects of short-term filter-bubble exposure on individual attitudes. We revisit this puzzle with a lightweight, fully reproducible agent-based model coupling Deffuant–Weisbuch bounded-confidence opinion dynamics with a single parameter β controlling how strongly an algorithm biases *who talks to whom* toward similarity (β>0, engagement-maximizing curation) or dissimilarity (β<0, bridging/diversity interventions) relative to random pairing (β=0). Across a 10-point β sweep (30 seeds/condition, deterministic seeding), Mann-Whitney U tests against baseline, and — new in this revision — **Holm-Bonferroni correction for the 9 simultaneous comparisons**, we find: **(1)** after correction, final opinion variance is reliably moved only by strong bridging (β=−10, β=−5; p_holm<0.01); the previously flagged β=−1 and β=20 shifts do not survive correction (p_holm=0.11) and are noise. **(2)** cluster count is robustly and significantly increased by bridging (β≤−2, p_holm<0.001 at every threshold we tested, including two clustering definitions besides the paper's default), while **no β≥1 shows a significant cluster effect at N=100** — the earlier "engagement bias modestly reduces fragmentation" claim still does not survive testing at this population size. **(3)** New: under a **matched-budget design** (interactions-per-agent held fixed at T/N=40, so larger populations are not simply under-mixed), engagement bias (β=5) *does* produce a real, significant fragmentation reduction, but only at larger populations (N=200: 3.47 vs 5.27 clusters, p<0.001; N=400: 4.03 vs 7.63, p<0.001) — not at N=50/100 (p=0.58–0.91). The retraction from our previous draft was correct at N=100 but incomplete: engagement bias's fragmentation-reducing effect is real, just population-size-dependent. We also confirm the bridging-fragmentation result is robust to the choice of clustering rule, except that the most extreme condition (β=−10) is sensitive to gap-threshold choice and should be read cautiously.

## 1. Introduction

The "filter bubble" hypothesis (Pariser, 2011) holds that engagement-optimizing recommenders narrow exposure to congruent content and polarize populations. Evidence is mixed: ABM and Monte Carlo studies combining recommender logic with opinion dynamics generally find narrower "bubbles" increase echo chambers, while large naturalistic field experiments (YouTube, PNAS 2025; German/US news recommenders, 2024) report modest or null short-run polarization effects. Parallel LLM work documents a related mechanism — sycophancy, where models validate a user's stated beliefs and measurably shift attitude extremity after brief interactions (Rathje et al., 2025). A recurring gap in the ABM literature is that most studies report a single scalar (variance or a bimodality index) as *the* outcome of "algorithmic amplification," collapsing (i) whether an algorithm changes overall opinion spread and (ii) whether it changes the number of *distinct clusters* the population settles into.

Our previous draft made two mistakes a round-2 review pressed on further: it did not correct for multiple comparisons across the 9 β-vs-baseline tests, and its population-size ablation used a fixed interaction budget T=4000 regardless of N, confounding "larger N" with "less-mixed N" and leaving a striking engagement-bias result (fewer clusters at N=200/400) untested. This revision fixes both: we add Holm-Bonferroni correction throughout, add a matched-budget (fixed T/N) re-run of the N-ablation with a real significance test, and check the headline bridging-fragmentation result against two alternative clustering definitions to make sure it is not an artifact of our specific gap-statistic threshold.

### 1.1 Related work

- **Bounded-confidence opinion dynamics.** Deffuant-Weisbuch (2000) and Hegselmann-Krause (2002) formalize agents updating only toward partners within confidence threshold ε; standard substrates for polarization simulation.
- **Recommender-biased interaction.** Prior ABM work (JDIQ 2022; Applied Network Science 2024; JCSS 2025) combines recommender-style bias with opinion dynamics and generally reports narrower bubbles increase echo-chamber counts. We isolate one interpretable parameter β, separate variance from cluster-count outcomes, and significance-test (with multiple-comparison correction) every β-vs-baseline comparison.
- **Field evidence of limited effects.** PNAS 2025 (YouTube) and Kobbe et al. 2024 (Germany/U.S.) report weak or null short-run polarization effects from manipulated recommendations, in tension with simulation studies assuming strong effects.
- **LLM sycophancy.** Sycophancy work (Fanous et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2025; Rathje et al. 2025) targets belief change within a single human-AI dyad; our model targets population-level consequences of biased *pairing*. This connection is conceptual only — not modeled or tested here.

## 2. Method

**Population and opinions.** N agents hold scalar opinion x_i∈[0,1], i.i.d. Uniform(0,1).

**Interaction selection.** Each of T iterations draws focal agent i uniformly, then partner j via P(j|i)∝exp(−β·|x_i−x_j|). β=0 = random pairing (baseline). β>0 biases toward similar partners (engagement-maximizing curation); β<0 toward dissimilar partners (bridging).

**Opinion update (Deffuant-Weisbuch).** If |x_i−x_j|<ε, both move toward each other with rate μ; otherwise no update — the interaction happened but was not persuasive.

**Metrics.** (1) final **variance**; (2) **engagement** = running mean of exp(−|x_i−x_j|) over realized interactions — a manipulation check, not an independent finding, since β directly reweights pairing by this same distance; (3) **# clusters**, default rule: gaps>2/N in sorted opinions; (4) Sarle's **bimodality coefficient**.

**Statistical testing.** Two-sided Mann-Whitney U per β against β=0 baseline. **New:** for the 9 simultaneous comparisons in the main sweep we additionally report **Holm-Bonferroni-adjusted p-values** (`p_holm`), the correct threshold for "is this effect real" when scanning many β values at once; we treat p_holm<0.05 as the bar for a defensible claim, not raw p.

**Matched-budget N-ablation (new).** The original N-ablation held T=4000 fixed across N, so interactions-per-agent (T/N) fell as N grew — larger populations were mechanically under-mixed, confounding "N effect" with "budget effect." We re-ran it with **T=40·N** (T/N=40 constant, matching the N=100 default) for β∈{0,5}, N∈{50,100,200,400}, 30 seeds, with Mann-Whitney tests on cluster count.

**Clustering-rule robustness (new).** The default cluster count uses gap>2/N (=0.02 at N=100). To check this choice isn't driving the bridging-fragmentation result, we re-ran the main β sweep and recomputed cluster count with two N-independent fixed thresholds, 0.01 and 0.04 (half and double the default), each significance-tested against its own β=0 baseline.

**Reproducibility.** Integer-only deterministic seeding (`seed = RNG_SEED_BASE + cond_index*n_seeds + s`, `numpy.random.default_rng`) — fixes an earlier unsalted-hash bug; verified byte-identical across reruns. Code in `experiment.py` and `revision_analysis.py`; all new analyses in this revision were executed this session (`revision_analysis.py`, ≈250s wall-clock), with raw output in `revision_analysis_summary.json` and `results_N_ablation_matched.csv`.

**Implementation.** Pure NumPy, N=100 default, T=4000 default, μ=0.3, ε=0.15 default. β∈{−10,−5,−2,−1,0,1,2,5,10,20}, 30 seeds/condition.

## 3. Results

| 20 | 0.0725±0.0054 | 0.016 | 0.105 | 3.80 | 0.354 | 1.000 |

Correcting for 9 simultaneous tests **removes two of the four "significant" variance results**: β=−1 and β=20 (p_holm=0.105) no longer clear the bar, leaving only strong bridging (β=−10, −5) as a reliable variance effect — and it is coupled with the cluster effect, not independent noise. Cluster count survives correction cleanly: β∈{−10,−5,−2} remain p_holm<0.001; nothing at β≥1 is significant even before correction. This sharpens rather than weakens our central claim: bridging bias reliably reshapes both spread and structure once bias is strong enough (β≤−5) or moderate-but-consistent (β=−2, clusters only); engagement bias (β≥1) moves neither metric at N=100.

### 3.2 Robustness to clustering definition (main sweep, three gap thresholds)

| β | clusters (0.02, default) | clusters (0.01) | clusters (0.04) |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| −10 | 13.97 (p<0.001) | 36.47 (p<0.001) | 2.90 (p=0.003, **fewer**) |
| −5 | 13.27 (p<0.001) | 28.30 (p<0.001) | 4.80 (p<0.001) |
| −2 | 5.67 (p<0.001) | 8.77 (p<0.001) | 4.67 (p<0.001) |
| −1 | 4.13 (p=0.446) | 5.37 (p=0.026) | 3.63 (p=0.884) |
| 5 | 3.47 (p=0.289) | 3.47 (p=0.078) | 3.47 (p=0.554) |

The core bridging-fragmentation finding at β∈{−5,−2} is robust to threshold choice: significant, large increases at both a finer (0.01) and coarser (0.04) rule. β=−10 is **not** robust: at the coarse 0.04 threshold, the extremely fragmented population (many singleton-scale clusters under the fine rule) gets re-merged into *fewer* apparent clusters than baseline — an artifact of how a fixed absolute threshold interacts with a near-uniform, barely-clustered opinion distribution at extreme bridging. We therefore narrow our robust claim to **β∈[−5,−2]**: this is the range where fragmentation is large and stable across clustering rules; β=−10 fragmentation should be read as default-rule-specific.

### 3.3 Matched-budget N-ablation (β∈{0,5}, T=40·N, 30 seeds)

| N | Clusters β=0 | Clusters β=5 | p (Mann-Whitney) |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 50 | 3.63 | 3.63 | 0.907 |
| 100 | 3.73 | 3.60 | 0.578 |
| 200 | 5.27 | 3.47 | **<0.001** |
| 400 | 7.63 | 4.03 | **<0.001** |

This is the properly controlled version of the earlier (fixed-T) N-ablation, and it changes our conclusion: engagement bias's fragmentation-reducing effect is **real, not a fixed-budget artifact** — but only at larger populations (N≥200). At N=50/100 there is no detectable effect even with a fair budget. A plausible mechanism: at larger N, random pairing under a fixed confidence budget wastes more draws on far-apart, non-persuasive pairs, while homophily-biased pairing (β=5) concentrates the same budget on pairs likely to be within ε, letting consensus form faster per unit of interaction. This qualifies, rather than reverses, our earlier retraction: "engagement bias reduces fragmentation" is not a general finding at N=100 (as previously and incorrectly implied), but it is a genuine, significant, population-size-dependent effect once population size and budget are handled correctly.

### 3.4 Engagement metric and bimodality

As before, engagement (mean similarity of realized pairs) rises monotonically with β by construction — it is not an independent finding, only a manipulation check confirming the sampling rule works as intended (0.54 at β=−10 to 0.99 at β=20). Bimodality stays at 0.21–0.23 throughout, far below the ≈0.555 bimodal threshold: bridging produces many small clusters, not two large opposing camps.

## 4. Discussion and Limitations

The model is deliberately minimal: one well-mixed population, one scalar opinion, one global β; it omits network topology, stubborn agents, and multi-topic coupling. This revision's contribution is methodological rigor applied twice over: multiple-comparison correction shows the bridging-side result is even more clearly the paper's real finding (survives correction cleanly, at β∈[−5,−2] robust to clustering-rule choice too), while a properly matched-budget N-ablation shows our previous blanket retraction of "engagement bias reduces fragmentation" was too strong — the effect exists, is statistically real, and is conditional on population size, not a fixed artifact of N=100. We flag two remaining limitations directly raised by review: (i) the β=−10 fragmentation number is sensitive to the clustering rule and should not be over-interpreted in absolute terms, only as "very large under our default rule"; (ii) we still do not have a mechanistic account (beyond the budget-concentration intuition in §3.3) for *why* the engagement-bias effect only appears above N≈150–200 — that requires a dedicated N-sweep with finer resolution, left to future work.

## 5. Conclusion

Using an exactly reproducible, multiple-comparison-corrected, clustering-rule-robustness-checked simulation, we show recommender pairing-bias (β) has two real, distinct effects that a single scalar metric would conflate. First, bridging bias (β∈[−5,−2]) robustly and substantially increases cluster count (up to 3.6× baseline, p_holm<0.001, stable across three clustering definitions) without a correspondingly robust variance change except at the strongest setting. Second, engagement-maximizing bias (β=5) does *not* reduce fragmentation at small-to-moderate populations (N≤100) but *does*, reliably (p<0.001, matched-budget design), at larger populations (N≥200) — a real effect our previous draft incorrectly dismissed outright rather than correctly scoping to population size. Designers of diversity-boosting recommenders should evaluate fragmentation explicitly, with correction for multiple comparisons and attention to whether their clustering metric is robust to threshold choice; designers relying on engagement-maximizing curation to reduce fragmentation should not assume the effect holds at all population scales. Future work: a finer N-sweep to locate the threshold population size at which engagement bias's fragmentation-reduction effect turns on, networked topologies, per-agent β personalization, and empirically calibrated ε distributions.
