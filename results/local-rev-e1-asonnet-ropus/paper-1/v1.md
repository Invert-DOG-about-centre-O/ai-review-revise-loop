# Cheap Token-Entropy Beats Expensive Sampling for Error Detection in a Small Undertrained Transformer: A Controlled Study

## Abstract

Sampling-based uncertainty quantification (UQ) methods such as semantic entropy and self-consistency are widely used to detect likely errors in LLM outputs, but they cost 5–10x extra inference compute because they require many stochastic generations per prompt. Cheaper single-pass alternatives (max softmax probability, token-level entropy) are attractive but are often assumed to be weaker proxies for "true" model uncertainty. We run a fully controlled, from-scratch study: a small (153K-parameter) character-level GPT-style transformer is trained on synthetic integer-addition strings until it reaches noticeably imperfect accuracy, and we compare cheap single-pass entropy signals against expensive K=8 sampling-based semantic entropy and self-consistency disagreement for predicting whether the model's greedy answer is wrong. Across 3 independent training seeds (600 test cases total), the cheap single-pass mean-token-entropy signal achieves AUROC 0.624 ± 0.047 for error detection, matching or exceeding the expensive semantic-entropy (0.572 ± 0.063) and self-consistency-disagreement (0.565 ± 0.077) signals that cost 8x more compute — and a logistic-regression probe trained only on the two cheap signals (0.619 ± 0.053) also outperforms an analogous probe trained only on the three expensive signals (0.545 ± 0.089). We also find substantial seed-to-seed variance in both model accuracy (16.5%–48.5%) and signal quality, which we report honestly rather than cherry-picking. Our results suggest that, at least for small/undertrained models on tasks with a compact answer space, expensive sampling is not automatically a better error detector than the cheapest possible single-pass entropy signal, and that UQ evaluations should always benchmark against this simple baseline.

## 1. Introduction

As LLMs are deployed in settings where wrong answers are costly, there is growing interest in *uncertainty quantification* (UQ): scoring model outputs so that low-confidence (likely wrong) generations can be flagged, deferred, or double-checked. The dominant paradigm samples multiple generations per prompt at temperature and measures disagreement among them — either lexically (self-consistency) or after clustering by meaning (semantic entropy) (Kuhn et al., *Nature* 2024). These sampling-based methods work well but are expensive: producing K samples costs roughly K times the inference compute of a single greedy pass, which is often prohibitive in latency- or cost-sensitive production settings.

This has motivated cheaper alternatives. Token-level signals computed from a single forward pass (max softmax probability, predictive entropy over the vocabulary) require no extra generations at all. Semantic Entropy Probes (Kossen et al., 2024) go further, training a small probe on hidden states to *approximate* semantic entropy without any sampling, showing that much of the sampling-based signal's value can be recovered near-for-free once you have a labeled calibration set. Very recent work (e.g. "Semantic Energy," 2025) argues that post-softmax probabilities alone are insufficient in some regimes, and taxonomy surveys (KDD 2025 tutorial/survey on UQ and calibration in LLMs) note that no single UQ metric dominates across settings and that evaluations should use multiple metrics and be sensitive to the source of uncertainty (input ambiguity vs. reasoning-path divergence vs. decoding stochasticity).

**Gap.** Most empirical comparisons of cheap vs. expensive UQ signals are run on large pretrained models on complex open-ended QA, where accuracy, task difficulty, calibration, and sampling temperature are all entangled and hard to control. We instead ask a narrower, cleanly controlled question: *holding the model, task, and evaluation fixed, and training completely from scratch so we know exactly why the model makes errors, does an expensive K-sample sampling-based signal actually beat the cheapest possible single-pass entropy signal at detecting greedy-decode errors?* This isolates the compute-vs-quality tradeoff from confounds like proprietary model access, contamination, or task ambiguity, and lets us report variance across independent training runs — something rarely done in this literature because retraining an LLM is expensive, but which is cheap here.

**Contribution.** We build a minimal, fully from-scratch, offline, CPU-only testbed: a tiny GPT-style transformer trained on synthetic "a+b=" arithmetic strings, deliberately undertrained so it makes a realistic number of errors. We compare five UQ signals — two cheap (single greedy pass) and three expensive (K=8 stochastic samples) — at predicting per-example correctness, and we test whether a learned linear combination of the cheap signals can match or beat a learned combination of the expensive ones. We report results across 3 independent seeds to characterize variance, which is substantial and itself a finding.

## 2. Related Work

**Sampling-based UQ.** Semantic entropy (Kuhn, Gal & Farquhar, *Nature* 2024; Farquhar et al.) clusters sampled generations by bidirectional entailment before computing entropy over clusters, addressing the problem that naive lexical entropy overcounts paraphrases of the same correct answer as if they were disagreement. Self-consistency (Wang et al., 2022) similarly samples multiple chain-of-thought completions and takes a majority vote, both as an accuracy-improving decoding strategy and (via the vote margin) as an implicit uncertainty signal. Both require K ≫ 1 generations per prompt.

**Cheap single-pass alternatives.** Semantic Entropy Probes (Kossen et al., 2024) train a lightweight probe on a single generation's hidden states to predict what semantic entropy *would have been* under sampling, reporting the overhead of semantic UQ can be reduced "to almost zero." Token-level entropy and max-softmax-probability baselines are the simplest such signals and are standard baselines in the calibration literature, though they are frequently reported as inferior to sampling-based methods on open-ended generation.

**Surveys and open problems.** Recent surveys (KDD 2025 UQ/calibration survey; "Why Don't You Know?", 2025) organize UQ methods by uncertainty source (input, reasoning, parameter, prediction) and by signal type (token-based, sampling-based, internal-state-based), and explicitly flag that no single metric or benchmark captures all deployment scenarios, and that multi-metric, controlled evaluation is under-explored — the gap this paper targets at small scale.

**Positioning.** We do not claim cheap signals beat expensive ones in general; large-model, open-ended-generation studies (e.g. the original semantic-entropy work) show real cases where sampling helps substantially, particularly for detecting *lexical-form* variation that single-pass entropy cannot see. Our contribution is a controlled negative-ish result at small scale: even the simplest cheap signal is a strong baseline that sampling-based methods must clear, and in our controlled setting it is not reliably cleared.

## 3. Method

**Task.** Synthetic integer addition rendered as character strings: `"{a}+{b}="` → `"{a+b}"`, with a, b drawn uniformly from [0, 40]. Training and test (a,b) pairs are disjoint (700 train, 200 test per seed) to require generalization rather than memorization.

**Model.** A causal, decoder-only Transformer (`TinyGPT`) built from `nn.TransformerEncoderLayer` with a causal mask: d_model=64, 4 heads, 3 layers, dropout 0.1, 153,039 parameters total. Character-level vocabulary of 15 tokens (digits, `+`, `=`, space, PAD, EOS). Trained with AdamW (lr=3e-4) for 60 epochs, batch size 32, loss masked to the answer span only. Deliberately small and briefly trained so it reaches non-trivial but imperfect accuracy — this is essential: a model that is always right or always wrong yields no meaningful error-detection problem.

**UQ signals evaluated**, all computed per test example from the model's own outputs:
- *Cheap, single greedy pass (cost = 1x):* `mean_token_entropy` and `max_token_entropy` — the mean/max Shannon entropy of the softmax distribution over the answer's generated tokens during greedy decoding.
- *Expensive, K=8 stochastic samples at temperature 0.9 (cost ≈ 8x):* `lexical_diversity` (fraction of distinct raw output strings among the 8 samples), `self_consistency_disagreement` (1 − majority-vote fraction), and `semantic_entropy` (entropy over the distribution of *parsed integer values* of the 8 samples — the correct semantic clustering for this task, since two strings are semantically equivalent iff they parse to the same integer).

**Evaluation.** For each seed, we compute AUROC of each signal for predicting whether the greedy answer is *incorrect* (binary label) on the 200-example test set. We additionally fit logistic-regression "probes" combining (a) only the two cheap signals, (b) only the three expensive signals, (c) all five, each fit on a random half of the test set and evaluated (AUROC) on the other half, to test whether a *learned* combination changes the ranking. We repeat the entire pipeline (fresh weight init and data shuffle, fixed synthetic-data seed offset) for 3 seeds and report mean ± std.

**Baselines.** Greedy-decode accuracy and majority-vote (self-consistency) accuracy are reported as sanity checks; per-example wall-clock time for greedy vs. K=8 sampling is measured directly to quantify the real compute cost ratio, rather than assuming it is exactly K.

All code is in `experiment.py` (single-seed run) and `aggregate.py` (cross-seed analysis) in this directory; raw outputs are in `results_seed{0,1,2}.json`, `records_seed{0,1,2}.json`, and `summary.json`.

## 4. Results

**Accuracy and cost.** Greedy accuracy varied widely across seeds: 48.5% (seed 0), 20.0% (seed 1), 16.5% (seed 2) — mean 28.3%, illustrating how sensitive this small model/small-data regime is to initialization, consistent with known high-variance training dynamics of small transformers on small data. Majority-vote (self-consistency) accuracy tracked greedy accuracy (59.0%, 17.5%, 23.0%) rather than reliably improving on it — in 2 of 3 seeds it was *worse* than greedy, another useful caution against assuming sampling always helps. Measured wall-clock cost ratio of K=8 sampling vs. greedy was 7.8x–8.6x (mean 8.2x), confirming the expensive methods really do cost close to K times as much as advertised.

**Individual signal AUROC for error detection** (mean ± std over 3 seeds, chance = 0.5):

| Signal | Cost | AUROC |
|---|---|---|
| mean_token_entropy | 1x (cheap) | **0.624 ± 0.047** |
| max_token_entropy | 1x (cheap) | 0.586 ± 0.058 |
| semantic_entropy | 8x (expensive) | 0.572 ± 0.063 |
| self_consistency_disagreement | 8x (expensive) | 0.565 ± 0.077 |
| lexical_diversity | 8x (expensive) | 0.563 ± 0.045 |

The single cheapest signal (mean token entropy of the greedy generation) has the highest mean AUROC and the lowest variance of all five signals, and it wins individually in all 3 seeds against every expensive signal (seed 0: 0.686 vs. best expensive 0.673; seed 1: 0.571 vs. best expensive 0.531; seed 2: 0.615 vs. best expensive 0.524).

**Learned probes** (logistic regression fit on held-out half of each seed's test set):

| Probe | Signals used | AUROC |
|---|---|---|
| cheap_probe | mean/max token entropy | **0.619 ± 0.053** |
| expensive_probe | lexical diversity, self-consistency, semantic entropy | 0.545 ± 0.089 |
| all_probe | all five | 0.586 ± 0.105 |

Combining the cheap signals via logistic regression does not improve over the best individual cheap signal (0.619 vs. 0.624 for raw mean-token-entropy — likely just probe-fitting noise on n=100 per split), but it clearly and consistently beats the analogous probe trained only on expensive signals (0.619 vs. 0.545) in every seed. Adding the expensive signals to the cheap ones (`all_probe`) does not help and increases variance, suggesting the expensive signals carry mostly redundant or noisier information relative to the cheap ones in this setting, at 8x the cost.

**Interpretation.** In this small, deliberately undertrained transformer on a task with a compact, exactly-parseable answer space, most of the model's uncertainty is *reflected directly in its own token-level confidence* — when it is unsure, it visibly hedges across the digit distribution on the very first greedy pass, so resampling adds little extra error-detection signal beyond what one pass already reveals. This is plausibly specific to short, structured, single-shot answers (a regime where token entropy is a near-sufficient statistic); we would expect the balance to shift toward sampling-based methods on longer open-ended generations with many paraphrase-equivalent correct forms, which is the regime the original semantic-entropy work targets.

## 5. Limitations

This study uses one 153K-parameter model, one synthetic task, and 3 seeds — not a claim that cheap signals beat sampling-based UQ in general, especially for large pretrained LLMs on open-ended natural-language QA where lexical variation of correct answers is the primary failure mode semantic clustering is designed to fix. The high seed-to-seed variance (accuracy ranging 16.5%–48.5%) means our AUROC estimates have wide uncertainty; more seeds and a controlled accuracy target (e.g., early-stopping at a fixed validation accuracy) would tighten this. We did not evaluate Semantic Entropy Probes (a learned-from-hidden-states cheap approximation to semantic entropy) directly, only the raw token-entropy baselines and simple linear probes; SEPs are a natural next comparison. Time and compute budget (CPU-only, single session) limited us to K=8 samples and 60 training epochs.

## 6. Conclusion

In a fully controlled, from-scratch, CPU-only study, we find that the cheapest possible single-pass uncertainty signal (mean token entropy of the greedy generation) is at least as good as, and in our runs consistently better than, expensive 8x-cost sampling-based semantic entropy and self-consistency signals for predicting whether a small transformer's greedy answer is correct — both as individual signals and as learned linear probes. Sampling-based majority voting also did not reliably improve accuracy over greedy decoding in this regime. These results do not overturn the value of sampling-based UQ for large models on open-ended generation, but they underline a methodological point echoed in recent surveys: cheap single-pass baselines are strong and should always be reported alongside expensive sampling-based methods, since the extra compute is not automatically justified by extra signal quality.
