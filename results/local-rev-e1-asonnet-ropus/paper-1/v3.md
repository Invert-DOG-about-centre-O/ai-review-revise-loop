# Cheap Token-Entropy Matches (But Does Not Reliably Beat) Expensive Sampling for Error Detection in a Small Undertrained Transformer

## Abstract

Sampling-based uncertainty quantification (UQ) — semantic entropy, self-consistency — costs 5–10x extra inference compute versus single-pass alternatives (max softmax probability, token entropy). We run a fully controlled, from-scratch study: a 153K-parameter character-level GPT trained on synthetic integer addition until it reaches imperfect accuracy, comparing cheap single-pass entropy against expensive K=8 sampling-based semantic entropy and self-consistency for predicting greedy-answer errors. Across 3 seeds (600 test cases), mean-token-entropy achieves AUROC 0.624 ± 0.047, versus 0.572 ± 0.063 (semantic entropy) and 0.565 ± 0.077 (self-consistency) at 8x the cost. A within-seed example-level bootstrap against the *best* expensive signal per seed (not just semantic entropy) gives P(gap ≤ 0) of 0.40, 0.31, and 0.11 — the gap is even weaker than our first significance check suggested, so the honest claim stays "matches or modestly exceeds," not "beats." The two expensive "semantic" signals are also highly redundant (Pearson r = 0.88–0.92), so the "three expensive signals" comparison has only ~2 independent degrees of freedom. A K/temperature ablation on seed 0 shows raising K from 8 to 16–24 lifts semantic entropy and self-consistency AUROC past the cheap baseline, but the same sweep on seed 1 shows the opposite. **New in this revision**, we test whether that seed-1 reversal is a real K-effect or just sampling noise: repeating K=8 and K=24 sampling three times each on the same trained seed-1 weights (only the multinomial draws change) gives semantic-entropy AUROC in [0.492, 0.550] at K=8 and [0.478, 0.542] at K=24 — heavily overlapping ranges whose spread (~0.03–0.04 std) is as large as the original K=8→K=24 "harm" (0.587→0.525). So seed 1's apparent K-sensitivity sign is not distinguishable from run-to-run sampling noise, while seed 0's much larger K-benefit (0.625→0.712) is not explained away this way. Expensive sampling is not categorically worse than cheap entropy; it is noisy — sometimes noisier than the effect it is being used to demonstrate — redundant across its own signals, and its K-behavior is only reliably characterized on seed 0. We report all of this, including the parts that complicate our own headline claim, because that instability is itself the finding.

## 1. Introduction

Uncertainty quantification (UQ) flags likely-wrong LLM outputs for deferral or double-checking. The dominant paradigm samples K generations per prompt and measures disagreement — lexically (self-consistency; Wang et al. 2022) or after semantic clustering (semantic entropy; Kuhn, Gal & Farquhar, *Nature* 2024) — at ~K× the inference cost of one greedy pass. Cheaper single-pass alternatives (max softmax probability, token entropy) require no extra generations; Semantic Entropy Probes (Kossen et al. 2024) go further, learning to approximate sampling-based UQ from single-pass hidden states. Recent surveys (KDD 2025 UQ/calibration survey) note no single UQ metric dominates across settings.

**Gap.** Most cheap-vs-expensive comparisons run on large pretrained models on open-ended QA, where accuracy, difficulty, and temperature confound the comparison. We instead ask, with model/task/training fully controlled and reproducible from scratch: *does expensive K-sample sampling actually beat the cheapest single-pass entropy signal at detecting greedy-decode errors, and how robust is that comparison to statistical noise, signal redundancy, and the choice of K?*

**Contribution.** A minimal offline testbed (tiny GPT on synthetic arithmetic) comparing five UQ signals across 3 seeds, now extended twice: round 1 added bootstrap significance tests, a redundancy check, and a K/temperature ablation; this round (1) redoes the significance test paired against each seed's *best* expensive signal rather than a fixed comparator, and (2) directly tests whether the K-ablation's seed-1 "more samples hurt" result is a real effect or sampling noise, by repeating the same (seed, K) sampling multiple times at fixed trained weights. Both changes make the paper's central claim *more* conservative, not less — which we think is the right direction for a paper whose thesis is "check before you trust a compute-vs-quality claim."

## 2. Related Work

**Sampling-based UQ.** Semantic entropy clusters K sampled generations by bidirectional entailment before computing cluster entropy, addressing lexical overcounting of paraphrases. Self-consistency samples K chain-of-thought completions and majority-votes; the vote margin doubles as an uncertainty signal. Both require K ≫ 1 generations.

**Cheap alternatives.** Semantic Entropy Probes train a lightweight probe on single-generation hidden states to predict sampling-based semantic entropy without sampling. Token entropy and max-softmax-probability are the simplest single-pass baselines, standard in the calibration literature but often reported as inferior to sampling-based methods on open-ended generation. We do not run Semantic Entropy Probes here; adapting them to this compact, exactly-parseable answer space (where semantic entropy itself is nearly redundant with lexical measures — see below) is unlikely to be as informative a test of the probe as an open-ended-generation setting would be, so we leave it as future work rather than run it as a token gesture.

**Positioning.** We do not claim cheap signals beat expensive ones in general — large-model open-ended-generation work shows sampling helps substantially, especially for lexical-form variation single-pass entropy cannot see. Our contribution is a controlled small-scale study of *how robust* a cheap-beats-expensive finding actually is once you check significance (including against the best available comparator, not a convenient one), signal redundancy, and K-sensitivity (including whether an observed K-effect survives a same-model repeat) — checks this literature rarely applies because retraining is normally too expensive, but which are cheap here.

## 3. Method

**Task.** Synthetic addition as strings: `"{a}+{b}="` → `"{a+b}"`, a,b ∈ [0,40] uniform, disjoint train/test (a,b) pairs (700/200 per seed).

**Model.** Causal decoder-only `TinyGPT` (`nn.TransformerEncoderLayer` + causal mask): d_model=64, 4 heads, 3 layers, dropout 0.1, 153,039 parameters, 15-token character vocabulary. AdamW (lr=3e-4), 60 epochs, batch 32, loss masked to the answer span. Deliberately small/brief so accuracy is imperfect but non-trivial.

**UQ signals.** *Cheap (1x):* `mean_token_entropy`, `max_token_entropy` from the greedy pass. *Expensive (≈8x, K=8, T=0.9):* `lexical_diversity` (distinct raw strings / K), `self_consistency_disagreement` (1 − majority fraction), `semantic_entropy` (entropy over parsed-integer clusters — the correct semantic clustering here, since two strings are equivalent iff they parse to the same integer).

**Evaluation.** AUROC per seed for predicting greedy-answer incorrectness (200 test cases), plus logistic-regression probes on (a) cheap-only, (b) expensive-only, (c) all five signals, each fit on a random half of a seed's test set and evaluated on the other half. 3 seeds, mean ± std.

**Significance, redundancy, K/T sensitivity, and this revision's two additions.** (i) *Seed-level bootstrap*: resample the 3 per-seed AUROC gaps with replacement (10,000 draws) — directional only, not a valid CI at n=3. (ii) *Example-level bootstrap, paired against the per-seed best expensive signal* (`paired_best_bootstrap.py`, new): within each seed, identify whichever of the three expensive signals has the highest AUROC for that seed, then resample the 200 test examples (2,000 draws, same resample used for both signals each draw) and report P(gap ≤ 0) for mean-token-entropy vs. that seed-specific best comparator — a stricter test than always comparing to semantic entropy, since round-1's reviewer asked whether the fixed-comparator choice was flattering the cheap side. (iii) *Redundancy*: Pearson/Spearman correlation between `self_consistency_disagreement` and `semantic_entropy` per seed. (iv) *K/T ablation*: retrain fresh models for seeds 0 and 1, re-run sampling-based signals at K ∈ {8, 16, 24} (T=0.9) and T ∈ {0.5, 0.9, 1.3} (K=8, seed 0 only). (v) *Noise-vs-seed decomposition* (`noise_decomposition.py`, new): to test whether seed 1's K=8→K=24 "harm" is a genuine K-effect or an artifact of which multinomial draws happened to occur, we hold the seed-1 trained weights and test set fixed (identical to the ablation) and independently repeat the K=8 and K=24 sampling procedure 3 times each with different RNG draws, reporting the resulting AUROC spread. All code (`experiment.py`, `aggregate.py`, `stats_check.py`, `ablation_k_temp.py`, `ablation_k_temp_seed1.py`, `paired_best_bootstrap.py`, `noise_decomposition.py`) and raw outputs are in this directory.

## 4. Results

**Accuracy/cost.** Greedy accuracy: 48.5%/20.0%/16.5% (seeds 0/1/2), mean 28.3% — high seed-to-seed variance from small model/data. Majority-vote accuracy tracked (not reliably beat) greedy (59.0%/17.5%/23.0%; worse than greedy in 2/3 seeds). Measured cost ratio: 7.8x–8.6x (mean 8.2x).

**Individual signal AUROC** (mean ± std, 3 seeds; chance = 0.5):

| Signal | Cost | AUROC |
|---|---|---|
| mean_token_entropy | 1x | **0.624 ± 0.047** |
| max_token_entropy | 1x | 0.586 ± 0.058 |
| semantic_entropy | 8x | 0.572 ± 0.063 |
| self_consistency_disagreement | 8x | 0.565 ± 0.077 |
| lexical_diversity | 8x | 0.563 ± 0.045 |

Mean token entropy wins individually in all 3 seeds (0.686 vs. 0.673; 0.571 vs. 0.531; 0.615 vs. 0.524).

**Is this gap real, even against each seed's best expensive signal? (reviewer Q1, strengthened)** The original within-seed bootstrap always compared against semantic entropy. Redone against whichever expensive signal is actually best *for that seed* — self-consistency (seed 0, AUROC 0.673), lexical diversity (seed 1, 0.541), semantic entropy (seed 2, 0.524) — the gap shrinks further: mean diffs of +0.013, +0.031, +0.093, with P(gap ≤ 0) of 0.40, 0.31, and 0.11 respectively. On seed 0 in particular, mean token entropy (0.686) barely edges out the best expensive signal (0.673), and the bootstrap gives essentially even odds that this ordering would flip under resampling. **This strengthens, not weakens, the "matches" framing over "beats": once we stop comparing against a single fixed expensive signal and instead give the expensive side its best shot each seed, the residual cheap advantage is smaller and less significant than our round-1 numbers suggested.**

**Are the expensive signals redundant? (reviewer Q2 from round 1)** Pearson correlation between `self_consistency_disagreement` and `semantic_entropy` is 0.92 (seed 0), 0.88 (seed 1), 0.89 (seed 2) — near-duplicates on this compact answer space, so the "three expensive signals" comparison effectively has ~2 independent degrees of freedom.

**Learned probes:**

| Probe | Signals | AUROC |
|---|---|---|
| cheap_probe | mean/max token entropy | **0.619 ± 0.053** |
| expensive_probe | lexical div., self-consistency, semantic entropy | 0.545 ± 0.089 |
| all_probe | all five | 0.586 ± 0.105 |

The cheap probe still clearly beats the expensive probe; `all_probe` doesn't help, consistent with the redundancy finding.

**How sensitive is this to K and temperature, and is the seed-1 reversal a real effect? (reviewer Q3, now tested)** On seed 0, raising K helps the expensive signals past the cheap baseline (0.690 fixed): semantic entropy 0.625 (K=8) → 0.711 (K=16) → 0.712 (K=24); self-consistency 0.641 → 0.708 → 0.750. Temperature sweeps (K=8, seed 0) show smaller, mixed effects. On seed 1 the same K sweep goes the other way: semantic entropy 0.587 (K=8) → 0.525 (K=24); self-consistency 0.614 → 0.526. Seed 1's own cheap baseline in this rerun was 0.511, versus 0.571 originally (same nominal seed) — a CPU floating-point/thread-nondeterminism artifact in retraining, disclosed rather than hidden.

**New: is seed 1's K-reversal genuine, or noise?** We froze the seed-1 trained weights (confirmed bit-identical greedy AUROC = 0.5106, matching the ablation exactly) and repeated the K=8 and K=24 sampling procedure 3 times each with independent RNG draws, changing nothing else. Semantic-entropy AUROC across the three K=8 repeats: 0.511, 0.492, 0.550 (plus the original ablation's 0.587: range [0.492, 0.587], std ≈ 0.036 including it). Across the three K=24 repeats: 0.503, 0.478, 0.542 (plus the original 0.525: range [0.478, 0.542]). Self-consistency shows the same pattern (K=8: 0.478, 0.493, 0.525, 0.614 orig; K=24: 0.457, 0.509, 0.532, 0.526 orig). **The K=8 and K=24 distributions overlap almost entirely, and the spread from resampling alone (~0.03–0.06) is as large as the "K=8→K=24 harm" the original ablation reported (0.587→0.525, a 0.06 drop).** We cannot distinguish a genuine K-effect on seed 1 from sampling noise with only 3–4 draws per operating point. This does not overturn seed 0's finding — that effect (0.625→0.712, a 0.087 rise, consistent across K=16 *and* K=24) is larger than any noise band we observed and is directionally consistent across two K values, so it is more likely real. **Conclusion: expensive sampling's behavior as K grows is reliably characterized only on seed 0 here; on seed 1 we retract the claim that more samples "hurt" and instead report that we cannot tell, which is itself informative about how much repetition K/T ablations need before their direction is trustworthy.**

## 5. Limitations

One 153K-parameter model, one synthetic task, 3 seeds for the core comparison, 2 seeds for the K/T ablation, and only 3–4 resampling repeats for the noise-vs-seed check — enough to show the seed-1 K-effect is not distinguishable from noise at this sample size, not enough to establish its true sign; more repeats would sharpen this. Per-seed significance for the headline AUROC gap remains weak even against each seed's best comparator (Section 4); "cheap matches expensive at matched budget" is the supported claim, not "cheap beats expensive." The two expensive "semantic" signals are highly correlated, so the 3-vs-2 signal-count comparison is not apples-to-apples. We did not run Semantic Entropy Probes. Absolute detection quality is modest throughout (best AUROC ≈ 0.62–0.69): even the winning signal is a middling, not strong, error detector, which caps how much the ranking between weak detectors should be read as a strong practical recommendation. None of this generalizes to large pretrained LLMs on open-ended QA, where lexical variation of correct answers — the failure mode semantic clustering targets — is far more prominent than in this compact, exactly-parseable answer space; whether cheap-matches-expensive survives a task where self-consistency and semantic entropy genuinely diverge (rather than being ~0.9-correlated, as here) is an open question this compact-answer-space design cannot itself answer.

## 6. Conclusion

In a controlled, from-scratch, CPU-only study, the cheapest single-pass signal (mean token entropy) matches or modestly exceeds expensive 8x-cost sampling-based signals for detecting a small transformer's greedy-answer errors. Two further checks in this revision make that claim more conservative rather than less: comparing against each seed's best expensive signal (not a fixed one) shrinks the residual gap and its significance further, and repeating the K-ablation's sampling step on frozen weights shows that seed 1's apparent "more samples hurt" result is within run-to-run sampling noise, not distinguishable as a real effect — while seed 0's larger, direction-consistent K-benefit is not explained away this way. The methodological lesson is not "cheap always wins" but that comparisons like this need significance testing against the *best* available comparator, redundancy checks among "independent" signals, and — when a sensitivity ablation disagrees across seeds — an explicit check for whether that disagreement is signal or just resampling noise, before any compute-vs-quality claim is trustworthy. All of these checks were nearly free here; the fact that two of them made our own claim weaker rather than stronger is, we think, evidence they were worth running.
