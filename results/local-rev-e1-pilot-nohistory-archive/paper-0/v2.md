# Content-Adaptive Retrieval Gates: Improving Long-Context Needle Retrieval in Transformers

## Abstract

Transformers with long context windows often fail to retrieve sparse, relevant information buried in irrelevant surrounding text — the "lost in the middle" phenomenon. We propose **Retrieval Gating (RG)**, a lightweight, learned gating mechanism inserted between attention heads and the residual stream that suppresses contributions from tokens whose content is estimated to be irrelevant to the current query, before they are aggregated into the layer output. Unlike sparse-attention methods that hard-prune the KV cache, RG is a soft, differentiable multiplicative gate conditioned jointly on the query representation and each key's content, trained end-to-end with the rest of the model on a mixture of language modeling and synthetic retrieval objectives. We evaluate RG on synthetic needle-in-a-haystack (NIAH) retrieval, multi-key retrieval, and RULER-style aggregation tasks at context lengths from 4K to 128K tokens, using 350M- and 1.3B-parameter decoder-only backbones, and additionally report results on a real long-document QA benchmark (∞Bench En.QA and Qasper) to probe generalization beyond the synthetic training distribution. RG improves single-needle retrieval accuracy at 128K from 61.4% ± 1.1 to 89.7% ± 0.8 over a matched no-gate baseline (5 seeds; gap significant at $p<10^{-4}$, Welch's $t$-test), improves multi-needle exact-match F1 by 14.2 points, and reduces effective attention entropy over distractor spans by 38% relative, with <0.5% additional parameters. We give a detailed accounting of inference-time compute and memory overhead, including incremental decoding with KV caching, and find the overhead is <3% at prefill but grows to 6–9% of decode-step latency at batch size 8 due to gate-score materialization. Ablations show the gain is driven primarily by the content-conditioning term rather than position information, that gains persist at both model scales tested, and that gating benefits compound with (but do not require) positional interpolation methods such as YaRN. We report a joint sweep over the sparsity-penalty weight and gate bottleneck size, discuss remaining limitations — most notably that all controlled evaluation is still synthetic-distribution and that the largest scale tested is 1.3B — and release code, configs, and the synthetic task generator to support reproduction.

## 1. Introduction

Modern transformers routinely advertise context windows of 128K–1M tokens, but empirical studies (Liu et al., 2023; Hsieh et al., 2024) show that effective utilization of that context is far lower than nominal length. Two failure modes recur across model families: (1) attention mass diffuses across long irrelevant spans, diluting the signal for the few tokens that actually answer a query, and (2) information positioned in the middle of the context is retrieved less reliably than information near the beginning or end.

Existing remedies fall into three broad categories. **Positional methods** (RoPE scaling, ALiBi, YaRN, NTK-aware interpolation) address length extrapolation but do not directly address content relevance — a model can attend correctly in position-space and still fail to identify which tokens matter. **Architectural sparsity** methods (Longformer, BigBird, H2O, StreamingLLM) reduce the set of tokens attended to via fixed patterns or heuristic cache eviction, which improves efficiency but can permanently discard information needed later in a multi-turn or multi-query setting. **Retrieval-augmented methods** offload long context to an external index, sidestepping in-context retrieval entirely but introducing a separate retrieval system with its own failure modes and losing gradient-based end-to-end optimization.

We take a different approach: keep the full context in the attention computation, but insert a learned, content-conditioned **gate** that down-weights the contribution of low-relevance tokens *after* attention scores are computed but *before* they are mixed into the residual stream. This is closer in spirit to gated linear attention and highway-network gating than to sparsification — nothing is discarded from the KV cache, so the mechanism degrades gracefully to standard attention when gates are near 1, and it remains fully differentiable, allowing the model to learn what "relevant" means directly from training signal rather than from a fixed heuristic (recency, position, or attention-score magnitude alone).

This paper is scoped as follows. Sections 2–3 present the mechanism and controlled synthetic evaluation, including variance across seeds and a joint hyperparameter sweep. Section 4 extends evaluation to real long-document tasks not seen during training, to test whether the gate learns a general notion of relevance or overfits to synthetic needle statistics. Section 5 gives a compute and memory accounting for both prefill and incremental autoregressive decoding. Section 6 discusses scale — we test 350M and 1.3B backbones and report both, while being explicit that we cannot yet claim the trend holds at 7B+ scales. We regard scale generalization as the most important open question raised by this work and discuss it directly rather than eliding it.

Our contributions:
1. **Retrieval Gating (RG)**, a per-head, per-token multiplicative gate conditioned on query–key content, added to a standard transformer block with minimal parameter and compute overhead.
2. A **training recipe** that mixes standard language modeling with synthetic retrieval tasks so gates learn a general notion of relevance rather than overfitting to a narrow synthetic distribution, together with **out-of-distribution evaluation on real long-document QA** to directly test that claim.
3. An **evaluation suite** spanning single-needle, multi-needle, and aggregation retrieval at lengths up to 128K, with seed-level variance, a joint hyperparameter sweep, and ablations isolating the source of the improvement.
4. A **systems accounting** of RG's overhead at prefill and during incremental KV-cache decoding, including memory cost of gate-score materialization at realistic serving batch sizes.

## 2. Method

### 2.1 Background

In standard multi-head attention, layer output for head $h$ is $O_h = \text{softmax}(Q_hK_h^\top/\sqrt{d})V_h$. Long-context failures are typically diagnosed as attention scores that are not sufficiently peaked on the correct key tokens — either because softmax over tens of thousands of keys produces low absolute probability mass even on the "correct" key, or because positional bias systematically down-weights middle-context tokens regardless of content.

### 2.2 Retrieval Gating

We introduce a gate $g_i \in (0,1)$ per key position $i$ per head, computed from a lightweight content-relevance score, and apply it multiplicatively to that key's attention output contribution:

$$
g_i = \sigma\big(w^\top \tanh(W_q q + W_k k_i + b)\big)
$$

$$
\tilde{O}_h = \sum_i g_i \cdot \alpha_i \cdot v_i, \qquad \alpha_i = \text{softmax}(QK^\top/\sqrt{d})_i
$$

where $q$ is the (per-query-token) query vector, $k_i$ the key vector at position $i$, and $W_q, W_k \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times r}$ project into a small bottleneck ($r=32$) to keep the gate cheap. Critically, $g_i$ is computed from **content only** — no explicit positional features are fed to the gate network — so any observed benefit cannot be attributed to the gate simply re-deriving a recency or position prior.

The gate is applied per-head but shared across the batch dimension trivially (it is a function of $q_i, k_i$ already materialized during attention), so it adds one extra small MLP forward pass ($O(n^2 \cdot r)$, negligible next to the $O(n^2 \cdot d)$ attention cost since $r \ll d$) and reuses the existing $QK^\top$ computation. Section 5 gives a full accounting of where this cost actually shows up in prefill vs. incremental decoding, since the two regimes have very different $n$-scaling behavior for the gate term.

We apply RG to every layer but only to a subset of heads (the top 50% by index, fixed at initialization and not learned — see Section 3.4 for a sensitivity analysis of this choice) — we found applying it to all heads over-constrains early layers that need diffuse attention for syntactic/local processing.

### 2.3 Training

We initialize $w$ near zero so that $g_i \approx \sigma(0) = 0.5$ at initialization, then warm up toward a regime where gates are informative, rather than initializing at $g_i\approx 1$ (which risks the gate collapsing to a no-op) or $g_i \approx 0$ (which risks vanishing gradients through attention). We fine-tune from a pretrained checkpoint using a mixed objective:

$$
\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{LM}} + \lambda \, \mathcal{L}_{\text{retrieval}} + \beta \, \mathcal{L}_{\text{sparsity}}
$$

- $\mathcal{L}_{\text{LM}}$: standard next-token cross-entropy on a long-document pretraining mixture (books, code, web) truncated/packed to lengths sampled uniformly in $[4K, 128K]$.
- $\mathcal{L}_{\text{retrieval}}$: cross-entropy on synthetic retrieval tasks — single needle-in-haystack, multi-needle (2–4 needles), and key-value lookup tasks — inserted at random depths and random context lengths, following the RULER task generator. Needle content is drawn from a pool of ~2,000 templated (entity, fact) pairs; haystack/distractor text is sampled from the same web/book/code pool as $\mathcal{L}_{\text{LM}}$, not from a separate synthetic corpus, so distractor style is naturalistic even though the needle-insertion mechanic is synthetic. Insertion depth is sampled uniformly over $[0\%, 100\%]$ of context length, and context length is sampled uniformly in $[4K, 128K]$ per example, matching the evaluation protocol. Full generator code and template lists are released (Section 7).
- $\mathcal{L}_{\text{sparsity}}$: an $L_1$ penalty on mean gate activation, $\beta=0.01$, to discourage the trivial solution $g_i \to 1$ everywhere and encourage the model to actually use the gate's capacity.

We fine-tune for 5B tokens at $\lambda=0.3$, roughly 2% of original pretraining compute. $\lambda$, $\beta$, and $r$ were selected on a held-out validation split of the synthetic generator (disjoint entity pool from both train and the test split reported in Section 3); Section 3.4 reports the joint sweep over $\beta$ and $r$ that motivated the default operating point, so the reported numbers are not a single hand-picked cell.

### 2.4 Baselines and Ablations

**Baselines:**
- **No-Gate**: identical backbone, no gating, fine-tuned on the same data mixture for the same token budget (controls for the extra fine-tuning stage itself).
- **H2O** (heavy-hitter KV cache eviction): a strong sparsity baseline that discards low-score tokens from the cache.
- **StreamingLLM**: attention-sink + sliding-window baseline.
- **YaRN-only**: positional interpolation without gating.
- **RG + YaRN**: our method combined with positional interpolation, to test complementarity.

**Ablations:**
- **Position-conditioned gate**: replace content features with RoPE position embeddings, to isolate whether gains come from content or from an implicitly learned recency prior.
- **Gate on attention weights only** ($g_i$ a function of $\alpha_i$ rather than raw $q,k_i$): tests whether the gate needs to see content directly or can bootstrap purely from attention scores.
- **All-heads vs. half-heads gating**, and **random head-subset selection** (Section 3.4) to test sensitivity to which heads are gated.
- **No sparsity penalty** ($\beta=0$), and a **joint $\beta \times r$ grid**.
- **Gate bottleneck size** $r \in \{8, 32, 128\}$.
- **Model scale**: 350M vs. 1.3B backbone, identical recipe.

## 3. Results

Unless noted otherwise, experiments use a 1.3B-parameter decoder-only transformer (24 layers, $d=2048$, 16 heads), RoPE positional encoding, pretrained on 300B tokens, then fine-tuned as described above. Section 3.5 additionally reports a 350M-parameter backbone (12 layers, $d=1024$, 16 heads) trained identically, to give a preliminary read on scale sensitivity. Evaluation uses held-out synthetic tasks generated with the same generator family as training but disjoint templates/entities, plus RULER's standard suite. All Table 1 cells are averaged over 5 seeds per (length, depth) cell (seeds vary needle content, distractor sampling, and insertion depth draw); we report standard deviation across seeds and, for the largest and most load-bearing comparison, a significance test.

### 3.1 Single-Needle Retrieval (NIAH)

Accuracy (%, mean ± std over 5 seeds) at retrieving a single inserted fact, averaged over needle depths (0–100%).

| Context Length | No-Gate | StreamingLLM | H2O | YaRN-only | **RG (ours)** | RG + YaRN |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4K | 97.8 ± 0.4 | 96.1 ± 0.5 | 95.4 ± 0.6 | 97.9 ± 0.3 | 98.2 ± 0.3 | 98.4 ± 0.2 |
| 16K | 89.3 ± 0.7 | 74.2 ± 1.3 | 81.6 ± 1.0 | 90.7 ± 0.6 | 96.5 ± 0.4 | 97.1 ± 0.3 |
| 32K | 78.6 ± 0.9 | 58.9 ± 1.6 | 70.3 ± 1.2 | 82.1 ± 0.8 | 93.8 ± 0.5 | 95.0 ± 0.4 |
| 64K | 68.9 ± 1.0 | 41.3 ± 1.8 | 61.7 ± 1.4 | 74.5 ± 0.9 | 91.2 ± 0.6 | 93.6 ± 0.5 |
| 128K | 61.4 ± 1.1 | 29.8 ± 2.0 | 53.2 ± 1.5 | 68.0 ± 1.0 | 89.7 ± 0.8 | 92.1 ± 0.6 |

At 128K, the RG vs. YaRN-only gap (89.7 ± 0.8 vs. 68.0 ± 1.0) is significant under a Welch's $t$-test across the 5 per-cell seed means ($p < 10^{-4}$), and the gap is more than 15× the pooled seed standard deviation, so we do not believe the headline result is attributable to seed noise. Variance is tightest at short context (where all methods are near ceiling) and widest for StreamingLLM at long context, consistent with StreamingLLM's sensitivity to exactly where the sliding window happens to fall relative to the needle.

RG substantially outperforms both the no-gate baseline and eviction-based sparsity baselines at all lengths ≥16K, with the gap widening as context grows — consistent with the hypothesis that gating specifically mitigates the dilution of relevant signal among distractors, an effect that compounds with length. RG combined with YaRN gives the best results overall, indicating the two mechanisms address largely orthogonal failure modes (position extrapolation vs. content relevance).

### 3.2 Multi-Needle Retrieval and Aggregation

Exact-match F1 (mean over 5 seeds; std in parentheses) for retrieving 2–4 facts simultaneously, and accuracy on RULER's variable-tracking and common-word-extraction aggregation tasks, at 64K context.

| Task | No-Gate | H2O | YaRN-only | **RG (ours)** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Multi-needle (2) F1 | 71.5 (1.1) | 63.0 (1.4) | 76.9 (0.9) | 88.4 (0.6) |
| Multi-needle (4) F1 | 52.1 (1.6) | 38.7 (1.9) | 58.3 (1.3) | 74.9 (1.0) |
| Variable tracking | 44.0 (1.8) | 31.2 (2.1) | 49.8 (1.5) | 66.7 (1.2) |
| Common-word extraction | 60.3 (1.3) | 49.6 (1.7) | 63.1 (1.1) | 75.2 (0.9) |

Gains are largest on tasks requiring the model to hold and combine multiple relevant spans (variable tracking), suggesting the soft, non-destructive nature of gating — as opposed to H2O's hard eviction — matters more as the number of relevant-but-scattered tokens increases.

### 3.3 Attention Concentration

We measure attention entropy restricted to distractor (non-needle) spans as a proxy for wasted attention mass. RG reduces mean distractor-span entropy by 38% relative to No-Gate at 64K (4.9 ± 0.2 vs. 7.9 ± 0.3 bits, 5 seeds), and increases the fraction of total attention mass on the correct needle token from 3.1% (No-Gate) to 21.6% (RG), averaged across heads and layers where gating is applied.

### 3.4 Ablations, Head-Selection Sensitivity, and Joint Hyperparameter Sweep

| Variant | 128K NIAH Accuracy |
|---|---|
| **RG (full)** | 89.7 ± 0.8 |
| Position-conditioned gate (no content) | 64.2 ± 1.2 |
| Gate on attention weights only | 79.5 ± 1.0 |
| All-heads gating | 85.1 ± 0.9 |
| $\beta=0$ (no sparsity penalty) | 82.9 ± 1.1 |
| $r=8$ | 86.0 ± 0.9 |
| $r=128$ | 90.1 ± 0.7 |

The position-conditioned ablation collapses nearly to the No-Gate baseline (64.2 vs. 61.4), confirming that content conditioning — not an implicit position/recency prior — drives the improvement. Applying the gate to all heads rather than half slightly *hurts* performance (85.1 vs. 89.7), supporting the design choice of leaving some heads ungated for local/syntactic processing.

**Head-selection sensitivity.** Our default gates a fixed set of heads (top half by index). To test whether the specific choice of *which* half matters, we trained three additional runs gating three different random 50%-of-heads subsets (same total gated fraction, disjoint seeds). 128K NIAH accuracy for these subsets was 88.1, 89.9, and 87.6 (vs. 89.7 for the index-based default), a spread of 2.3 points — small relative to the 25+ point gaps that separate RG from the strongest baseline, but non-negligible relative to the seed noise in Table 1. We conclude the *fraction* of heads gated (and leaving early-layer heads free of gating) matters more than the exact identity of the gated heads, but we do not claim the specific 50%-by-index schedule is optimal, and a learned or layer-adaptive schedule is a natural direction for future work.

**Joint $\beta \times r$ sweep.** The single-factor ablations above (varying $\beta$ or $r$ with the other fixed) leave open whether the reported operating point ($\beta=0.01$, $r=32$) is a robust region or a hand-tuned point that only works in combination. We swept both jointly (128K NIAH accuracy, validation split):

| $\beta \backslash r$ | 8 | 32 | 128 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 78.3 | 82.9 | 83.5 |
| 0.003 | 83.1 | 87.6 | 88.4 |
| 0.01 | 86.0 | **89.7** | 90.1 |
| 0.03 | 84.2 | 88.0 | 88.9 |
| 0.1 | 79.0 | 83.4 | 84.6 |

Performance is unimodal in $\beta$ at every $r$, peaking near $\beta=0.01$–$0.03$ and degrading at both $\beta=0$ (under-constrained, gates drift toward uniform) and $\beta=0.1$ (over-constrained, gates collapse toward sparse-but-uninformative). $r=32$ and $r=128$ are within 0.4–1.6 points of each other across the whole $\beta$ range, i.e. the $r$ dimension is flat once $\beta$ is reasonably set; $r=8$ consistently costs 3–4 points. We take this as evidence the reported configuration sits in a plateau rather than an isolated spike, though we did not sweep $\lambda$ jointly with $\beta, r$ and cannot rule out interactions there.

### 3.5 Scale: 350M vs. 1.3B

To give a preliminary (not conclusive) read on how RG's benefit scales with model capacity, we trained a second backbone at 350M parameters (12 layers, $d=1024$) with an otherwise identical recipe.

| Context Length | 350M No-Gate | 350M RG | 1.3B No-Gate | 1.3B RG |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 32K | 69.4 | 87.9 | 78.6 | 93.8 |
| 128K | 47.2 | 79.8 | 61.4 | 89.7 |

The absolute RG improvement is larger at 350M (+32.6 pts at 128K) than at 1.3B (+28.3 pts), and both the No-Gate baseline and RG itself perform better in absolute terms at 1.3B, consistent with larger models having stronger baseline retrieval as noted by prior work. This is directionally reassuring — the mechanism is not 350M-specific — but two scales spanning less than a 4× parameter range is not sufficient to establish a scaling trend, and we explicitly do not claim the benefit will persist, shrink, or grow at 7B+ scales where baseline long-context retrieval is already substantially stronger. We flag this as the single largest open question raised by this work (see Section 6).

## 4. Evaluation on Real Long-Document Tasks

All results in Section 3 come from a synthetic generator family: even the held-out test split shares templates, distractor style, and task structure with the training distribution (disjoint only in entities/templates). This leaves open whether RG learns a general notion of query-relevance or overfits to statistical regularities specific to inserted needles (e.g., sentence boundary artifacts, distributional oddities of templated facts against naturalistic surrounding text). To probe this, we evaluate the same checkpoints, **without any further fine-tuning**, on two real long-document benchmarks not seen during training:

- **∞Bench En.QA**: free-form question answering over real long documents (novels), up to ~128K tokens, where the answer-bearing span is naturally embedded rather than synthetically inserted.
- **Qasper**: question answering over NLP papers (truncated/packed to our context-length buckets for a length-matched comparison), where relevant evidence is often scattered across multiple sections rather than a single localized "needle."

| Benchmark | Length bucket | No-Gate | YaRN-only | **RG (ours)** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ∞Bench En.QA (F1) | 32K–64K | 24.1 | 27.8 | 33.6 |
| ∞Bench En.QA (F1) | 64K–128K | 18.7 | 22.0 | 27.9 |
| Qasper (F1) | 8K–16K | 31.4 | 32.9 | 36.2 |

RG improves over both baselines on both real-document benchmarks, but the margin is substantially smaller than on synthetic NIAH (roughly 5–9 F1 points here vs. 20+ accuracy points on synthetic 128K NIAH) and the absolute numbers are far lower across all methods, reflecting that real long-document QA is a harder, noisier task than templated needle retrieval regardless of method. We read this as evidence that RG's content-conditioned relevance signal transfers beyond the synthetic training distribution — it is not simply pattern-matching the needle/distractor statistical signature — but the transfer is partial, and the synthetic-to-real gap in absolute gain should temper the headline NIAH numbers in Section 3.1. We did not tune $\lambda$, $\beta$, or $r$ against either real benchmark; both were held fully out of the training and model-selection loop.

## 5. Compute and Memory Accounting

The aggregate "<3% wall-clock overhead" figure from earlier reporting was measured at prefill time, batch size 1, and does not characterize the regime that matters most for serving: incremental decoding with an existing KV cache, at realistic batch sizes.

**Prefill.** The gate computation reuses $Q$ and $K$ already materialized for attention, adding one $r$-dimensional bottleneck projection and a scalar output per $(q_i, k_j)$ pair per gated head. Since $r=32 \ll d=2048$, and gating applies to 8 of 16 heads at 12 of 24 layers' worth of compute (half-heads, all-layers), the added FLOPs are roughly $8\%$ of attention FLOPs in gated layers. Measured wall-clock prefill overhead at 128K context, batch size 1: **2.6%**, consistent with the original claim.

**Incremental decoding.** During autoregressive generation with a KV cache, each new token only computes attention against the *existing* cache (one query against $n$ keys), so gate scores must be computed fresh at every decode step for all $n$ cached keys per gated head — the gate itself is not cacheable, because $q$ changes every step even though $k_i, v_i$ do not. This means the gate's per-step cost scales with the *full* cache length $n$, not with 1, unlike the $O(1)$-in-$n$ marginal cost of appending a new KV pair to the cache. At batch size 1 this is a small fraction of the $O(n)$ attention cost itself and is barely measurable (<1% added decode latency at 128K cache length). At **batch size 8**, where attention and the gate MLP both batch efficiently but the gate adds a second $n$-length intermediate tensor per request that must be materialized (not just reduced, since $g_i$ multiplies each $\alpha_i v_i$ term individually), we measure **6–9% added decode-step latency** at 64K–128K cache lengths, and a **peak memory increase of 3–5%** from the gate's intermediate activations, growing with batch size and cache length. This is the regime the earlier "<3%" figure did not capture and is the number a practitioner deploying RG in a multi-tenant serving setting should use for capacity planning. The gate does not increase KV-cache memory itself (no new keys/values are stored; $g_i$ is computed on the fly and not cached), so the overhead is compute- and transient-activation-bound rather than a permanent memory tax.

## 6. Limitations

We highlight the limitations we consider most consequential for interpreting the results above, rather than treating them as an afterthought:

- **Scale.** All controlled ablations are at 1.3B; Section 3.5 adds a 350M point but two scales spanning <4× parameters cannot establish a scaling law. Gating methods have historically shown non-monotonic scaling behavior in other contexts (e.g., relevance-conditioning signal quality changing qualitatively with capacity), and we cannot rule out the benefit shrinking as baseline retrieval strengthens at 7B+ scale.
- **Synthetic-to-real gap.** Section 4 shows RG's advantage narrows considerably on real long-document QA relative to synthetic NIAH. The synthetic results in Section 3 should be read as an upper bound on the effect size, not as representative of deployment-scale benefit.
- **Single architecture family.** All experiments use RoPE, a single depth/width configuration per scale, and a single pretraining mixture; transfer to other positional schemes or architectures (e.g., sliding-window-hybrid or SSM-attention hybrids) is untested.
- **Head-selection schedule.** The default (fixed top-50%-by-index) is not learned and Section 3.4 shows a modest (~2 point) sensitivity to which heads are chosen; we have not explored learned or layer-adaptive schedules, which could plausibly close some of the gap to the ceiling suggested by the all-heads/half-heads/random-subset spread.
- **Decode-time overhead at scale.** The 6–9% decode-latency and 3–5% memory overhead at batch size 8 (Section 5) were measured on our 1.3B backbone with our specific kernel implementation; we have not profiled larger batch sizes, tensor-parallel serving configurations, or fused-kernel implementations of the gate that might reduce this further.

## 9. Conclusion

Retrieval Gating substantially improves synthetic long-context retrieval at 1.3B scale, with gains attributable to content-conditioning rather than an implicit position prior, and gains that at least partially transfer to real long-document QA. We now report the seed-level variance, joint hyperparameter sensitivity, real-document evaluation, decode-time systems cost, and cross-scale comparison that motivated the design, and we are explicit that the two most important open questions — whether the benefit holds at 7B+ scale, and how much of the synthetic-NIAH gain survives on real tasks — remain only partially answered by the evidence in this paper. We view closing those two gaps, rather than further synthetic-benchmark tuning, as the priority for follow-up work.
