# A Curriculum for Tool-Use Agents That Reduces Error Cascades

## Abstract

Large language model (LLM) agents that invoke external tools (search, code execution, file I/O, APIs) frequently fail not because any single action is wrong, but because an early, moderate error goes uncorrected and compounds through subsequent tool calls — an *error cascade*. We propose **Cascade-Aware Curriculum (CAC)**, a training curriculum that orders tool-use tasks by *recoverability* rather than by raw difficulty, and interleaves a fixed budget of injected, recoverable tool failures whose frequency anneals over training. CAC combines (1) staged task ordering along a recoverability axis, (2) synthetic fault injection with escalating cascade depth, and (3) a lightweight self-check step inserted after each tool call during early stages, faded out by the final stage. We evaluate on three tool-use benchmarks (ToolBench-Sub, a custom multi-step API-orchestration suite, and a code-execution debugging suite) against five baselines: vanilla SFT, difficulty-ordered curriculum, random-order fault injection (no curriculum), self-consistency-only, and an outcome-reward RL baseline. Across 3 training seeds, CAC reduces cascade-failure rate (task failures attributable to an uncorrected early error) by 41–58% relative to vanilla SFT and 19–33% relative to the next-best baseline, with only a 3.4% (±0.6%) increase in total training-pipeline compute and no regression in single-step tool-call accuracy. We report per-seed variance, quantify oracle disagreement on all three benchmarks (including an external-dataset check for ToolBench-Sub), test the sensitivity of the recoverability-based curriculum to the choice and model family of the reference policy used to estimate it, and detail the RL baseline's algorithm, reward shaping, and compute-matched budget. We report a limited secondary-scale check (1.3B and 13B in addition to the primary 7B) and are explicit that this does not constitute a full scaling study. We release the fault-injection taxonomy, curriculum scheduler, and oracle-construction protocol used to generate results.

## 1. Introduction

Tool-use agents chain multiple actions — querying an API, parsing a result, calling a calculator, writing and executing code — where each step's input depends on the (possibly incorrect) output of a previous step. Unlike single-turn generation errors, tool-use errors are **path-dependent**: a small mistake in step 2 (e.g., a slightly malformed query, an off-by-one in a parsed value) can silently propagate, causing steps 3–7 to fail in ways that look unrelated to the true root cause. We call this an *error cascade*, and we observe empirically that a large fraction of end-to-end task failures in multi-step tool agents trace back to a single early, individually low-severity error (Section 3.2).

Standard training pipelines for tool-use agents (SFT on successful trajectories, RLHF/RLAIF on outcome reward, or difficulty-ordered curricula based on task length or tool count) do not directly target this failure mode. Difficulty curricula order by *task complexity*, which correlates with but is not identical to *cascade risk*: a long task with well-isolated steps (each error is locally detectable and correctable) is easier to learn robustly than a short task with tightly coupled steps where one bad value silently corrupts everything downstream. Outcome-reward RL, in principle, could learn recovery behavior implicitly if failures caused by uncorrected cascades are penalized enough relative to their frequency — but reward is sparse and terminal, so credit assignment to a shallow early error buried under five correct-looking downstream steps is a difficult exploration problem in its own right. We include an outcome-reward baseline (§3.6) to test this directly rather than asserting it.

We propose training on a curriculum ordered explicitly by **recoverability** — how easy it is, given a step's output, to detect that something has gone wrong and to recover — and pairing this with **fault injection** that deliberately introduces recoverable errors at increasing depth into the tool-call chain as training progresses. Early stages teach the model to detect and repair shallow errors (error at step 1, caught before step 2); later stages introduce deeper, more entangled faults. A self-check prompt ("does this tool output match expectations for the next step?") is used as scaffolding in early stages and is annealed away so the final policy performs recovery without explicit prompting.

A natural concern with any curriculum built from a learned or estimated difficulty signal is circularity: if the signal used to construct the curriculum is derived from the same model family used to train and evaluate it, apparent gains may partly reflect alignment between the curriculum's construction and the evaluation metric, rather than a generalizable capability improvement. We take this concern seriously and address it directly in §3.5 by re-deriving the curriculum from an independent reference policy — including one from a different model family — and measuring how much the resulting staging and downstream cascade-failure rate change.

We also flag, up front, a limitation we do not fully resolve: our primary results use a single 7B base model. §3.8 reports a limited secondary check at 1.3B and 13B, but this is not a scaling study, and we discuss the external-validity implications in §6.

**Contributions:**
1. A recoverability-based task ordering metric and curriculum scheduler for tool-use training data, with an analysis of its sensitivity to the reference policy used to estimate it, including cross-model-family robustness.
2. A fault-injection taxonomy (5 fault classes × 3 depths) for synthetic cascade construction, with an annealing schedule.
3. Empirical evaluation across three tool-use domains, with multi-seed variance estimates, oracle-noise quantification (including an external-dataset spot check), ablations isolating the contribution of ordering vs. fault injection vs. self-check scaffolding, and a compute-matched comparison against an outcome-reward RL baseline.

## 2. Method

### 2.1 Defining cascade failure

For a trajectory of tool calls $a_1, \dots, a_T$ with intermediate observations $o_1, \dots, o_T$, we label step $t^*$ as the **root error** if it is the earliest step whose output deviates from a correctness oracle (available at training-data-construction time via known-good reference trajectories or verifiable tool semantics, e.g., a calculator or code interpreter). A trajectory is a **cascade failure** if it fails at the end (task not completed / wrong final answer) *and* $t^* < T$, i.e., the root error occurred strictly before the final step and was never corrected. This distinguishes cascade failures from *terminal errors* (the last step is simply wrong despite everything before it being correct), which curricula targeting cascades are not designed to fix and which we track separately as a control metric.

**Oracle construction and noise.** For ToolBench-Sub, the oracle is the dataset's original reference trajectory plus tool-level executable semantics (search relevance judgments and calculator/API return values, which are deterministic given the query). Because these reference trajectories are reused from an external dataset rather than authored in-house, we additionally ran the same two-author independent-labeling protocol used for CodeDebug-150 on a random 15% sample (180 tasks) of ToolBench-Sub: agreement on the root-error step index was 94% exact match (Wilson 95% CI: [89.9%, 96.5%]), with the remaining disagreements concentrated in tasks with ambiguous multi-hop search queries where two different steps could plausibly be labeled as "first divergence." For API-Orchestration-200, each task's environment is a mock API with fully deterministic state transitions we control, so $t^*$ is computed exactly by diffing the agent's intermediate state against the environment's ground-truth state after each call — there is no oracle labeling noise by construction, though see §3.4 for a check on environment-design bias. For CodeDebug-150, $t^*$ is the first run-edit-run iteration whose edit does not reduce failing-test count on a held-out test set not shown to the agent; two authors independently labeled $t^*$ on a random 15% sample (23 tasks): 91% exact match (Wilson 95% CI: [72.6%, 97.4%] — wide, reflecting the small sample), with disagreements concentrated in tasks where two edits both partially improved test pass rate. Given the width of this interval we treat CodeDebug-150's oracle-noise estimate as directional rather than precise, and note in §3.4 that we do not observe it materially affecting CFR, since disagreements shift $t^*$ by at most one step and both candidate steps are still strictly before $T$ in every disagreement case (the cascade-vs-terminal classification is unaffected; only fine-grained recovery-depth attribution shifts by ≤1).

### 2.2 Recoverability score

For each training trajectory, we define a recoverability score $R(t)$ for step $t$ as the empirical probability, estimated from a pool of rollout perturbations, that an agent recovers task success after a synthetic fault is injected at step $t$:

$$R(t) = \Pr[\text{task success} \mid \text{fault injected at } t, \text{no additional intervention}]$$

estimated via $k=8$ rollouts per (trajectory, injection point) using a fixed reference policy (by default, the SFT-warm-started base model before curriculum training; §3.5 varies this choice). Tasks are then bucketed into three curriculum stages by $\min_t R(t)$ over their trajectory: **Stage A** (high recoverability, $R > 0.6$: errors are locally detectable, e.g., a malformed search query that returns an empty result), **Stage B** (moderate, $0.25 \le R \le 0.6$: errors that produce plausible-looking but wrong intermediate results), **Stage C** (low, $R < 0.25$: errors that are only detectable much later, e.g., a unit mismatch that silently propagates through several arithmetic steps).

### 2.3 Fault injection taxonomy

We inject five fault classes, each parameterized by **depth** (how many downstream steps must consume the corrupted value before the error surfaces):
- **F1 Malformed call**: syntactically invalid tool arguments (shallow; usually errors immediately).
- **F2 Wrong-but-plausible retrieval**: tool returns a real but off-target result (e.g., wrong entity in a search).
- **F3 Silent unit/type drift**: numeric result correct in magnitude but wrong unit/scale.
- **F4 Stale state**: agent acts on a cached/outdated observation after an intervening state change.
- **F5 Partial success**: tool call partially succeeds (e.g., 8/10 records written) without raising an error.

Each class is injected at shallow (1-step), medium (2–3 step), or deep (4+ step) distance from the point where it becomes consequential. Injection rate anneals across stages: Stage A trains with 40% of trajectories fault-injected (F1/F2, shallow only), Stage B with 25% (F2/F3/F4, shallow+medium), Stage C with 12% (all classes, all depths), and a final **Stage D** fine-tune uses clean trajectories only (0% injection) to prevent the model from learning to expect faults at test time.

### 2.4 Self-check scaffolding with annealing

During Stages A–C, after each tool observation the training sequence includes a self-check span: *"Expected: \<schema/type/range\>. Observed: \<value\>. Consistent? \<yes/no + action\>."* This span is present in the training target with probability $p_{\text{check}}$ that decays linearly from 1.0 (Stage A) to 0.0 (Stage D), so the model is not dependent on an externally prompted check at inference time — it internalizes the check as part of its default reasoning trace, matching prior findings that scaffolded reasoning can be distilled into unscaffolded behavior when faded gradually rather than dropped abruptly.

### 2.5 Curriculum schedule

Training proceeds Stage A → B → C → D, each stage a fixed number of gradient steps (equal compute per stage), with a soft mixing window (10% of steps) at each transition that blends the outgoing and incoming stage's data to avoid distributional shock.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Setup

**Benchmarks:** (i) *ToolBench-Sub*, a 1,200-task subset of ToolBench filtered to trajectories requiring ≥3 chained tool calls; (ii) *API-Orchestration-200*, a custom suite of 200 tasks requiring coordinated calls across 3–6 mock APIs (booking, inventory, notification) with programmatically checkable end states; (iii) *CodeDebug-150*, 150 multi-file debugging tasks requiring run-edit-run loops in a sandboxed interpreter. All primary results use a 7B-parameter base model, LoRA fine-tuned (rank 32, alpha 64, applied to attention and MLP projection matrices), with held-out test splits (20% of each benchmark) disjoint from curriculum construction data — no task used to estimate $R(t)$ or to construct fault-injected training examples appears in any test split. We report all main results averaged over 3 independent training seeds (data shuffling order, LoRA init, and rollout sampling seed varied jointly per run), and report per-seed values alongside means. Baselines are compute-matched to CAC within ±5% total training FLOPs (see §3.7 for how compute is measured). Full hyperparameters, the fault-injection taxonomy implementation, the curriculum scheduler, and the oracle-construction scripts (including the two-author labeling interface used for CodeDebug-150 and ToolBench-Sub) are released at the project repository referenced in the Reproducibility Statement (§7).

**Baselines:**
- **Vanilla SFT**: standard supervised fine-tuning on clean reference trajectories, no curriculum ordering, no fault injection.
- **Difficulty-ordered curriculum**: same data, ordered by task length (number of tool calls) and tool-type diversity rather than recoverability.
- **Random-order fault injection**: same fault-injection taxonomy and overall injection rate as CAC, but faults and clean trajectories are shuffled randomly rather than staged by recoverability, and no annealing schedule is applied (fixed 22% injection rate throughout, matched to CAC's time-averaged rate).
- **Self-consistency-only**: vanilla SFT model, but at inference time samples 5 trajectories and selects via majority vote on final answer (no training-time change; included as an inference-time control since some cascade failures could in principle be masked post hoc rather than prevented).
- **Outcome-reward RL**: PPO fine-tuning from the vanilla SFT checkpoint with a terminal task-success reward only (no process reward); see §3.6 for full details.

**Metrics:** Cascade-failure rate (CFR, primary metric, §2.1), terminal-error rate (control metric), single-step tool-call accuracy (does each individual call's arguments/schema match a valid call, independent of downstream success), and end-to-end task success rate.

**Terminal-error rate (control)** is statistically indistinguishable across methods (28.4–30.1% pooled across benchmarks, no pairwise comparison significant at $p<0.05$), confirming CAC targets cascade failures specifically rather than acting as a general task-success booster. **Single-step tool-call accuracy** is also flat across methods (CAC 92.1% vs. vanilla SFT 91.8%, not significant), confirming the abstract's claim of no regression in single-step accuracy — CAC's gains come from recovery behavior, not from making individual calls more accurate.

| Configuration | Mean CFR (%) |
|---|---|

| + Fault injection only (random order, as in main table) | 25.8 |
| + Self-check scaffolding only (clean data, checks present, annealed) | 28.9 |

Each component contributes independently (all pairwise-combination rows beat either single component), and the full combination beats every ablation, indicating the components are complementary rather than redundant — recoverability ordering determines *when* the model sees which failure modes, fault injection determines *what* it practices recovering from, and self-check scaffolding determines *how* it is taught to notice a problem before it compounds.

### 3.4 Oracle and environment validity

We extend the oracle-noise discussion from §2.1 with two additional checks requested by reviewers.

**ToolBench-Sub external-reference check.** As noted in §2.1, our two-author spot check against the externally-sourced ToolBench-Sub reference trajectories found 94% agreement (180-task sample, 95% CI [89.9%, 96.5%]), comparable to or tighter than our in-house CodeDebug-150 estimate. This suggests the external dataset's reference trajectories are not a weaker oracle than our own hand-authored ones.

**API-Orch-200 environment-bias check.** Because the mock APIs were authored by the same team that designed the CAC fault taxonomy, we checked for circularity in the *environment* (as distinct from the *curriculum signal*, addressed in §3.5): we had a second, independent engineer (not involved in taxonomy design) construct 40 additional tasks against the same mock-API framework, blind to the F1–F5 taxonomy, using only the API documentation and general task templates ("book X subject to constraint Y", "reconcile inventory after Z"). We then classified the naturally-occurring failure modes in vanilla-SFT rollouts on these 40 tasks against the F1–F5 taxonomy post hoc. 89% of observed root-error instances fell into one of the five existing classes, with the remainder (11%) being combinations of two classes co-occurring at the same step rather than a genuinely new failure mode. We take this as evidence the taxonomy was not gerrymandered to the original 200 tasks, though we note this check cannot fully rule out that the *mock-API framework itself* (as opposed to individual tasks) is more fault-prone in CAC-legible ways than a real-world API integration would be — we flag this as a residual external-validity caveat in §6 rather than claiming it is resolved.

**CodeDebug-150 sample size.** We acknowledge the 23-task oracle-agreement spot check yields a wide confidence interval ([72.6%, 97.4%]) and should be read as directional. We do not have compute budget in this revision to expand the double-labeled sample to the full 150 tasks, and flag this as a concrete item for follow-up work (§6).

### 3.5 Reference-policy sensitivity (circularity check)

The recoverability score $R(t)$ (§2.2) is estimated via rollouts from a reference policy. Our default choice is the SFT-warm-started base model prior to curriculum training — from the same model family subsequently trained with CAC. To test whether this choice inflates apparent gains, we re-derived the full Stage A/B/C bucketing three additional ways and re-ran the complete CAC pipeline (fault injection, self-check annealing, and staging) from each resulting curriculum, holding everything else fixed:

1. **Same-family, different checkpoint**: reference policy = the vanilla-SFT *baseline's* final checkpoint (i.e., a model that has already seen all training data once, rather than the pre-curriculum warm start).
2. **Cross-family, matched scale**: reference policy = a different 7B-class open-weights model not otherwise used anywhere in this paper.
3. **Cross-family, larger scale**: reference policy = a ~34B-class open-weights model, used only for the $k=8$ rollout estimation of $R(t)$ (not for training).

Table 4 reports mean CFR (averaged across the three benchmarks) for CAC trained on curricula derived from each reference policy, plus the stage-bucket agreement rate (fraction of tasks assigned to the same Stage A/B/C bucket as under the default reference policy).

| Reference policy for $R(t)$ | Stage-bucket agreement vs. default | Resulting mean CFR (%) |
|---|---|---|
| Default (pre-curriculum SFT warm start) | 100% (by definition) | 17.2 |
| Same-family, post-SFT checkpoint | 86% | 17.9 |
| Cross-family, matched scale (~7B) | 71% | 19.4 |
| Cross-family, larger scale (~34B) | 68% | 18.6 |

CFR under the two cross-family reference policies (19.4%, 18.6%) is higher than under the default (17.2%) but still far below every baseline in Table 1 (next-best baseline mean CFR pooled across benchmarks: 25.8%, random-order fault injection). In other words, using a curriculum derived entirely from a different model's judgment of recoverability still recovers roughly 70–75% of CAC's advantage over the next-best baseline, even though the specific stage assignment agrees with the default only 68–71% of the time. This indicates the benefit is not purely an artifact of curriculum-construction and evaluation sharing a model family, though the ~2 point CFR gap between same-family and cross-family reference policies shows the effect is not fully family-independent either — we report this as a partial rather than complete resolution of the circularity concern, and note the gap is directionally consistent with cross-family reference policies producing noisier (lower-agreement) recoverability estimates rather than systematically miscalibrated ones (F1/F2 shallow-fault buckets agree at >90% across all reference policies; disagreement concentrates in the Stage B/C boundary, where recoverability estimates are inherently closer to the $k=8$ rollout sampling-noise floor).

### 3.6 Outcome-reward RL baseline details

The RL baseline in Table 1 is PPO, initialized from the vanilla-SFT checkpoint, with a terminal reward of +1 for task success and 0 otherwise (no partial credit, no process reward, no explicit cascade penalty — intentionally, since the point of this baseline is to test whether cascade-recovery behavior emerges from outcome reward alone, without hand-designed credit assignment). KL penalty coefficient 0.02 against the SFT checkpoint, batch size 256 rollouts/update, learning rate 1e-6, value-function warm-start of 200 steps before policy updates begin, 4 PPO epochs per batch. Training compute is matched to CAC's total pipeline compute (SFT-equivalent forward/backward passes plus rollout cost) to within 4%, using the same accounting described in §3.7 — this required roughly 3,400 PPO updates, at which point held-out task-success reward had plateaued for >300 updates.

Outcome-reward RL improves over vanilla SFT (mean CFR 34.2% → 27.2% pooled, a 20% relative reduction) but underperforms CAC by a wide margin (27.2% vs. 17.2%, i.e., CAC's improvement over vanilla SFT is roughly 2.4× larger). This is consistent with the credit-assignment argument in §1: RL's terminal, sparse reward does provide *some* pressure toward recovery behavior, but a shallow, individually low-severity root error buried under several correct-looking downstream steps is difficult for outcome-only reward to attribute correctly, especially at the compute budget available here. We do not claim RL could not eventually close this gap with substantially more compute, additional reward shaping, or process supervision — only that, at compute parity with CAC, it does not.

### 3.7 Compute overhead accounting

The 3.4% (±0.6%) figure in the abstract is total training-*pipeline* wall-clock-equivalent FLOPs, measured as: (SFT-phase forward+backward FLOPs across all four curriculum stages) + (fault-injection and self-check token overhead, since injected/annotated trajectories are on average 6–9% longer than clean ones) + (the one-time $k=8$ rollout cost for estimating $R(t)$ over the full training pool, amortized once per benchmark since $R(t)$ is computed a single time before Stage A begins and is not re-estimated during training). We report FLOPs rather than wall-clock or GPU-hours because our baselines were run on a mix of hardware over the course of the project and FLOPs is the hardware-independent quantity; we cross-checked that FLOPs and measured wall-clock time agreed to within 5% on a fixed-hardware subset (single 8×A100 node, all methods), so the two accountings are not in tension for our setup. Excluding the one-time $R(t)$ rollout cost, the incremental cost of curriculum/injection/self-check tokens alone is 2.1% (±0.4%); including it brings the total to 3.4% (±0.6%). We consider the rollout cost part of the method's real cost (it must be paid once, whether or not it is amortized across benchmarks) and report the inclusive figure as our headline number.

### 3.8 Secondary-scale check (limited)

All results above use a 7B base model, and we do not claim this establishes how CAC's benefit scales with model size. As a partial check — not a scaling study — we reran the full CAC vs. vanilla-SFT comparison (single seed, ToolBench-Sub only, due to compute constraints) at 1.3B and 13B parameters, keeping the curriculum schedule, fault taxonomy, and self-check design fixed.

| Scale | Vanilla SFT CFR | CAC CFR | Relative reduction |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.3B | 41.6% | 29.8% | 28.4% |
| 7B (primary, seed-1) | 35.8% | 16.0% | 55.3% |
| 13B | 31.2% | 15.9% | 49.0% |

The relative benefit is smaller at 1.3B than at 7B or 13B, consistent with a plausible hypothesis that some minimum capacity is needed for the self-check scaffolding and recovery behavior to be learnable at all — but this is a single seed at each of two additional scales on one benchmark, and we explicitly do not extrapolate beyond it. We flag scale as an open external-validity question in §6 rather than treating this table as resolving it.

## 4. Related Work

Curriculum learning for LLM fine-tuning has typically ordered by task difficulty proxies (length, perplexity under a reference model, or human-annotated complexity), which we compare against directly as the "difficulty-ordered curriculum" baseline. Fault/perturbation injection has been used in robustness training for classification and RL agents; our contribution is the tool-use-specific taxonomy (F1–F5) and pairing injection depth with a recoverability-derived curriculum rather than a fixed or random schedule. Self-correction and self-critique prompting in LLMs is typically studied as an inference-time technique; we instead treat it as *training-time scaffolding* that is deliberately annealed away, so the trained policy does not depend on it at inference.

## 5. Limitations

- **Single primary scale.** Our headline results are at 7B. §3.8's 1.3B/13B check is single-seed, single-benchmark, and shows benefit magnitude varies with scale — we cannot rule out that the effect attenuates, plateaus, or behaves non-monotonically at scales well beyond 13B.
- **Two of three benchmarks are custom.** API-Orchestration-200 and CodeDebug-150 are author-constructed. §3.4's environment-bias check (independent task author, taxonomy-blind) and the oracle-agreement studies partially mitigate but do not eliminate the risk that our benchmarks are more legible to the CAC taxonomy than an arbitrary real-world tool-use distribution would be.
- **CodeDebug-150 oracle-noise estimate is imprecise.** The 91% agreement figure rests on 23 tasks (95% CI spanning 72.6–97.4%); we have not yet expanded this to the full benchmark.
- **Circularity is only partially resolved.** §3.5 shows cross-family reference policies retain most but not all of CAC's advantage over the next-best baseline (a ~2-point CFR gap remains), so we cannot claim the recoverability signal is fully independent of the trained model family.
- **RL baseline is one configuration.** §3.6's PPO baseline uses a specific reward design and compute budget; a differently shaped or better-tuned RL baseline (e.g., with process reward or substantially more compute) might close more of the gap to CAC than reported here.

## 6. Conclusion

CAC — recoverability-ordered curriculum staging, annealed fault injection, and annealed self-check scaffolding — reduces cascade-failure rate substantially and consistently across three tool-use benchmarks and three training seeds, without regressing single-step accuracy or terminal-error rate, at a modest (3.4% ± 0.6%) compute overhead. The improvement over an outcome-reward RL baseline at matched compute supports the paper's motivating claim that sparse terminal reward alone is a weak signal for learning cascade recovery. The reference-policy sensitivity analysis shows the approach is not purely an artifact of curriculum construction and evaluation sharing a model family, though it is not fully family-independent, and we report this as a partial rather than complete answer to the circularity concern. The largest open questions are external validity at scales beyond 13B and on tool-use distributions not authored by the same team that designed the fault taxonomy; we view both as the natural next steps rather than as resolved by this paper.
