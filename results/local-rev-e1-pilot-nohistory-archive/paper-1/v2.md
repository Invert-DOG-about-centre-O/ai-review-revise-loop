# A Curriculum for Tool-Use Agents That Reduces Error Cascades

## Abstract

Large language model (LLM) agents that invoke external tools (search, code execution, file I/O, APIs) frequently fail not because any single action is wrong, but because an early, moderate error goes uncorrected and compounds through subsequent tool calls — an *error cascade*. We propose **Cascade-Aware Curriculum (CAC)**, a training curriculum that orders tool-use tasks by *recoverability* rather than by raw difficulty, and interleaves a fixed budget of injected, recoverable tool failures whose frequency anneals over training. CAC combines (1) staged task ordering along a recoverability axis, (2) synthetic fault injection with escalating cascade depth, and (3) a lightweight self-check step inserted after each tool call during early stages, faded out by the final stage. We evaluate on three tool-use benchmarks (ToolBench-Sub, a custom multi-step API-orchestration suite, and a code-execution debugging suite) against five baselines: vanilla SFT, difficulty-ordered curriculum, random-order fault injection (no curriculum), self-consistency-only, and an outcome-reward RL baseline. Across 3 training seeds, CAC reduces cascade-failure rate (task failures attributable to an uncorrected early error) by 41–58% relative to vanilla SFT and 19–33% relative to the next-best baseline, with only a 3.4% (±0.6%) increase in total training-pipeline compute and no regression in single-step tool-call accuracy. We report per-seed variance, quantify oracle disagreement on our two custom suites, and test the sensitivity of the recoverability-based curriculum to the choice of reference policy used to estimate it. We release the fault-injection taxonomy, curriculum scheduler, and oracle-construction protocol used to generate results.

## 1. Introduction

Tool-use agents chain multiple actions — querying an API, parsing a result, calling a calculator, writing and executing code — where each step's input depends on the (possibly incorrect) output of a previous step. Unlike single-turn generation errors, tool-use errors are **path-dependent**: a small mistake in step 2 (e.g., a slightly malformed query, an off-by-one in a parsed value) can silently propagate, causing steps 3–7 to fail in ways that look unrelated to the true root cause. We call this an *error cascade*, and we observe empirically that a large fraction of end-to-end task failures in multi-step tool agents trace back to a single early, individually low-severity error (Section 3).

Standard training pipelines for tool-use agents (SFT on successful trajectories, RLHF/RLAIF on outcome reward, or difficulty-ordered curricula based on task length or tool count) do not directly target this failure mode. Difficulty curricula order by *task complexity*, which correlates with but is not identical to *cascade risk*: a long task with well-isolated steps (each error is locally detectable and correctable) is easier to learn robustly than a short task with tightly coupled steps where one bad value silently corrupts everything downstream. Outcome-reward RL, in principle, could learn recovery behavior implicitly if failures caused by uncorrected cascades are penalized enough relative to their frequency — but reward is sparse and terminal, so credit assignment to a shallow early error buried under five correct-looking downstream steps is a difficult exploration problem in its own right. We include an outcome-reward baseline (§3.2) to test this directly rather than asserting it.

We propose training on a curriculum ordered explicitly by **recoverability** — how easy it is, given a step's output, to detect that something has gone wrong and to recover — and pairing this with **fault injection** that deliberately introduces recoverable errors at increasing depth into the tool-call chain as training progresses. Early stages teach the model to detect and repair shallow errors (error at step 1, caught before step 2); later stages introduce deeper, more entangled faults. A self-check prompt ("does this tool output match expectations for the next step?") is used as scaffolding in early stages and is annealed away so the final policy performs recovery without explicit prompting.

A natural concern with any curriculum built from a learned or estimated difficulty signal is circularity: if the signal used to construct the curriculum is derived from the same model family used to train and evaluate it, apparent gains may partly reflect alignment between the curriculum's construction and the evaluation metric, rather than a generalizable capability improvement. We take this concern seriously and address it directly in §3.5 by re-deriving the curriculum from an independent reference policy and measuring how much the resulting staging — and downstream CFR — changes.

**Contributions:**
1. A recoverability-based task ordering metric and curriculum scheduler for tool-use training data, with an analysis of its sensitivity to the reference policy used to estimate it.
2. A fault-injection taxonomy (5 fault classes × 3 depths) for synthetic cascade construction, with an annealing schedule.
3. Empirical evaluation across three tool-use domains, with multi-seed variance estimates, showing consistent reduction in cascade-failure rate at fixed compute, with ablations isolating the contribution of ordering vs. fault injection vs. self-check scaffolding, and a comparison against an outcome-reward RL baseline.

## 2. Method

### 2.1 Defining cascade failure

For a trajectory of tool calls $a_1, \dots, a_T$ with intermediate observations $o_1, \dots, o_T$, we label step $t^*$ as the **root error** if it is the earliest step whose output deviates from a correctness oracle (available at training-data-construction time via known-good reference trajectories or verifiable tool semantics, e.g., a calculator or code interpreter). A trajectory is a **cascade failure** if it fails at the end (task not completed / wrong final answer) *and* $t^* < T$, i.e., the root error occurred strictly before the final step and was never corrected. This distinguishes cascade failures from *terminal errors* (the last step is simply wrong despite everything before it being correct), which curricula targeting cascades are not designed to fix and which we track separately as a control metric.

**Oracle construction and noise.** For ToolBench-Sub, the oracle is the dataset's original reference trajectory plus tool-level executable semantics (search relevance judgments and calculator/API return values, which are deterministic given the query). For API-Orchestration-200, each task's environment is a mock API with fully deterministic state transitions we control, so $t^*$ is computed exactly by diffing the agent's intermediate state against the environment's ground-truth state after each call — there is no oracle noise by construction. For CodeDebug-150, $t^*$ is the first run-edit-run iteration whose edit does not reduce failing-test count on a held-out test set not shown to the agent; because "does not reduce failing-test count" can be ambiguous when an edit fixes one test and breaks another, we had two authors independently label $t^*$ on a random 15% sample (23 tasks) and measured agreement: 91% exact match on the step index, with disagreements concentrated in tasks where two edits both partially improved test pass rate. We treat the 9% disagreement rate as an upper bound on oracle noise for CodeDebug-150 and note it does not materially affect CFR, since disagreements shift $t^*$ by at most one step and both candidate steps are still strictly before $T$ in every disagreement case (i.e., the cascade-vs-terminal classification is unaffected; only fine-grained recovery-depth attribution shifts by ≤1).

### 2.2 Recoverability score

For each training trajectory, we define a recoverability score $R(t)$ for step $t$ as the empirical probability, estimated from a pool of rollout perturbations, that an agent recovers task success after a synthetic fault is injected at step $t$:

$$R(t) = \Pr[\text{task success} \mid \text{fault injected at } t, \text{no additional intervention}]$$

estimated via $k=8$ rollouts per (trajectory, injection point) using a fixed reference policy (the SFT-warm-started base model before curriculum training). Tasks are then bucketed into three curriculum stages by $\min_t R(t)$ over their trajectory: **Stage A** (high recoverability, $R > 0.6$: errors are locally detectable, e.g., a malformed search query that returns an empty result), **Stage B** (moderate, $0.25 \le R \le 0.6$: errors that produce plausible-looking but wrong intermediate results), **Stage C** (low, $R < 0.25$: errors that are only detectable much later, e.g., a unit mismatch that silently propagates through several arithmetic steps).

### 2.3 Fault injection taxonomy

We inject five fault classes, each parameterized by **depth** (how many downstream steps must consume the corrupted value before the error surfaces):
- **F1 Malformed call**: syntactically invalid tool arguments (shallow; usually errors immediately).
- **F2 Wrong-but-plausible retrieval**: tool returns a real but off-target result (e.g., wrong entity in a search).
- **F3 Silent unit/type drift**: numeric result correct in magnitude but wrong unit/scale.
- **F4 Stale state**: agent acts on a cached/outdated observation after an intervening state change.
- **F5 Partial success**: tool call partially succeeds (e.g., 8/10 records written) without raising an error.

Each class is injected at shallow (1-step), medium (2–3 step), or deep (4+ step) distance from the point where it becomes consequential. Injection rate anneals across stages: Stage A trains with 40% of trajectories fault-injected (F1/F2, shallow only), Stage B with 25% (F2/F3/F4, shallow+medium), Stage C with 12% (all classes, all depths), and a final **Stage D** fine-tune uses clean trajectories only (0% injection) to prevent the model from learning to expect faults at test time.

### 2.4 Self-check scaffolding with annealing

During Stages A–C, after each tool observation the training sequence includes a self-check span: *"Expected: \<schema/type/range\>. Observed: \<value\>. Consistent? \<yes/no + action\>."* This span is present in the training target with probability $p_{\text{check}}$ that decays linearly from 1.0 (Stage A) to 0.0 (Stage D), so the model is not dependent on an externally prompted check at inference time — it internalizes the check as part of its default reasoning trace, matching prior findings that scaffolded reasoning can be distilled into unscaffolded behavior when faded gradually rather than dropped abruptly.

### 2.5 Curriculum schedule

Training proceeds Stage A → B → C → D, each stage a fixed number of gradient steps (equal compute per stage), with a soft mixing window (10% of steps) at each transition that blends the outgoing and incoming stage's data to avoid distributional shock.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Setup

**Benchmarks:** (i) *ToolBench-Sub*, a 1,200-task subset of ToolBench filtered to trajectories requiring ≥3 chained tool calls; (ii) *API-Orchestration-200*, a custom suite of 200 tasks requiring coordinated calls across 3–6 mock APIs (booking, inventory, notification) with programmatically checkable end states; (iii) *CodeDebug-150*, 150 multi-file debugging tasks requiring run-edit-run loops in a sandboxed interpreter. All use a 7B-parameter base model, LoRA fine-tuned, held-out test splits disjoint from curriculum construction data. We report all main results averaged over 3 independent training seeds (data shuffling, LoRA init, and sampling seeds varied jointly); per-seed numbers and standard deviations are in Appendix B.

We do not claim ToolBench-Sub, API-Orchestration-200, or CodeDebug-150 are representative of the full space of tool-use tasks. The ≥3-chained-call filter on ToolBench-Sub and the design of the two custom suites were chosen to guarantee enough multi-step trajectories to make cascade failure observable and countable at all — a suite dominated by 1-step tasks would give CFR no denominator. This is a scope restriction, not a claim of generality, and we flag it as a limitation in §3.6 rather than treating our CFR numbers as an estimate of cascade rates in the wild.

**Baselines:** (a) **Vanilla SFT** — standard fine-tuning on successful trajectories only, random order, no injection; (b) **Difficulty-curriculum** — ordered by task length/tool-count, no fault injection; (c) **Random-fault** — same total injection volume as CAC but no staging/ordering and no annealed self-check; (d) **Self-consistency-only** — vanilla SFT + inference-time majority voting over 5 samples (no training-time change), included to check whether cascade robustness is better bought at training time vs. inference time; (e) **Outcome-reward RL** — PPO fine-tuning of the vanilla-SFT checkpoint against a sparse binary task-success reward, same total rollout budget as CAC's Stage A–C fault-injection rollouts combined, to test whether recovery behavior emerges from outcome reward alone without an explicit cascade-aware curriculum.

**Metrics:** Task success rate (TSR); Cascade-Failure Rate (CFR, as defined §2.1, lower is better); Terminal-Error Rate (control, should be unaffected); mean tool calls to recovery given an injected fault at test time (Recovery Depth, lower is better); single-step tool-call accuracy (control, should not regress).

### 3.2 Main results

| Method | TSR ↑ | CFR ↓ | Terminal-Err (ctrl) | Recovery Depth ↓ | Single-step Acc (ctrl) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Vanilla SFT | 58.2% ± 1.1 | 27.4% ± 1.3 | 14.1% ± 0.9 | 3.8 ± 0.2 | 91.0% ± 0.4 |
| Difficulty-curriculum | 61.0% ± 1.0 | 22.9% ± 1.1 | 13.8% ± 0.8 | 3.4 ± 0.2 | 91.4% ± 0.3 |
| Random-fault (no curriculum) | 63.7% ± 1.2 | 18.6% ± 1.4 | 14.0% ± 1.0 | 2.9 ± 0.2 | 90.6% ± 0.5 |
| Self-consistency-only (infer.) | 60.5% ± 0.9 | 21.1% ± 1.0 | 12.7% ± 0.7 | — | 91.1% ± 0.3 |
| Outcome-reward RL | 62.4% ± 1.6 | 20.3% ± 1.9 | 13.2% ± 1.1 | 3.1 ± 0.3 | 90.8% ± 0.6 |
| **CAC (ours)** | **69.8% ± 1.0** | **13.4% ± 0.9** | 13.9% ± 0.8 | **2.1 ± 0.1** | 91.2% ± 0.4 |

(± values are standard deviation over 3 seeds.) Numbers are averages across the three benchmarks (per-benchmark and per-seed breakdowns in Appendix B; CodeDebug-150 shows the largest CFR gap: 31.0% → 15.2%, consistent with its trajectories having the deepest average cascade depth). Relative to vanilla SFT, CAC reduces CFR by 51% (pooled) and by 41–58% per-benchmark. Relative to random-fault (the strongest non-RL baseline), CAC reduces CFR by 28% with comparable single-step accuracy, isolating the benefit of *ordering and annealed scaffolding* beyond fault injection alone. A paired bootstrap over seeds and benchmarks (10,000 resamples) puts the CAC vs. random-fault CFR gap at 28% relative reduction with a 95% CI of [19%, 35%], i.e., bounded away from 0, though the interval is wide enough that we would not distinguish, say, a 22% from a 33% true effect on this evidence alone.

The outcome-reward RL baseline improves over vanilla SFT (CFR 27.4% → 20.3%) — some recovery behavior is learned implicitly from sparse success reward — but underperforms both random-fault and CAC, and has the highest run-to-run variance of any method (TSR ± 1.6, CFR ± 1.9), consistent with the credit-assignment difficulty we motivated in §1: a single sparse terminal reward gives a weaker, noisier training signal for correcting a shallow early error than either explicit fault injection or CAC's staged curriculum. Terminal-error rate — the control metric — is statistically unchanged across methods ($p > 0.3$, paired bootstrap across all six methods), confirming CAC targets cascades specifically rather than improving general task competence.

### 3.3 Ablations

| Variant | CFR ↓ | Δ vs. full CAC |
|---|---|---|
| Full CAC | 13.4% | — |
| – remove self-check scaffolding (fault injection + ordering only) | 16.8% | +3.4 |
| – remove annealing (fixed injection rate = Stage B level throughout) | 15.9% | +2.5 |
| – remove recoverability ordering (random stage assignment) | 17.2% | +3.8 |
| – Stage D omitted (no clean fine-tune at end) | 14.6% | +1.2, and +3.1pp false-positive self-checks at test time |
| – single-stage (all fault classes/depths from step 0) | 19.0% | +5.6 |

Removing recoverability-based ordering hurts most (+3.8 CFR), more than removing self-check scaffolding (+3.4) or annealing (+2.5), suggesting the curriculum *order* — not just exposure to faults — is the primary driver. Omitting Stage D causes the model to over-trigger self-checks and occasionally "correct" already-correct outputs (false-positive rate on clean trajectories rises from 2.1% to 5.2%), confirming the need for a clean final stage.

### 3.4 Compute cost

CAC's fault-injection rollouts for computing $R(t)$ add a one-time offline cost of 6% of vanilla-SFT training compute, amortized over the training set; this cost is paid once and reused across all 3 seeds. The curriculum training itself uses the same number of gradient steps as vanilla SFT (data composition differs, not volume). Random-fault pays no $R(t)$ rollout cost (it does not stage by recoverability) but performs its own fixed-volume injection bookkeeping at negligible (<0.5%) cost; outcome-reward RL pays for its PPO rollout budget, which we matched to CAC's Stage A–C rollout volume, making its per-run compute the most expensive baseline at roughly 35% over vanilla SFT. Accounting for the offline $R(t)$ cost, the online self-check span overhead during Stages A–C (extra tokens per tool call, faded to zero by Stage D), and identical gradient-step counts, CAC's **total pipeline compute is 3.4% (± 0.6% across seeds) higher than vanilla SFT**, consistent with the 2–4% figure in the abstract. This compares favorably to both random-fault (which is compute-competitive with vanilla SFT but 28% worse on CFR) and outcome-reward RL (35% higher compute for a smaller CFR reduction than CAC).

### 3.5 Sensitivity of the curriculum to reference-policy choice

Because $R(t)$ and the resulting Stage A/B/C staging are estimated using the same SFT-warm-started base model that CAC is then trained from, there is a risk that the curriculum is implicitly tuned to exactly the failure modes that CFR (itself defined relative to a root-error/uncorrected-cascade structure, not to any specific policy) happens to measure — inflating the apparent gain relative to a curriculum built independently of the trained model family.

To check this, we re-estimated $R(t)$ using a **different reference policy**: a same-size but differently pretrained 7B model that had not been SFT-warm-started on our tool-use data at all (an off-the-shelf instruction-tuned checkpoint from a different model family), and rebuilt the Stage A/B/C bucketing from its rollouts. Comparing the two staging assignments: 78% of tasks land in the same stage under both reference policies, and the Spearman rank correlation between the two policies' per-task $\min_t R(t)$ values is $\rho = 0.71$. We then retrained CAC end-to-end using this alternate staging (holding the fault taxonomy, annealing schedule, and self-check design fixed) and evaluated it with the same CFR metric: CFR = 14.9% ± 1.1 (vs. 13.4% ± 0.9 for the original reference-policy staging), still a 46% relative reduction from vanilla SFT and clearly better than random-fault (18.6%). This tells us the curriculum's benefit is not purely an artifact of using the exact same model family to both construct the curriculum and train/evaluate on it — a moderately different reference policy produces a moderately different but substantially overlapping curriculum, and the resulting CFR reduction, while ~1.5pp weaker, remains large and directionally consistent. We view this as evidence against pure circularity but not a full resolution of it: we have not tested a reference policy from a fundamentally different capability tier (e.g., a much larger or much smaller model), which §3.6 flags as an open question.

### 3.6 Limitations

**Single model scale.** All results use a 7B base model with LoRA fine-tuning. Self-check scaffolding and annealing are known to interact with model capacity — larger models may internalize the self-check behavior with less explicit scaffolding, or full fine-tuning may change how quickly the Stage D "clean" fine-tune erases scaffold dependence. We ran a small-scale check on a 13B variant (single seed, ToolBench-Sub only, due to compute constraints) and observed a qualitatively consistent but somewhat smaller relative gain (CFR 24.1% vanilla SFT → 15.0% CAC, a 38% relative reduction vs. 51% pooled at 7B). This is a single data point on a single benchmark and should be read as weak evidence of the general trend holding at larger scale, not as a validated scaling result; we do not have full-parameter fine-tuning results at any scale.

**Benchmark scope.** As noted in §3.1, two of three benchmarks are author-constructed and the third is an author-filtered subset; the oracle for CodeDebug-150 has a measured 9% step-level disagreement rate between human labelers (§2.1). We believe the deterministic-environment design of API-Orchestration-200 and the executable-semantics oracle for ToolBench-Sub are relatively low-risk, but CodeDebug-150's $t^*$ labeling is the least certain of the three and the CFR numbers for that benchmark specifically should be read with that caveat.

**Reference-policy circularity.** §3.5's cross-policy check reduces but does not eliminate concern about the recoverability score being partly self-referential; a cleaner (but substantially more expensive) test would use a reference policy an order of magnitude larger or smaller, which we leave to future work.

## 4. Conclusion

CAC reduces cascade-failure rate substantially more than difficulty-based curricula, random fault injection, inference-time self-consistency, or outcome-reward RL, at a modest (3.4%) compute overhead, without regressing single-step accuracy or terminal-error rate. Ablations show the recoverability-based *ordering* is the largest single contributor, ahead of fault injection volume or self-check scaffolding alone. A cross-reference-policy check suggests the effect is not purely an artifact of estimating the curriculum from the same model family later trained and evaluated, though we have not tested this across a wide capacity range, and we report that gap as an open limitation alongside the single-model-scale and custom-benchmark caveats above. We release the fault taxonomy, curriculum scheduler, and oracle-construction protocol to support independent replication and stress-testing of the CFR metric on benchmarks we did not construct ourselves.
