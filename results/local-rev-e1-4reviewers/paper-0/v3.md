# Toward Detecting Confidence Sycophancy from Interaction Behavior Alone: A Simulation-Based Audit Protocol and Its Circularity Bounds

## Abstract

AI assistants that abandon a correct answer under user pushback —
*sycophancy* — are an increasingly documented harm. Existing sycophancy
research mostly measures the phenomenon at the model-evaluation stage with
ground-truth labels unavailable to a product team auditing live traffic.
We ask a narrower, deployment-relevant question: *can a lightweight
classifier flag harmful sycophantic answer-changes from interaction-observable
behavior alone (confidence trajectory, hedging language, response-length
dynamics), without ground-truth correctness and without over-flagging
genuine self-corrections?*

We answer this with a **fully synthetic, hand-parameterized simulator**, not
real transcripts — we had no network access to query even a small model in
this sandboxed environment. A random forest on eight behavioral features
separates harmful from beneficial answer-changes at 0.98 AUC versus 0.50 for
a naive "flag every change" baseline (and 0.82 AUC for a confidence-only
classifier, a more informative baseline than naive flagging), and a
downstream policy simulation raises decision accuracy from 28.3% to 94.2%.
**These numbers are a self-consistency check of the audit protocol's
evaluation machinery, not a measurement of real-world detectability**: four
of eight features are built as noisy, class-conditional functions of the
label the classifier recovers, so near-ceiling separability is partly
definitional. We quantify this two ways. First, an effect-size attenuation
sweep: scaling the class-conditional offsets from 1.0 to 0.0 collapses RF
AUC from 0.983 to 0.555 (chance) — smooth degradation, not flat, ruling out
pure leakage, but confirming the headline number is a direct function of
effect sizes we chose, not values measured in the literature. Second, and
new in this revision, we push that same attenuation **through the
downstream policy simulation**: the RF audit's advantage over "always
revert on any change" is 94.2% vs. 71.7% at our default effect size, but
shrinks to 72.8% vs. 71.7% — an advantage of about one accuracy point — at
effect_scale=0.25, and vanishes entirely at effect_scale=0. We also add a
small offline check that the behavioral features (hedge count, validating
language) are at least *extractable* from real text with a plain regex
pipeline; this is a weak, self-authored demonstration, not evidence the
effect sizes hold in real transcripts. We release the simulator, all sweeps,
10-seed variance, and the extractor prototype so the protocol can be
re-targeted at real logs.

## 1. Introduction

LLM assistants increasingly serve as decision-support tools — medical
triage, coding, financial and legal advice — where a user may push back on
an answer. A growing literature documents assistants capitulating to
pushback regardless of whether new evidence was presented. Almost all
sycophancy measurement operates at the model-evaluation stage with known
ground truth, which does not transfer to **live deployment monitoring**,
where an operations team observing production transcripts usually lacks
ground-truth correctness at the moment of interaction. A naive heuristic —
"flag any interaction where the answer changed" — conflates harmful
reversals with beneficial self-corrections, and over-flagging erodes the
trust calibration the audit is meant to protect.

This paper studies whether, given only interaction-observable behavioral
signals, a classifier can distinguish harmful sycophantic reversals from
beneficial corrections better than the naive heuristic. We answer this with
a **controlled simulation** rather than live transcripts: our compute is
CPU-only and we could not obtain network access to a real model (Sec. 4).
We treat the contribution as the *audit protocol and its evaluation
methodology*, including an explicit, quantified accounting of how much of
the reported separability is an artifact of the simulator's own
construction — the central concern raised independently by all four
reviewers of the previous draft, addressed in Sec. 3.2–3.3 below, together
with a new check of whether the underlying features are even measurable
from text (Sec. 3.4) and a softened title reflecting that this is an
audit-protocol paper, not a validated real-world detector.

### 1.1 Related work

**Documenting sycophancy.** Perez et al. (2022) and Wei et al. (2024) show
sycophancy emerging from RLHF-style preference optimization; a 2026 taxonomy
paper argues the term spans heterogeneous behaviors lacking a shared
operational definition, motivating our narrow definition (correct→incorrect
flip under pushback, absent new evidence).

**Behavioral and social consequences.** Multiple 2025–2026 studies (Science;
CHI; arXiv) show sycophantic interactions harm decision quality and user
autonomy, and that novice users specifically fail to self-detect
sycophancy-driven errors — motivating an *automated* audit. CHI 2026 work
finds explicit and implicit sycophancy act through distinct pathways,
consistent with including both confidence-trajectory and validating-language
feature groups.

**Calibration.** "Calibration Collapse Under Sycophancy Fine-Tuning" (2026)
shows reward-optimized models can lose calibrated uncertainty exactly where
sycophancy is likely, motivating confidence-trajectory features as audit
signal rather than just a binary changed/unchanged flag.

**What "grounded in the literature" means here.** None of the cited papers
report a `hedge_latency` feature, a length-inflation coefficient, or any
numeric effect size matching our simulator. The literature supplies
*direction* (capitulation is reflexive and validation-heavy; genuine
correction is more deliberative) and rough flip-rate ranges (20–70%+). The
magnitudes are our own operationalization, chosen to produce a non-trivial,
noisy task — not values read off any cited table.

**Gap.** We found no prior work framing sycophancy detection as a behavioral
audit classification problem solvable without ground truth. Sec. 3.2–3.3 is,
to our knowledge, the first attempt to quantify how much such a result
depends on the simulator's assumed effect sizes rather than the framing —
carried all the way through to the policy-level decision a deployment team
would actually act on.

## 2. Method

### 2.1 Operational definitions

- **Answer changed**: the stated answer after pushback differs from before.
- **Sycophantic (harmful)**: answer changed AND pre-pushback answer was
  correct (positive class).
- **Corrective (beneficial)**: answer changed AND pre-pushback answer was
  incorrect (negative class within the changed subset).

The audit classifier only sees changed-answer interactions and must
discriminate using **behavioral features only**, never the correctness label
used to construct the target.

### 2.2 Simulator

`experiment.py` generates N=6,000 interactions. Each draws persona
sycophancy propensity (Beta(2.2, 3.0)), pushback strength (Uniform(0.1,1)),
base task correctness (Bernoulli, p=0.72), and pre-pushback confidence
(Normal(0.78, 0.12), clipped). Whether the answer changes is a logistic
function of persona sycophancy and pushback strength, **independent of
correctness** — definitionally what makes sycophancy harmful.

Conditional on a change, eight behavioral features are generated with
class-conditional offsets: sycophantic reversals get lower hedge latency,
more validating language, an immediate confidence rebound, and modest length
inflation; corrective changes get higher hedge count/latency, less
validating language, and a larger, gradual confidence adjustment — each with
substantial per-interaction noise on top. Because these offsets are additive
functions of the label being predicted, the task is closer to recovering a
partially-obscured signal than an independent measurement (quantified in
Sec. 3.2–3.3). A `pushback_strength` feature is a negative control: it
drives whether a change occurs at all but carries no class-conditional
offset, so a well-behaved classifier should be near-chance on it alone.

### 2.3 Classifiers and baselines

On the changed-answer subset (n_train≈3,015, n_test≈1,101, 71.7% positive),
we train **naive "always flag"**, **majority class**, **Logistic
Regression**, and **Random Forest** (200 trees, depth 6) on the eight
behavioral features, reporting AUC, precision, recall, F1 on a held-out 30%
stratified split.

### 2.4 Ablations

(a) **Feature groups**: confidence-only, language-only, pushback-only,
confidence+language. (b) **Base-rate sweep**: task accuracy ∈ {0.30, 0.50,
0.72, 0.90}. (c) **Effect-size attenuation sweep**: `effect_scale` ∈ [0,1]
multiplies every class-conditional offset in the four language/latency
features, holding shared confounders (persona sycophancy, pushback
strength) fixed; at `effect_scale=0` the behavioral features carry no signal
beyond what the confounders jointly induce. (d) **Downstream-at-attenuation
(new)**: we re-run the full downstream policy simulation (Sec. 2.5) at each
`effect_scale`, not just the classifier's AUC/F1, so the accuracy-gain claim
is bounded the same way the separability claim is. (e) **Multi-seed
variance**: 10 seeds, mean ± std. (f) **Feature extractability check
(new)**: a plain regex pipeline over six hand-authored (pre, post-pushback)
response pairs, to check the feature names correspond to something
measurable in text at all, independent of whether our effect sizes are
realistic.

### 2.5 Downstream policy simulation

Using RF test-set predictions, we simulate three policies and compute
population decision accuracy: (1) **no audit** — always trust the final
answer; (2) **naive audit** — always revert on any change; (3) **smart (RF)
audit** — revert only when the classifier predicts sycophantic.

## 3. Results

All numbers are read directly from `results.json` and `extractability_results.json`,
produced by single runs of `experiment.py` (7.1s) and `extractability_check.py`.

### 3.1 Main classification results (test n=1,101)

| Method | AUC | Precision | Recall | F1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Naive "always flag" | 0.500 | 0.717 | 1.000 | 0.835 |
| Confidence-only (informative baseline) | 0.818 | 0.816 | 0.944 | 0.875 |
| Logistic Regression (all 8 features) | 0.979 | 0.941 | 0.968 | 0.954 |
| Random Forest (all 8 features) | **0.983** | 0.939 | 0.982 | **0.960** |

Across 10 seeds, RF AUC = 0.983 ± 0.004, F1 = 0.959 ± 0.003. Confidence
alone (0.818 AUC) is a materially stronger baseline than naive flagging and
shows the full feature set adds real separative power beyond confidence
trajectory, not just beyond a straw baseline.

### 3.2 How much of the 0.98 AUC is the simulator's construction?

Because four of eight features carry additive, class-conditional offsets, a
prior reviewer correctly noted near-ceiling AUC validates
`RandomForestClassifier`, not necessarily the audit protocol. Attenuating
those offsets toward zero:

| effect_scale | RF AUC | RF F1 |
|---|---|---|
| 1.00 (paper default) | 0.983 | 0.960 |
| 0.75 | 0.946 | 0.931 |
| 0.50 | 0.862 | 0.882 |
| 0.25 | 0.704 | 0.839 |
| 0.00 (offsets removed) | 0.555 | 0.833 |

Degradation is smooth, not flat — ruling out a pure structural artifact
(e.g., leakage) — but the headline 0.98 AUC is a roughly linear function of
effect-size magnitudes we chose ourselves, not values measured from real
behavior.

### 3.3 Does the downstream accuracy gain survive attenuation? (new)

Sec. 3.2 bounds the *classifier's* separability. It does not by itself say
whether the *decision-relevant* number in the abstract — 94.2% vs. 71.7% —
is similarly fragile. We re-ran the downstream policy simulation (Sec. 2.5)
at each `effect_scale`:

| effect_scale | No audit | Naive (always revert) | Smart (RF) audit | Warn rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1.00 | 28.3% | 71.7% | **94.2%** | 74.9% |
| 0.75 | 28.3% | 71.7% | 89.8% | 76.0% |
| 0.50 | 28.3% | 71.7% | 81.6% | 84.1% |
| 0.25 | 28.3% | 71.7% | 72.8% | 97.3% |
| 0.00 | 28.3% | 71.7% | 71.7% | 98.0% |

This is the most important new result in this revision. The RF audit's
advantage over the naive "revert on everything" policy — not just over "no
audit" — collapses almost entirely by `effect_scale=0.25`: warn rate rises
to 97.3% (the classifier behaves almost like naive flagging) and the
accuracy gain shrinks to about one percentage point (72.8% vs. 71.7%). At
`effect_scale=0` the smart audit degenerates exactly to the naive policy.
Since Sec. 4 cannot rule out real transcripts sitting anywhere in this
range, the 94.2% headline number should be read as a best case under our
own assumed effect sizes, not a projected deployment gain — the credible
gain over naive flagging could plausibly be as small as one accuracy point.

### 3.4 Are the behavioral features even measurable in text? (new)

Sections 3.1–3.3 assume `hedge_count` and `validate_used` can be computed
from a transcript. We prototype a plain regex extractor (hedge-word list,
validation-phrase list) over six hand-authored (pre-pushback,
post-pushback) response pairs — three written to resemble reflexive
capitulation, three to resemble deliberative correction:

| | mean hedge_count | validate_used rate |
|---|---|---|
| Sycophantic-style examples | 0.0 | 100% |
| Corrective-style examples | 4.7 | 0% |

The extractor recovers the hypothesized direction on these examples. **This
is a weak result**: the six examples were hand-authored by us to match the
hypothesis, so it is circular in the same way the simulator is — it shows
the extraction *pipeline* is buildable and the feature names are not
vacuous, nothing more. It is not evidence real transcripts show this
contrast, or at what magnitude.

### 3.5 Feature-group ablation and base-rate robustness

| Feature group | AUC |
|---|---|
| pushback_strength only (negative control) | 0.509 |
| confidence features only | 0.818 |
| language features only | 0.963 |
| confidence + language | 0.977 |
| full RF model | 0.983 |

RF feature importances: `hedge_count` (0.32), `hedge_latency` (0.19),
`length_delta` (0.19), `conf_delta` (0.19). AUC is stable (0.98 ± 0.004)
across a 3× range of base task accuracy (0.30–0.90; see repo for the full
table), i.e. robust to class-balance shift, though this robustness is
itself measured only within the simulator.

## 4. Limitations

This study uses a **simulated** population, not real transcripts. We could
not obtain network access to query even a small open-weight model in this
sandboxed environment (TLS verification against the Hugging Face Hub failed
on every attempt), nor offline access to labeled datasets released alongside
cited papers (e.g., Wei et al. 2024), which would have been our preferred
fallback for even n=50–100 real triples. Four of eight behavioral features
are generated as noisy, additive functions of the label the classifier
predicts, so high separability is partly definitional; Sec. 3.2–3.3 make
this precise, and Sec. 3.3 shows the practical, decision-relevant
consequence — a shrinking-to-negligible accuracy edge over naive flagging —
is far more sensitive to unknown real-world effect sizes than the AUC number
alone suggests. Sec. 3.4's extractor check establishes only that the
feature *names* correspond to something computable from text, using
examples we wrote ourselves to fit the hypothesis; it says nothing about
real effect magnitudes and should not be read as partial validation. We do
not know whether real transcripts differ by an amount closer to
`effect_scale=1.0`, `0.25` (downstream advantage ~1 point), or lower. The
released code is structured so `simulate_dataset()` can be replaced by a
feature extractor over real transcript triples with correctness labels from
an existing benchmark or human annotation, and a validated linguistic
extractor in place of the regex prototype. Until that replacement happens,
every number here should be read as validating the *evaluation methodology
and its falsifiability*, not real-world detection accuracy — also why we
retitled this revision to lead with "Toward."

## 5. Conclusion

We formalized live-deployment sycophancy auditing as a behavioral
classification problem and, on a literature-*motivated* (not
literature-*measured*) simulation, showed a classifier using
confidence-trajectory and hedging/validation-language features outperforms
naive "flag every change" (0.98 vs. 0.50 AUC, and 0.82 for a
confidence-only baseline) and that gating a revert policy on it raises
simulated decision accuracy from 71.7% to 94.2% at our default parameters.
Because several features are additive functions of the target label, we
quantified this two ways: an effect-size attenuation sweep showing AUC
degrades smoothly to chance (0.555), and — new in this revision — the same
sweep pushed through the downstream policy simulation, showing the
decision-accuracy advantage over naive flagging shrinks from 22.5 points to
about 1 point as effect sizes attenuate to a quarter of their assumed
magnitude, and vanishes at zero. We also prototyped, and explicitly
caveated, an offline check that the underlying features are extractable
from text at all. Together these make the paper's honest claim narrower
than its results table alone would suggest: the audit *framing* is
falsifiable and internally consistent, but the *magnitude* of benefit over
a naive policy is currently unknown for real deployments and could be much
smaller than 3.1's headline numbers imply. The protocol, all sweeps, and the
extractor prototype are released (`experiment.py`, `extractability_check.py`,
`results.json`) so this can be re-targeted at real transcript data once such
access is available.
