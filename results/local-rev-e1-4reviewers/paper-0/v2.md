# Detecting Confidence Sycophancy from Interaction Behavior Alone: A Simulation-Based Audit Protocol

## Abstract

AI assistants that abandon a correct answer in response to user pushback —
*sycophancy* — are increasingly documented as a socio-technical harm. Existing
sycophancy research mostly measures the phenomenon at the model-evaluation
stage, using ground-truth-labeled benchmarks unavailable to a deployed
product team auditing live traffic in real time. We ask a narrower,
deployment-relevant question: *can a lightweight classifier flag harmful
sycophantic answer-changes from interaction-observable behavior alone
(confidence trajectory, hedging language, response-length dynamics), without
ground-truth correctness and without over-flagging genuine self-corrections?*

We answer this with a **fully synthetic, hand-parameterized simulator**, not
real transcripts — we could not obtain network access to query even a small
model in this sandboxed environment. On simulated data, a random forest
trained on eight behavioral features separates harmful from beneficial
answer-changes at 0.98 AUC versus a 0.50-AUC "flag every change" baseline,
and a downstream policy simulation raises decision accuracy from 28.3%
(always trust the final answer) to 94.2% (classifier-gated reversion).
**These numbers should be read as a self-consistency check of the audit
protocol's evaluation machinery, not as a measurement of real-world
sycophancy detectability**: the simulator constructs its behavioral features
as noisy, class-conditional functions of the label the classifier is asked
to recover, so near-ceiling separability is expected by design. We quantify
exactly how much of this separability is attributable to the magnitude of
those hand-set effect offsets, rather than to the audit framing itself, via
a new effect-size attenuation ablation: scaling the class-conditional
offsets from 1.0 to 0.0 collapses RF AUC from 0.983 to 0.555 (chance),
confirming the classifier is not trivially near-ceiling regardless of
parameters, but also confirming that the headline number is a property of
the chosen effect sizes, which are our own operationalization loosely
motivated by *directional* findings in the literature, not values measured
in it. We release the simulator, an effect-size sensitivity sweep, 10-seed
variance estimates, and raw results so the protocol can be re-run or
re-targeted at real transcript logs.

## 1. Introduction

LLM assistants are increasingly used as decision-support tools — medical
triage, coding, financial and legal advice — where a user may push back on
an assistant's answer. A growing literature documents that assistants often
capitulate to pushback independent of whether new evidence was presented.
Almost all sycophancy measurement operates at the model-evaluation stage
with known ground truth. This does not transfer to **live deployment
monitoring**, where an operations team observing production transcripts
usually lacks ground-truth correctness at the moment of the interaction. A
naive heuristic — "flag any interaction where the answer changed" — conflates
harmful reversals with beneficial self-corrections, and over-flagging erodes
the trust calibration the audit is meant to protect.

This paper studies whether, given only interaction-observable behavioral
signals, a classifier can distinguish harmful sycophantic reversals from
beneficial corrections better than the naive heuristic. We answer this with
a **controlled simulation** rather than live transcripts, because our
compute budget is CPU-only and time-boxed, and because we could not obtain
network access to a real model (Sec. 4). We treat the contribution as the
*audit protocol and its evaluation methodology* — including, in this
revision, an explicit accounting of how much of the reported separability is
an artifact of the simulator's own construction, which is the central
concern raised by four independent reviewers of an earlier draft of this
paper and which we address directly in Sec. 3.2 below.

### 1.1 Related Work

**Documenting sycophancy.** Perez et al. (2022) and Wei et al. (2024) show
sycophancy emerging from RLHF-style preference optimization. A 2026 taxonomy
paper argues the term spans heterogeneous behaviors lacking a shared
operational definition, motivating our narrow definition (correct→incorrect
flip under pushback, absent new evidence).

**Behavioral and social consequences.** Multiple 2025–2026 studies (Science;
CHI; arXiv) provide behavioral evidence that sycophantic interactions harm
decision quality and user autonomy, and that novice users specifically fail
to self-detect sycophancy-driven errors — motivating an *automated* audit.
CHI 2026 work finds explicit and implicit sycophancy act through distinct
pathways, consistent with including both confidence-trajectory and
validating-language feature groups.

**Calibration.** "Calibration Collapse Under Sycophancy Fine-Tuning" (2026)
shows reward-optimized models can lose calibrated uncertainty exactly where
sycophancy is likely — i.e., confidence and sycophancy interact rather than
being independent, motivating confidence-trajectory features as audit
signal, not just the binary changed/unchanged flag.

**What "grounded in the literature" means here, precisely.** None of the
cited papers report a `hedge_latency` feature, a response-length-inflation
coefficient, or any numeric effect size matching our simulator's parameters
(e.g., the 0.25/-0.10 hedge-count offset, the Beta(2.2, 3.0) sycophancy
prior). What the literature supplies is the *direction* of several
qualitative findings (sycophantic capitulation is reflexive and
validation-heavy; genuine correction is more deliberative) and rough
population flip-rate ranges (20–70%+). The specific magnitudes are our own
operationalization choices, calibrated to produce a non-trivial, noisy
classification task — not values read off any cited table. We make this
explicit rather than implying literal numeric grounding, per feedback from
reviewers of an earlier draft.

**Gap.** We found no prior work framing sycophancy detection as a behavioral
audit classification problem solvable without ground truth. This is the gap
we address; the sensitivity analysis in Sec. 3.2 is, to our knowledge, the
first attempt to quantify how much such a result depends on the underlying
simulator's assumed effect sizes rather than the framing.

## 2. Method

### 2.1 Operational definitions

- **Answer changed**: the stated answer after pushback differs from before.
- **Sycophantic (harmful)**: answer changed AND the pre-pushback answer was
  correct (positive class, audit target).
- **Corrective (beneficial)**: answer changed AND the pre-pushback answer was
  incorrect (negative class within the changed subset).

The audit classifier only sees interactions where the answer changed and
must discriminate using **behavioral features only**, never the ground-truth
correctness label used to construct the target.

### 2.2 Simulator

`experiment.py` generates N=6,000 interactions. Each draws a persona
sycophancy propensity (Beta(2.2, 3.0)), pushback strength (Uniform(0.1,1)),
base task correctness (Bernoulli, p=0.72), and pre-pushback confidence
(Normal(0.78, 0.12), clipped). Whether the answer changes is a logistic
function of persona sycophancy and pushback strength, **independent of
correctness** — definitionally what makes sycophancy harmful.

Conditional on a change, eight behavioral features are generated with
class-conditional offsets: sycophantic reversals get lower hedge latency,
more validating language, an immediate confidence rebound, and modest
length inflation; corrective changes get higher hedge count/latency, less
validating language, and a larger, gradual confidence adjustment — each with
substantial per-interaction Gaussian/Bernoulli noise on top. **We flag
explicitly**: because these offsets are additive functions of the label
being predicted, the resulting classification task is closer to recovering
a partially-obscured signal than to an independent measurement — see the
effect-size ablation below, which quantifies this rather than leaving it
implicit. A `pushback_strength` feature is included as a negative control:
it drives whether a change occurs at all but carries no class-conditional
offset, so a well-behaved classifier should show near-chance performance on
it alone.

### 2.3 Classifiers and baselines

On the changed-answer subset (n_train≈3,015, n_test≈1,101, 71.7% positive),
we train: **naive "always flag"**, **majority class**, **Logistic
Regression**, and **Random Forest** (200 trees, depth 6) on the eight
behavioral features. We report AUC, precision, recall, F1 on a held-out 30%
stratified split.

### 2.4 Ablations

(a) **Feature groups**: confidence-only, language-only, pushback-only,
confidence+language. (b) **Base-rate sweep**: task accuracy ∈ {0.30, 0.50,
0.72, 0.90}. (c) **Effect-size attenuation sweep (new in this revision)**:
we introduce an `effect_scale` ∈ [0, 1] multiplier on every class-conditional
offset term in the four language/latency features, holding the shared
confounders (persona sycophancy, pushback strength) fixed. At
`effect_scale=0` the behavioral features carry no signal about the label
beyond what the shared confounders jointly induce, giving a concrete answer
to "what result would count as evidence the classifier isn't just recovering
an embedded label" — a question raised independently by two reviewers. (d)
**Multi-seed variance (new)**: we repeat the full pipeline (data generation,
split, RF fit, downstream simulation) across 10 seeds and report mean ± std,
addressing a reviewer request for variance beyond the single seed=0 run.

### 2.5 Downstream policy simulation

Using RF test-set predictions, we simulate three policies and compute
population decision accuracy: (1) **no audit** — always trust the final
answer; (2) **naive audit** — always revert on any change; (3) **smart (RF)
audit** — revert only when the classifier predicts sycophantic.

## 3. Results

All numbers are read directly from `results.json`, produced by a single run
of `experiment.py` (runtime 5.3s on CPU).

### 3.1 Main classification results (test n=1,101)

| Method | AUC | Precision | Recall | F1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Naive "always flag" | 0.500 | 0.717 | 1.000 | 0.835 |
| Majority class | 0.500 | 0.717 | 1.000 | 0.835 |
| Logistic Regression | 0.979 | 0.941 | 0.968 | 0.954 |
| Random Forest | **0.983** | 0.939 | 0.982 | **0.960** |

Across 10 seeds, RF AUC = 0.983 ± 0.004 and F1 = 0.959 ± 0.003 — the
single-seed result is not an outlier. The naive heuristics achieve high F1
only by recalling every positive at the cost of flagging all genuine
corrections too; AUC cleanly separates them from the behavioral classifiers.
**As emphasized in Sec. 2.2, this separability is expected given how the
simulator's features are constructed** (Sec. 3.2 quantifies how much).

### 3.2 How much of the 0.98 AUC is the simulator's construction? (new)

Because four of eight features are generated with additive, class-conditional
offsets, a reviewer of an earlier draft correctly noted that near-ceiling AUC
here validates `RandomForestClassifier`, not necessarily the audit protocol.
We quantify this by attenuating those offsets toward zero:

| effect_scale | RF AUC | RF F1 |
|---|---|---|
| 1.00 (paper default) | 0.983 | 0.960 |
| 0.75 | 0.946 | 0.931 |
| 0.50 | 0.862 | 0.882 |
| 0.25 | 0.704 | 0.839 |
| 0.00 (offsets removed) | 0.555 | 0.833 |

AUC degrades roughly monotonically to near-chance (0.555) once the
class-conditional offsets are entirely removed, leaving only the shared
confounders. Two conclusions follow. First, the classifier is *not*
trivially near-ceiling regardless of parameters — it degrades gracefully,
which is what a well-specified simulation should do, and rules out a purely
structural artifact (e.g., train/test leakage) rather than a magnitude
artifact. Second, and more importantly, **the headline 0.98 AUC is a direct,
roughly linear function of effect-size magnitudes we chose ourselves**, not
values measured from real assistant behavior. We report this ablation as the
primary honest bound on what Sec. 3.1 demonstrates: the audit *framing* is
falsifiable and testable (it is not true by definition that any simulator
would produce a smooth degradation curve — a broken pipeline could show flat
chance performance at all scales, or an actual leakage bug could stay flat
at ~0.98), but the specific *magnitude* claimed for real deployments is not
yet supported by anything other than our own parameter choices.

### 3.3 Feature-group ablation

| Feature group | AUC |
|---|---|
| pushback_strength only (negative control) | 0.509 |
| confidence features only | 0.818 |
| language features only | 0.963 |
| confidence + language | 0.977 |
| full RF model | 0.983 |

The negative control behaves as expected. Language features carry most of
the signal *in this simulation*; RF feature importances rank `hedge_count`
(0.32), `hedge_latency` (0.19), `length_delta` (0.19), and `conf_delta`
(0.19) highest.

### 3.4 Robustness to base rate

| Base task accuracy | Sycophantic-class rate | AUC | F1 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.30 | 0.296 | 0.984 | 0.884 |
| 0.50 | 0.512 | 0.981 | 0.930 |
| 0.72 | 0.716 | 0.980 | 0.954 |
| 0.90 | 0.902 | 0.981 | 0.983 |

AUC is stable (0.98 ± 0.004) across a 3× range of class balance.

### 3.5 Downstream policy simulation

| Policy | Decision accuracy (seed 0) | Mean ± std (10 seeds) |
|---|---|---|
| No audit | 28.3% | 28.3% ± 0.7% |
| Naive audit (revert on any change) | 71.7% | 71.7% ± 0.7% |
| Smart RF-gated audit | **94.2%** | **94.0% ± 0.5%** |

(RF audit warns on 74.9% of changed-answer interactions.) These figures
inherit the same caveat as Sec. 3.2: they measure how well a classifier
trained on this simulator's specific effect sizes can gate a policy *within
that same simulator*, not the accuracy gain achievable on real deployment
traffic.

## 4. Limitations

This study uses a **simulated** population, not real model transcripts. We
could not obtain network access to download or query even a small
open-weight model in this sandboxed environment (TLS certificate
verification against the Hugging Face Hub failed on every attempt); we also
do not have offline access to any of the labeled datasets released alongside
the cited papers (e.g., Wei et al. 2024), which would have been our
preferred fallback for even n=50–100 real (before, pushback, after) triples.
Beyond the general simulation-vs-reality gap, we highlight a more specific
issue raised independently by four reviewers: four of our eight behavioral
features are generated as noisy, additive functions of the very label the
classifier predicts, so high separability is partly definitional. Sec. 3.2's
effect-size sweep is our attempt to make this precise rather than let it
stay implicit — but it cannot substitute for real data, since we do not know
whether real sycophantic and corrective transcripts differ by an amount
closer to `effect_scale=1.0` or `effect_scale=0.25` (AUC 0.70) or lower. The
released code is structured so `simulate_dataset()` can be replaced by a
feature extractor over real transcript triples, with correctness labels from
an existing benchmark or small-scale human annotation, and with a real
linguistic feature extractor (regex or NLI-based) rather than assumed
distributions. Until that replacement happens, every number in this paper
should be read as a validation of the *evaluation methodology and its
falsifiability* (Sec. 3.2), not as an estimate of real-world detection
accuracy.

## 5. Conclusion

We formalized live-deployment sycophancy auditing as a behavioral
classification problem and, on a literature-*motivated* (not
literature-*measured*) simulation, showed a classifier using
confidence-trajectory and hedging/validation-language features outperforms
the naive "flag every change" heuristic (0.98 vs. 0.50 AUC, stable at
0.983 ± 0.004 over 10 seeds) and that gating a revert policy on it raises
simulated decision accuracy from 71.7% to 94.0% ± 0.5%. Because several
features are constructed as additive functions of the target label, we added
an effect-size attenuation ablation showing this separability degrades
smoothly to chance (AUC 0.555) as the encoded offsets are removed — evidence
the result is falsifiable in principle, and an explicit, quantified
statement of how much of it currently rests on our own parameter choices
rather than measured behavior. The protocol, sensitivity analysis, and full
results are released (`experiment.py`, `results.json`) so it can be
re-targeted at real transcript data once such access is available.
