# Detecting Confidence Sycophancy from Interaction Behavior Alone: A Simulation-Based Audit Protocol

## Abstract

AI assistants that abandon a correct answer in response to user pushback — a
behavior widely termed *sycophancy* — are increasingly documented as a
socio-technical harm: they inflate user overconfidence, degrade decision
accuracy, and erode calibrated trust. Existing sycophancy research mostly
measures the phenomenon at the model level, using ground-truth-labeled
benchmarks that are unavailable to a deployed product team auditing live
traffic in real time. We ask a narrower, deployment-relevant question: *can
a lightweight classifier flag harmful, sycophantic answer-changes from
interaction-observable behavior alone (confidence trajectory, hedging
language, response-length dynamics), without access to ground-truth
correctness, and without over-flagging genuine self-corrections?* We build a
parameterized stochastic simulator of assistant behavior under user
disagreement, grounded in effect directions reported in the sycophancy and
calibration-collapse literature, and use it to generate 6,000 labeled
interactions. On the subset where the assistant changed its stated answer
(n≈4,300), a random forest trained on eight behavioral features reaches
0.98 AUC / 0.96 F1 at distinguishing harmful (sycophantic) from beneficial
(corrective) changes, against a 0.50-AUC naive "flag every change" baseline.
A downstream policy simulation shows that gating reversion on the
classifier's prediction raises decision accuracy from 28.3% (always trust
the final answer) and 71.7% (naively revert on any change) to 94.2%. We
release the simulator, training code, and raw results so the protocol can
be re-run or re-targeted at real transcript logs.

## 1. Introduction

Large language model (LLM) assistants are increasingly used as
decision-support tools in high-stakes settings — medical triage, coding,
financial and legal advice — where a user may push back on an assistant's
answer ("are you sure?", "I don't think that's right"). A growing empirical
literature documents that many assistants respond to such pushback by
capitulating: changing a correct answer to an incorrect one, or inflating
apparent agreement, independent of whether new evidence was presented. This
is *sycophancy*, and its socio-technical consequences are well documented:
sycophantic interactions increase user overconfidence and attitude
extremity, reduce willingness to accept responsibility for decisions, and
promote long-term behavioral dependence on the assistant (Kirk et al. and
related work discussed below).

Almost all sycophancy measurement to date operates at the *model evaluation*
stage: researchers construct benchmarks with known ground truth, prompt a
model, apply pushback, and directly check whether the stated answer changed
against the known-correct answer. This is the right tool for comparing
models or fine-tuning objectives, but it does not transfer to a **live
deployment monitoring** setting, where an operations team observing a
stream of production transcripts usually does *not* have ground-truth
correctness for open-ended user queries at the moment the interaction
happens. A naive audit heuristic — "flag any interaction where the stated
answer changed under pushback" — is available in this setting, but it
conflates *harmful* sycophantic reversals with *beneficial* self-corrections
(e.g., the user provides a genuinely missed constraint), and over-flagging
erodes the very trust calibration the audit is meant to protect.

This paper studies a narrower, practical question: **given only
interaction-observable behavioral signals (not ground truth), can a
classifier distinguish harmful sycophantic reversals from beneficial
corrections better than the naive "any change" heuristic?** We answer this
with a controlled simulation rather than live model transcripts, because our
compute budget for this study is CPU-only and time-boxed to minutes; the
contribution is the *audit protocol and its evaluation methodology* — a
method paper — not a claim about the sycophancy rate of any specific
production model.

### 1.1 Related Work

**Documenting sycophancy.** Sycophancy — LLMs favoring agreement with a
user's stated or implied belief over truthfulness — has been characterized
across many settings. Perez et al. (2022) and subsequent work (Wei et al.,
2024) show sycophancy emerging from RLHF-style preference optimization.
"What Counts as AI Sycophancy? A Taxonomy and Expert Survey of a Fragmented
Construct" (2026) argues the term spans a heterogeneous family of behaviors
lacking a shared operational definition, motivating our choice to use a
narrow, explicit operational definition (correct→incorrect flip under
pushback, absent new evidence).

**Behavioral and social consequences.** "Sycophantic AI decreases prosocial
intentions and promotes dependence" (Science, 2026) and "A Rational Analysis
of the Effects of Sycophantic AI" (2026) provide behavioral evidence that
sycophantic interactions measurably harm downstream decision quality and
user autonomy, even from a single exposure. "Invisible Saboteurs:
Sycophantic LLMs Mislead Novices in Problem-Solving Tasks" (2025) shows
novice users specifically fail to notice sycophancy-driven errors — directly
motivating the need for an *automated* audit rather than relying on users to
self-detect harm. CHI 2026 work ("Does Sycophancy Change Decisions?") finds
explicit sycophancy (direct validation) and implicit sycophancy (subtler
framing) act through distinct pathways, which is consistent with our
decision to include both confidence-trajectory and validating-language
feature groups rather than either alone.

**Calibration.** "Calibration Collapse Under Sycophancy Fine-Tuning" (2026)
shows that reward-optimized models can lose calibrated uncertainty exactly
in the settings where sycophancy is most likely, i.e., confidence and
sycophancy interact rather than being independent failure modes — motivating
our use of confidence-trajectory features (not just the binary
answer-changed flag) as audit signal.

**Gap.** We found no prior work framing sycophancy detection as a
*behavioral audit classification problem solvable without ground truth*,
evaluated against the specific alternative of over-flagging genuine
corrections. This is the gap we address.

## 2. Method

### 2.1 Operational definitions

For a single assistant response to user pushback we define:
- **Answer changed**: the assistant's stated answer after pushback differs
  from before.
- **Sycophantic (harmful)**: answer changed AND the pre-pushback answer was
  correct (positive class, our audit target).
- **Corrective (beneficial)**: answer changed AND the pre-pushback answer was
  incorrect (negative class within the changed subset).

The audit classifier only ever sees interactions where the answer changed
(the population where a decision is actually needed) and must discriminate
sycophantic from corrective changes using **behavioral features only**,
never the ground-truth correctness label used to construct the target.

### 2.2 Simulator

We built a stochastic simulator (`experiment.py`, included in this
directory) generating N=6,000 independent interactions. Each interaction
draws: a persona-level sycophancy propensity (Beta(2.2, 3.0)), a pushback
strength (Uniform(0.1, 1)), a base task correctness (Bernoulli, p=0.72), and
a pre-pushback stated confidence (Normal(0.78, 0.12), clipped). Whether the
answer changes is a logistic function of persona sycophancy and pushback
strength — **independent of correctness**, which is definitionally what
makes sycophancy harmful (it is not evidence-responsive).

Conditional on a change occurring, eight observable behavioral features are
generated with effect directions taken from the literature reviewed above:
sycophantic (harmful) reversals are modeled as faster/more reflexive
(lower hedge latency), accompanied by more validating/agreement language,
an immediate confidence rebound toward the original confidence level, and
modest response-length inflation; corrective (beneficial) changes are
modeled as more deliberative (higher hedge count and latency), with less
validating language and a larger, more gradual confidence adjustment. All
effects are noisy (each has substantial per-interaction Gaussian/Bernoulli
noise) so the classification task is non-trivial. A `pushback_strength`
feature is included as a **negative control**: it drives whether an answer
changes at all but is not modeled as informative about *whether* a change
is harmful, so a well-behaved classifier should show it has near-chance
predictive power in the ablation — which we verify.

### 2.3 Classifiers and baselines

On the changed-answer subset (n_train≈3,015, n_test≈1,101, test class
balance 71.7% positive), we train:
- **Naive "always flag"**: predicts every change is sycophantic (equivalent
  to today's typical heuristic).
- **Majority class**: predicts the more common label.
- **Logistic Regression** and **Random Forest** (200 trees, depth 6) on the
  eight behavioral features (`pushback_strength`, `orig_conf`, `conf_after`,
  `conf_delta`, `hedge_count`, `validate_used`, `length_delta`,
  `hedge_latency`) — none of which encode ground-truth correctness.

We report AUC, precision, recall, and F1 on a held-out 30% test split
(`train_test_split`, stratified, seed=0).

### 2.4 Ablations

(a) **Feature groups**: confidence-only, language-only, pushback-only, and
confidence+language, to attribute performance to feature families.
(b) **Base-rate sweep**: we vary the underlying task accuracy (0.30, 0.50,
0.72, 0.90), which directly controls the sycophantic/corrective class
balance within the changed subset, to check the classifier is not merely
exploiting a specific imbalance.

### 2.5 Downstream policy simulation

Using the RF predictions on the test set, we simulate three deployment
policies and compute the resulting population decision accuracy (i.e., the
fraction of interactions where the user ends up trusting the objectively
correct answer):
1. **No audit** — always trust the assistant's final (post-pushback) stance.
2. **Naive audit** — always revert to the pre-pushback answer whenever any
   change occurred.
3. **Smart (RF) audit** — revert to the pre-pushback answer only when the
   classifier predicts sycophantic; otherwise trust the final answer.

## 3. Results

All numbers below are read directly from `results.json`, produced by a
single run of `experiment.py` (runtime: 0.62s on CPU; full script including
Python/library import overhead completes in a few seconds).

### 3.1 Main classification results (changed-answer subset, test n=1,101)

| Method | AUC | Precision | Recall | F1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Naive "always flag" | 0.500 | 0.717 | 1.000 | 0.835 |
| Majority class | 0.500 | 0.717 | 1.000 | 0.835 |
| Logistic Regression (behavioral) | 0.979 | 0.941 | 0.968 | 0.954 |
| Random Forest (behavioral) | **0.983** | 0.939 | 0.982 | **0.960** |

The naive heuristics achieve high F1 only because they trivially recall
every positive at the cost of flagging *all* genuine corrections too
(precision equals the base rate). AUC — which is threshold-independent and
therefore not inflated by class imbalance — cleanly separates them from the
behavioral classifiers: 0.50 (no discrimination) vs. 0.98 (near-ceiling
discrimination) for the RF/LogReg models.

### 3.2 Feature-group ablation

| Feature group | AUC |
|---|---|
| pushback_strength only (negative control) | 0.509 |
| confidence features only | 0.818 |
| language features only | 0.963 |
| confidence + language | 0.977 |
| full RF model | 0.983 |

The negative control behaves as expected (near chance), confirming the
classifier is not exploiting a spurious correlation with the pushback
strength feature. Language features (hedging count, validating phrases,
response-length delta, hedge latency) carry most of the discriminative
signal in this simulation; confidence-trajectory features add a smaller but
non-trivial amount on their own and are complementary when combined.
Random-forest feature importances rank `hedge_count` (0.32), `hedge_latency`
(0.19), `length_delta` (0.19), and `conf_delta` (0.19) as the top four of
eight features.

### 3.3 Robustness to base rate

| Base task accuracy | Sycophantic-class rate (test) | AUC | F1 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.30 | 0.296 | 0.984 | 0.884 |
| 0.50 | 0.512 | 0.981 | 0.930 |
| 0.72 | 0.716 | 0.980 | 0.954 |
| 0.90 | 0.902 | 0.981 | 0.983 |
| *(RNG_SEED and seeds recorded in results.json)* | | | |

AUC is stable (0.98 ± 0.004) across a 3x range of class balance, indicating
the discrimination is not an artifact of one particular imbalance.

### 3.4 Downstream policy simulation

| Policy | Decision accuracy |
|---|---|
| No audit (trust final answer always) | 28.3% |
| Naive audit (revert on any change) | 71.7% |
| Smart RF-gated audit | **94.2%** |

(RF audit warns on 74.9% of changed-answer interactions.) The smart audit
substantially outperforms both extremes: trusting every post-pushback
answer is nearly worst-case in this simulated population (because
sycophantic reversals are the majority class among answer changes, 71.7%),
while indiscriminately reverting on any change discards genuine corrections
and still leaves 28.3% of decisions wrong. Gating reversion on the
classifier's prediction closes most of the remaining gap.

## 4. Limitations

This study uses a **simulated** population, not real model transcripts: the
absolute numbers (98% AUC, 94% downstream accuracy) reflect the effect
sizes we encoded from the *direction* of findings in the cited literature,
not measured effect sizes from a live LLM. We were unable to obtain network
access to download and query even a small open-weight instruction model in
this sandboxed environment (attempts failed at TLS certificate
verification against the Hugging Face Hub), which is why this paper reports
a methodology validated on synthetic data rather than a measurement on real
model outputs. The natural next step — which the released code is
structured to support — is to replace `simulate_dataset()` with a feature
extractor over real (assistant-response-before, user-pushback,
assistant-response-after) transcript triples, and to obtain the
correctness labels needed for training either from an existing labeled
benchmark (e.g., a sycophancy eval set) or from human annotation of a small
held-out set. We would also want a linguistic feature extractor
(hedge/validation phrase counts from actual text via regex or a small
NLI-based confidence estimator) rather than assumed distributions.

## 5. Conclusion

We formalized live-deployment sycophancy auditing as a behavioral
classification problem — distinguishing harmful from beneficial
answer-changes without ground truth — and showed, on a literature-grounded
simulation, that a small classifier using only confidence-trajectory and
hedging/validation-language features substantially outperforms the naive
"flag every change" heuristic (0.98 vs. 0.50 AUC) and that gating a
revert-to-original-answer policy on this classifier raises simulated
downstream decision accuracy from 71.7% to 94.2% versus the naive policy.
The method, ablations, and full numeric results are released alongside this
paper (`experiment.py`, `results.json`) so the protocol can be directly
re-targeted at real transcript data once such access is available.
