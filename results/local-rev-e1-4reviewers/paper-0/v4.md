# Toward Detecting Confidence Sycophancy from Interaction Behavior Alone: A Simulation-Based Audit Protocol and Its Circularity Bounds

## Abstract

AI assistants that abandon a correct answer under user pushback —
*sycophancy* — are a documented harm. Most sycophancy research measures the
phenomenon at model-evaluation time with ground-truth labels unavailable to
a product team auditing live traffic. We ask a narrower question: *can a
lightweight classifier flag harmful sycophantic answer-changes from
interaction-observable behavior alone (confidence trajectory, hedging
language, response-length dynamics), without ground-truth correctness and
without over-flagging genuine self-corrections?*

We answer this with a **fully synthetic, hand-parameterized simulator**, not
real transcripts — we had no network access in this sandboxed environment. A
random forest on eight behavioral features separates harmful from beneficial
answer-changes at 0.98 AUC versus 0.50 for a naive "flag every change"
baseline and 0.82 for a confidence-only baseline, and a downstream policy
simulation raises decision accuracy from 28.3% to 94.2%. **These numbers are
a self-consistency check of the audit protocol's evaluation machinery, not a
measurement of real-world detectability**: four of eight features are
noisy, class-conditional functions of the label the classifier recovers, so
near-ceiling separability is partly definitional. We quantify this several
ways. An effect-size attenuation sweep collapses RF AUC from 0.983 to 0.555
(chance) as class-conditional offsets shrink to zero — smooth, not flat,
ruling out pure leakage but confirming the headline number is a function of
effect sizes we chose. Pushed through the downstream policy simulation, the
RF audit's advantage over "always revert on any change" shrinks from 94.2%
vs. 71.7% at default effect size to 72.8% vs. 71.7% at a quarter that
magnitude, and vanishes at zero. New in this revision, we (a) extend the offline text-extractability check
beyond hedge/validation language to length and confidence-trajectory
features and find one of them — length — runs **opposite** the simulator's
assumed direction on our own hand-written examples; and (b) rebuild the
simulator under a structurally different (multiplicative, not additive)
generative mechanism and confirm the ordering RF > confidence-only > naive
survives, i.e. is not an artifact of the additive form specifically. We
release the simulator, all sweeps, both extractability checks, the
alternate-form check, and 10-seed variance so the protocol can be
re-targeted at real logs.

## 1. Introduction

LLM assistants increasingly serve as decision-support tools — medical
triage, coding, financial and legal advice — where users push back on
answers. A growing literature documents assistants capitulating to pushback
regardless of whether new evidence was presented. Almost all sycophancy
measurement happens at the model-evaluation stage with known ground truth,
which does not transfer to **live deployment monitoring**, where an
operations team observing production transcripts usually lacks
ground-truth correctness at the moment of interaction. A naive heuristic —
"flag any interaction where the answer changed" — conflates harmful
reversals with beneficial self-corrections, and over-flagging erodes trust.

This paper studies whether interaction-observable behavioral signals alone
let a classifier beat that naive heuristic. We answer with a **controlled
simulation** rather than live transcripts: compute is CPU-only and we could
not obtain network access to a real model (Sec. 4). The contribution is the
*audit protocol and its evaluation methodology*, including an explicit,
quantified accounting of how much reported separability is an artifact of
the simulator's own construction — the concern raised independently by all
four reviewers of prior drafts, addressed in Sec. 3.2–3.3 — plus two new
checks: (i) whether the features are measurable from text at all, extended
beyond two of the eight features (Sec. 3.4), and (ii) whether the result
depends on the additive functional form (Sec. 3.5).

### 1.1 Related work

**Documenting sycophancy.** Perez et al. (2022) and Wei et al. (2024) show
sycophancy emerging from RLHF-style preference optimization; a 2026 taxonomy
paper argues the term spans heterogeneous behaviors lacking a shared
operational definition, motivating our narrow definition (correct→incorrect
flip under pushback, absent new evidence). Multiple 2025–2026 studies
(Science; CHI; arXiv) show sycophantic interactions harm decision quality
and user autonomy, and that novices fail to self-detect sycophancy-driven
errors — motivating an *automated* audit; CHI 2026 work finds explicit and
implicit sycophancy act through distinct pathways, consistent with including
both confidence-trajectory and validating-language feature groups.
"Calibration Collapse Under Sycophancy Fine-Tuning" (2026) shows
reward-optimized models can lose calibrated uncertainty exactly where
sycophancy is likely, motivating confidence-trajectory features rather than
a binary changed/unchanged flag.

**What "grounded in the literature" means here.** None of the cited papers
report a `hedge_latency` feature, a length-inflation coefficient, or any
numeric effect size matching our simulator. The literature supplies
*direction* (capitulation is reflexive and validation-heavy; correction is
deliberative) and rough flip-rate ranges (20–70%+); the magnitudes are our
own operationalization, not values read off any cited table.

**Gap.** We found no prior work framing sycophancy detection as a
ground-truth-free behavioral audit classification problem. Sec. 3.2–3.3
quantify how much such a result depends on assumed effect sizes rather than
the framing, carried through to the policy-level decision a deployment team
would act on; Sec. 3.5 is new and checks whether that framing survives a
different generative mechanism.

## 2. Method

### 2.1 Operational definitions

- **Answer changed**: the stated answer after pushback differs from before.
- **Sycophantic (harmful)**: answer changed AND pre-pushback answer correct.
- **Corrective (beneficial)**: answer changed AND pre-pushback answer
  incorrect.

The audit classifier sees only changed-answer interactions and discriminates
using **behavioral features only**, never the correctness label used to
construct the target.

### 2.2 Simulator

`experiment.py` generates N=6,000 interactions: persona sycophancy
propensity (Beta(2.2,3.0)), pushback strength (Uniform(0.1,1)), base
correctness (Bernoulli, p=0.72), pre-pushback confidence (Normal(0.78,0.12),
clipped). Whether the answer changes is logistic in persona sycophancy and
pushback strength, **independent of correctness** — definitionally what
makes sycophancy harmful.

Conditional on a change, eight features are generated with class-conditional
offsets: sycophantic reversals get lower hedge latency, more validating
language, an immediate confidence rebound, modest length inflation;
corrective changes get more hedging, less validation, a larger, gradual
confidence adjustment — each with substantial noise. Because these offsets
are additive functions of the label, the task is closer to recovering a
partially-obscured signal than an independent measurement (Sec. 3.2–3.3,
stress-tested structurally in Sec. 3.5). `pushback_strength` is a negative
control: it drives whether a change occurs but carries no class-conditional
offset.

### 2.3 Classifiers and baselines

On the changed-answer subset (n_train≈3,015, n_test≈1,101, 71.7% positive),
we train **naive "always flag"**, **majority class**, **Logistic
Regression**, and **Random Forest** (200 trees, depth 6) on the eight
features, reporting AUC, precision, recall, F1 on a held-out 30% split.

### 2.4 Ablations and robustness checks

(a) **Feature groups**: confidence-only, language-only, pushback-only,
confidence+language. (b) **Base-rate sweep**: task accuracy ∈
{0.30,0.50,0.72,0.90}. (c) **Effect-size attenuation sweep**: `effect_scale`
∈[0,1] scales every class-conditional offset in the four language/latency
features. (d) **Downstream-at-attenuation**: the full policy simulation
(Sec. 2.5) re-run at each `effect_scale`. (e) **Multi-seed variance**: 10
seeds, mean±std. (f) **Feature extractability check, extended**: a plain
regex/heuristic pipeline over six hand-authored response pairs, now
covering `hedge_count`, `validate_used`, `length_delta`, and a crude
confidence-trajectory proxy (Sec. 3.4), not just the first two as before.
(g) **Alternate functional form (new)**: a second simulator,
`simulate_dataset_multiplicative()`, where class membership scales
feature-generating rate parameters multiplicatively instead of additively —
used to test whether the qualitative classifier ordering survives (Sec.
3.5).

### 2.5 Downstream policy simulation

Using RF test-set predictions, we simulate three policies over population
decision accuracy: (1) **no audit** — always trust the final answer; (2)
**naive audit** — always revert on any change; (3) **smart (RF) audit** —
revert only when the classifier predicts sycophantic.

## 3. Results

All numbers are read directly from `results.json` and
`extractability_results.json`, produced by single runs of `experiment.py`
(7.5s) and `extractability_check.py`.

### 3.1 Main classification results (test n=1,101)

| Method | AUC | Precision | Recall | F1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Naive "always flag" | 0.500 | 0.717 | 1.000 | 0.835 |
| Confidence-only (informative baseline) | 0.818 | 0.816 | 0.944 | 0.875 |
| Logistic Regression (all 8 features) | 0.979 | 0.941 | 0.968 | 0.954 |
| Random Forest (all 8 features) | **0.983** | 0.939 | 0.982 | **0.960** |

Across 10 seeds, RF AUC = 0.983±0.004, F1 = 0.959±0.003. RF and LogReg are
close (0.983 vs. 0.979); given the gap is within seed noise, an audit team
would likely prefer LogReg's inspectable coefficients over RF's marginal
edge — we report RF as headline only because it is uniformly (if slightly)
better across every ablation and seed, not because the gap warrants the
added complexity.

### 3.2 How much of the 0.98 AUC is the simulator's construction?

| effect_scale | RF AUC | RF F1 |
|---|---|---|
| 1.00 (paper default) | 0.983 | 0.960 |
| 0.75 | 0.946 | 0.931 |
| 0.50 | 0.862 | 0.882 |
| 0.25 | 0.704 | 0.839 |
| 0.00 (offsets removed) | 0.555 | 0.833 |

Degradation is smooth, not flat — ruling out pure leakage — but the headline
0.98 AUC is roughly a function of effect-size magnitudes we chose, not
values measured from real behavior.

### 3.3 Does the downstream accuracy gain survive attenuation?

| effect_scale | No audit | Naive (always revert) | Smart (RF) audit | Warn rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1.00 | 28.3% | 71.7% | **94.2%** | 74.9% |
| 0.75 | 28.3% | 71.7% | 89.8% | 76.0% |
| 0.50 | 28.3% | 71.7% | 81.6% | 84.1% |
| 0.25 | 28.3% | 71.7% | 72.8% | 97.3% |
| 0.00 | 28.3% | 71.7% | 71.7% | 98.0% |

The RF audit's advantage over "revert on everything" collapses almost
entirely by `effect_scale=0.25` (warn rate 97.3%, gain ~1 point) and
vanishes at 0. Since Sec. 4 cannot rule out real transcripts sitting
anywhere in this range, 94.2% is a best case under our own assumed effect
sizes, not a projected deployment gain.

### 3.4 Are the behavioral features even measurable in text? (extended)

The prior revision's extractor covered only `hedge_count` and
`validate_used` — two reviewers independently asked us to extend it to
features with comparable RF importance (`conf_delta` 0.19, `length_delta`
0.19, vs. `hedge_count` 0.32). We now also compute `length_delta` (already
collected but previously unreported) and a crude `immediate_rebound_proxy`
(whether the first clause, before any comma/dash, commits to an answer
without a hedge word — the closest a regex can get to "immediate vs.
gradual" without model internals) over the same six hand-authored pairs:

| | hedge_count | validate rate | length_delta | immediate-rebound rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sycophantic-style | 0.0 | 100% | **−0.3** | 100% |
| Corrective-style | 4.7 | 0% | **+20.7** | 67% |

Two of four features extend cleanly in the hypothesized direction
(hedge_count, validate rate). The other two do not, and we report this
rather than only the confirming pair: `length_delta` runs **opposite** the
simulator's assumption on these examples — our hand-written corrective
example is longer, deliberative, and hedged, our sycophantic one is short
and blunt — a genuine tension between the simulator's length-inflation
assumption and how we ourselves wrote a capitulation versus a correction.
`immediate_rebound_proxy` (100% vs. 67%) is barely discriminating and a weak
heuristic regardless. This is still circular — six examples we wrote to
match a hypothesis, now showing the hypothesis does not even fully survive
contact with our own writing — but it is more informative than the
two-feature version, and weakens rather than strengthens confidence in
`length_delta`, which we now flag as the feature least supported by any
evidence beyond the simulator.

### 3.5 Does the result depend on the additive functional form? (new)

Sections 3.2–3.4 bound *magnitude*. A separate question, raised by a
reviewer, is whether the ordering (RF > confidence-only > naive) is a
property of sycophancy auditing as a task, or an artifact of specifically
*additive* class-conditional offsets. We rebuilt the simulator with class
membership acting **multiplicatively** on rate parameters (hedge/validation
multipliers, and a multiplicative confidence-rebound blend toward an anchor
rather than an additive shift) — a structurally different mechanism, not a
rescaled version of the same one — and re-ran the same comparison at that
simulator's default parameters:

| Method | AUC (multiplicative form) |
|---|---|
| Naive "always flag" | 0.500 |
| Confidence-only | 0.990 |
| Random Forest | 1.000 |

The ordering RF > confidence-only > naive is preserved, and confidence alone
is even more discriminating here because the multiplicative rebound blend
injects a stronger confidence signal than the additive form did. This is
reassuring but not a second independent validation: both simulators encode
the same hand-chosen *direction* of effects, so it rules out "additive
offsets specifically" as the source of separability without ruling out that
the behavioral hypothesis itself is wrong for real transcripts.

### 3.6 Feature-group ablation and base-rate robustness

| Feature group | AUC |
|---|---|
| pushback_strength only (negative control) | 0.509 |
| confidence features only | 0.818 |
| language features only | 0.963 |
| confidence + language | 0.977 |
| full RF model | 0.983 |

Language features dominate the ablation, but this ranking reflects where we
happened to inject the strongest synthetic effect sizes, not a claim about
which signal *type* matters most in real deployment — a point one reviewer
pressed us on, and Sec. 3.4's finding that `length_delta` (a language-group
feature) doesn't even survive our own hand-written check reinforces that
this ranking should not be over-read. RF feature importances: `hedge_count`
(0.32), `hedge_latency` (0.19), `length_delta`
(0.19), `conf_delta` (0.19). AUC is stable (0.98±0.004) across a 3× range of
base task accuracy (0.30–0.90; full table in repo).

## 4. Limitations

This study uses a **simulated** population, not real transcripts. We could
not obtain network access to query even a small open-weight model in this
sandboxed environment (TLS verification against the Hugging Face Hub failed
every attempt), nor offline access to labeled datasets released alongside
cited papers, which would have been our preferred fallback for even
n=50–100 real triples. Four of eight behavioral features are noisy,
additive functions of the label the classifier predicts, so high
separability is partly definitional; Sec. 3.2–3.3 make this precise, and
Sec. 3.3 shows the shrinking-to-negligible accuracy edge over naive flagging
is far more sensitive to unknown real-world effect sizes than the AUC number
alone suggests. Sec. 3.4's extended extractor check establishes only that
some feature *names* correspond to something computable from text, using
examples we wrote ourselves; it now also shows `length_delta` contradicting
our own hypothesis on our own examples, reason for lower, not higher,
confidence in that feature pending real data. Sec. 3.5's alternate
functional form rules out one narrow objection (additive-form artifact) but
does not validate the behavioral hypothesis itself. We do not know whether
real transcripts differ by an amount closer to `effect_scale=1.0`, `0.25`
(downstream advantage ~1 point), or lower, or whether `length_delta`
generalizes at all. The released code is structured so `simulate_dataset()`
can be replaced by a feature extractor over real transcript triples with
correctness labels from an existing benchmark or human annotation. Until
then, every number here validates the *evaluation methodology and its
falsifiability*, not real-world detection accuracy.

## 5. Conclusion

We formalized live-deployment sycophancy auditing as a behavioral
classification problem and, on a literature-*motivated* (not
literature-*measured*) simulation, showed a classifier using
confidence-trajectory and hedging/validation-language features outperforms
naive "flag every change" (0.98 vs. 0.50 AUC, 0.82 for confidence-only) and
that gating a revert policy on it raises simulated decision accuracy from
71.7% to 94.2% at default parameters. Because several features are additive
functions of the target label, we quantified this via an attenuation sweep
(AUC degrades smoothly to chance) pushed through the downstream simulation
(advantage over naive flagging shrinks from 22.5 to ~1 point at a quarter of
assumed effect sizes, vanishing at zero). New this revision: extending the
text-extractability check surfaced a genuine tension — `length_delta` runs
opposite our own hypothesis on hand-written examples, reported as evidence
*against* over-trusting that feature, not smoothed over; and rebuilding the
simulator under a multiplicative mechanism shows RF > confidence-only >
naive survives, ruling out one narrow objection without validating the
behavioral hypothesis against real data. The claim stays narrow: the audit
*framing* is falsifiable and consistent across two mechanisms, one feature
is now flagged as weakly supported, and the *magnitude* of benefit over a
naive policy in real deployments remains unknown and could be much smaller
than Sec. 3.1 implies. The protocol and both extractability/robustness
checks are released (`experiment.py`, `extractability_check.py`,
`results.json`) for re-targeting at real transcript data once access is
available.
