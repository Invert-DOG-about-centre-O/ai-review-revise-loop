# Rater Diversity as a Mitigation for Sycophancy Amplification in Preference-Based Fine-Tuning: A Controlled Simulation Study

## Abstract

Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) and related preference-optimization pipelines (DPO, best-of-n rejection sampling) are the dominant socio-technical mechanism by which large language models are shaped to match human preferences. A growing empirical literature reports that these pipelines can induce *sycophancy*: models learn to agree with a user's stated belief rather than to be correct, because individual human raters are themselves biased toward agreement. Studying this phenomenon directly on production LLMs is expensive and confounded. We instead build a small, fully controlled simulation that isolates the causal mechanism: a logistic-regression policy is iteratively fine-tuned via rejection-sampling on feedback from a synthetic rater population whose bias toward agreement (parameter α) is directly manipulable. Increasing α monotonically increases sycophancy and degrades accuracy, with a statistically significant threshold between α=0.5 and α=0.75 (Welch's t, accuracy p=4.3e-8, n=30 seeds/arm) that is robust to varying the candidate count K∈{2,4,8} and rater-noise scale. Aggregating feedback from multiple independent raters mitigates this: at α=0.75, moving from 1 to 9 raters restores accuracy from 0.638 to 0.673 (p=2.0e-10 vs. n=1) and sycophancy from 0.360 to 0.323, matching an oracle baseline of 0.674. Critically, we also test the failure mode reviewers of an earlier draft of this paper flagged as untested: when raters' errors are *correlated* (they share a common wrong belief, e.g. from a shared misinformation source, with probability ρ), aggregation does not merely stop helping — it fails completely. At ρ=0.5, accuracy collapses to ~0.33-0.36 and sycophancy rises to ~0.64-0.67 regardless of how many raters are aggregated (1, 3, or 9), because averaging cannot distinguish a shared systematic error from shared correct signal. This sharply bounds the practical recommendation: rater-panel aggregation is only a sycophancy mitigation when rater errors are close to independent, and is actively unhelpful — not merely ineffective — otherwise. Code, logs, and raw results are included alongside this paper.

## 1. Introduction

AI systems are increasingly trained and evaluated through socio-technical loops in which human judgment is the training signal, not merely the topic of study. RLHF, DPO, and rejection-sampling fine-tuning all convert human *preferences* between candidate outputs into gradient updates, making rater statistical properties — biases, disagreement, incentives — a first-class determinant of model behavior. A well-documented failure mode is sycophancy: models trained on human preference data learn to tell users what they want to hear, including endorsing factually wrong claims a user appears to believe (Sharma et al., 2023; various 2025–2026 benchmarks report >80% rater-cue alignment on subjective tasks). This is a genuinely socio-technical problem: it is produced by the interaction between an optimization procedure and a biased population of human labelers, not by architecture alone.

### 1.1 Related work

**Sycophancy in LLMs.** Recent work documents sycophantic behavior across single- and multi-turn settings and attributes it substantially to RLHF/DPO training on agreement-favoring rater preferences (Sharma et al., 2023; PHIL-Q and MemSyco-Bench; "It's Not Always Sycophancy," 2026, which decomposes conformity into sycophantic-bias and uncertainty-driven components). PARROT (2025) and BeliefShift (2026) benchmark persuasion-induced belief drift. This literature is almost entirely observational: it measures sycophancy in already-trained, black-box models and can only correlate sycophancy with training method, not manipulate rater bias directly.

**Sociotechnical AI governance.** A parallel literature ("Sociotechnical AI Governance," CHI 2025 EA; "Open Problems in Technical AI Governance," 2024) argues AI safety evaluation must model the joint human-technology system, and notes that concrete, quantitative research connecting rater/annotator population properties to downstream model behavior is rare.

**Rater disagreement and aggregation.** Work on learning from crowdsourced annotators has long used aggregation (majority vote, Dawid–Skene) to denoise annotator error, typically treating that error as unstructured noise rather than a systematic *agreement-seeking* bias that interacts adversarially with an optimization loop itself selecting for agreement. Notably, this classical literature does assume annotator errors are conditionally independent given the true label — an assumption we stress-test directly for the sycophancy setting in Section 3.3, since it is the load-bearing assumption behind the mitigation we propose.

### 1.2 Gap and contribution

We are not aware of a controlled, mechanistic study that (a) directly manipulates the fraction of rater judgment driven by agreement-with-user-belief versus fact-checking, (b) measures the resulting sycophancy rate and accuracy of a policy trained by iterated preference optimization on that feedback, (c) tests whether aggregating multiple independent raters mitigates sycophancy amplification specifically, and (d) tests the boundary condition under which that mitigation breaks — correlated rater error. Because real raters cannot be dialed to a target bias level, we build a minimal simulation in which every socio-technical quantity of interest is a controllable parameter, isolating a causal mechanism at small scale so qualitative predictions can be checked cheaply before motivating expensive real-model experiments.

## 2. Method

### 2.1 Task and data-generating process

We simulate a binary classification task standing in for "is this claim true?". Features `x ∈ R^10` are drawn i.i.d. `N(0, I)`. A fixed unit-norm ground-truth weight vector `w*` (redrawn per seed) defines the true label via `y ~ Bernoulli(sigmoid(w*·x))`. A simulated user holds a stated belief `b` about the label, correct with probability `q = 0.6`, otherwise flipped.

### 2.2 Policy and training loop (rejection-sampling fine-tuning)

The policy is logistic regression `π_w(x) = sigmoid(w·x)`, initialized at `w = 0`. Training runs 60 rounds; each round draws 256 examples and samples `K = 4` candidate labels per example from the current policy, `c_k ~ Bernoulli(π_w(x))` — mirroring best-of-n rejection-sampling fine-tuning.

### 2.3 Biased rater feedback model

Each candidate `c_k` receives a rater score `score(c_k) = α·1[c_k = b] + (1-α)·1[c_k = y] + ε`, `ε ~ N(0, 0.05²)`. `α ∈ [0,1]` is the core independent variable: the fraction of the rater's judgment driven by agreement with the user's belief `b` versus ground truth `y`. The highest-scoring candidate becomes the fine-tuning target. When aggregating over `n_raters > 1` independent raters, each draws its own belief `b_r` independently (correct w.p. `q`) and scores sum before selecting the top candidate.

**Correlated-rater-error extension.** To test the assumption underlying aggregation, we add a parameter `ρ ∈ [0,1]`: with probability `ρ`, a rater's belief is not an independent noisy draw but is instead a fixed, shared "misinformation" claim `1-y` (the same wrong claim every correlated rater would have seen), and only with probability `1-ρ` does the rater draw an independent belief as before. `ρ=0` recovers the independent case; `ρ=1` means every rater shares the identical wrong claim whenever they are wrong.

### 2.4 Evaluation

On a held-out set of 2000 fresh examples (independently drawn beliefs), we report **accuracy** = P(prediction = y) and **sycophancy rate** = P(prediction = b | b ≠ y) — conditional on the user being wrong, how often the model follows the wrong belief anyway. This metric has a nonzero floor even for an unbiased classifier: with q=0.6 and irreducible task noise, an α=0-trained policy (~67% accurate) still matches a wrong belief roughly 33% of the time by chance, since among the ~1/3 of predictions that are themselves errors, some coincide with the (also incorrect) user belief. We use this α=0 value (0.326, see Section 3.1) as the empirical no-sycophancy reference point throughout; the α-driven *increase* above that floor, not the raw rate, is the sycophancy signal of interest.

### 2.5 Baselines, statistics, and experimental design

- **Oracle SFT baseline**: standard supervised fine-tuning on true labels y (no rater/candidate sampling), giving an accuracy ceiling.
- **α-sweep**: α ∈ {0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0}, single rater, 30 seeds each.
- **Rater-aggregation ablation**: fixed α = 0.75, n_raters ∈ {1, 3, 5, 9}, 30 seeds each.
- **Correlated-rater-error ablation** (new): fixed α = 0.75, ρ ∈ {0, 0.5, 1.0} × n_raters ∈ {1, 3, 9}, 30 seeds each.
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity** (new): K ∈ {2, 4, 8} × noise scale ∈ {0.05, 0.2}, at α ∈ {0.5, 0.75}, 15 seeds each, checking whether the α=0.5→0.75 threshold is an artifact of specific design constants.
- **Significance**: for the headline comparisons we report Welch's two-sample t-test (unequal variance) across the 30 per-seed final values.

All code is in `experiment.py` and `experiment_v2.py` (numpy + scipy); raw results in `results.json` and `results_v2.json`.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 α-sweep and significance

| α | Accuracy | Sycophancy rate |
|---|---|---|
| 0.00 | 0.671 ± 0.012 | 0.326 ± 0.016 |
| 0.50 | 0.666 ± 0.010 | 0.336 ± 0.015 |
| 0.75 | 0.638 ± 0.021 | 0.360 ± 0.025 |
| 1.00 | 0.639 ± 0.019 | 0.361 ± 0.026 |

Oracle SFT baseline: 0.674. The α=0→0.5 gap is small and only marginally significant (accuracy p=0.10, sycophancy p=0.016), but the α=0.5→0.75 transition is large and highly significant (accuracy t=6.2, p=4.3e-8; sycophancy p=5.8e-5), while α=0.75→1.0 is not distinguishable from noise (p=0.78). This confirms the paper's earlier qualitative claim of a "sharp transition" is statistically real, not an artifact of eyeballing means ± std, and pins the transition specifically to the 0.5–0.75 interval, consistent with the mechanistic account that the fact-checking weight (1−α) must exceed 0.5 to reliably outvote the noise term ε in the argmax over K=4 candidates.

### 3.2 Rater aggregation restores accuracy — and this is significant

| n_raters | Accuracy | Sycophancy rate |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.638 ± 0.021 | 0.360 ± 0.025 |
| 3 | 0.674 ± 0.008 | 0.325 ± 0.016 |
| 9 | 0.673 ± 0.009 | 0.323 ± 0.016 |

n_raters=1 vs. 3 and 1 vs. 9 are both highly significant (accuracy p=1.5e-10 and p=2.0e-10 respectively; sycophancy p=1.1e-7 and p=2.3e-8). Three independent raters, each individually as biased as the single-rater case (α=0.75), already restore accuracy to the oracle level and return sycophancy to the α=0 floor (0.326), consistent with idiosyncratic wrong-belief errors washing out under independent aggregation while the shared fact-checking signal survives.

### 3.3 Correlated rater errors break aggregation completely (new)

This is the central untested limitation flagged by all four reviewers of the previous draft. Fixing α=0.75 and varying the fraction ρ of raters that share a single common wrong belief instead of drawing independently:

| ρ | n_raters=1 | n_raters=3 | n_raters=9 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.0 (independent) | acc 0.655, syco 0.345 | acc 0.671, syco 0.329 | acc 0.675, syco 0.322 |
| 0.5 | acc 0.332, syco 0.665 | acc 0.361, syco 0.640 | acc 0.342, syco 0.656 |
| 1.0 (fully shared) | acc 0.327, syco 0.672 | acc 0.323, syco 0.676 | acc 0.327, syco 0.670 |

The result is qualitatively different from what "aggregation helps less" would predict: it is not a graceful degradation. Once even half of rater error is a shared, common misinformation source (ρ=0.5), accuracy collapses to near chance-on-the-flipped-label (~0.33-0.36) and sycophancy rises to ~0.64-0.67 — *worse* than the single-rater α=0.75 case (0.360) — and adding more raters (3, 9) provides essentially no benefit and is sometimes marginally worse. The mechanism is direct: averaging over raters cancels variance in *independent* errors but is powerless against a *systematic* bias shared by the whole panel, because from the optimizer's perspective a unanimous-albeit-wrong panel looks identical to a unanimous-and-correct one. This means the practical recommendation from Section 3.2 (panel-based preference labeling as a sycophancy mitigation) is only valid when annotator errors are close to independent — e.g., annotators queried without seeing each other's judgments and without a shared misinformation source — and is not merely ineffective but actively counterproductive to rely on if that assumption fails, since it can create false confidence that "we already aggregated raters" while leaving sycophancy at its worst level.

### 3.4 The threshold is robust to K and rater-noise scale (new)

Repeating the α=0.5 vs. α=0.75 comparison across K ∈ {2,4,8} candidates and rater-score noise scale ∈ {0.05, 0.2} (15 seeds/cell): accuracy at α=0.5 stays in a narrow 0.667–0.672 band across all six (K, noise) combinations, while α=0.75 stays in a separate, non-overlapping 0.626–0.646 band. The gap (roughly 3–4 accuracy points) persists at every setting tested, indicating the threshold location is not an artifact of the specific K=4, ε~N(0,0.05²) design used in Sections 3.1–3.2, though we note the *existence* of a threshold near (1−α)=0.5 is expected from the additive-score design itself (the fact-checking term needs a plurality of the score mass to reliably win the argmax) — the new evidence here is that the threshold's approximate location and the qualitative sharp-transition shape survive reasonable perturbation of the auxiliary constants, not that the mechanism is a surprise.

## 4. Discussion and Limitations

This is a toy model; the numeric magnitudes should not be read as predictions about real LLM sycophancy rates, which depend on far higher-dimensional representations, real human rater populations, and multi-turn dynamics not modeled here. The value is mechanistic: (1) a rejection-sampling/preference-optimization loop amplifies sycophancy once rater agreement-bias crosses roughly the point where it outweighs fact-checking signal, via a statistically confirmed, sharp rather than gradual transition that is robust to the auxiliary simulation constants; (2) rater aggregation is a genuinely effective, statistically significant mitigation — but only under an independence assumption that, when violated even partially (ρ=0.5), causes the mitigation to fail entirely rather than degrade gracefully. This last point sharpens rather than weakens the paper's practical recommendation: panel-based preference labeling is worth adopting for sycophancy mitigation specifically when there is reason to believe annotator errors are close to independent (e.g., annotators sourced and queried so they cannot see each other's judgments or share a common information feed about the claim being rated), and should not be trusted as a mitigation — and may need to be paired with independence-inducing sourcing practices or explicit correlated-error detection — when annotators plausibly share a misinformation source, a common training background, or see each other's ratings before submitting their own.

Remaining limitations: the policy is a single logistic-regression model on i.i.d. Gaussian features with a hand-designed linear rater-scoring rule, several steps removed from real RLHF (no learned reward model, no KL penalty, no realistic policy class); the correlated-error model itself is a simplified all-or-nothing shared claim rather than a graded correlation structure; and we have not validated any of the qualitative predictions against real LLM training. Extending the simulation with a learned reward model, graded (rather than binary) rater-error correlation, and multi-turn belief-restatement dynamics, and validating on a small open-weight model with a controllable synthetic-annotator harness, are natural next steps.

## 5. Conclusion

We presented a minimal, fully reproducible simulation isolating the causal link between human-rater agreement-bias and sycophancy amplification in preference-based fine-tuning. Rater aggregation is a statistically significant, effective mitigation when rater errors are independent (restoring accuracy and sycophancy rate to oracle levels even at α=0.75, p<1e-9), but we show for the first time in this simulation that the mitigation collapses entirely — not gradually — once rater errors are correlated even partially (ρ=0.5), with accuracy falling to near-chance and sycophancy nearly doubling regardless of panel size. This reframes the practical recommendation for real RLHF/DPO pipelines: panel-based preference labeling helps only insofar as the panel's errors are actually independent, which is an empirical claim about annotator sourcing that practitioners should verify rather than assume, and future work testing correlated annotator structure (e.g., annotators with shared training, shared information sources, or visible mutual influence) should be a priority before relying on aggregation alone as a sycophancy safeguard.
