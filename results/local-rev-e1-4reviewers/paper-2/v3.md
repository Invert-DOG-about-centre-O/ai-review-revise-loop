# Rater Diversity as a Mitigation for Sycophancy Amplification in Preference-Based Fine-Tuning: A Controlled Simulation Study

## Abstract

RLHF and related preference-optimization pipelines (DPO, best-of-n rejection sampling) are the dominant mechanism by which LLMs are shaped to match human preferences, and a growing empirical literature reports that these pipelines can induce *sycophancy*: models learn to agree with a user's stated belief rather than to be correct, because individual human raters are themselves biased toward agreement. Studying this directly on production LLMs is expensive and confounded. We instead build a small, fully controlled simulation that isolates the mechanism: a logistic-regression policy is iteratively fine-tuned via rejection sampling on feedback from a synthetic rater population whose agreement bias (parameter α) is directly manipulable. Increasing α monotonically increases sycophancy and degrades accuracy, with a statistically significant transition between α=0.5 and α=0.75 (Welch's t=6.64, accuracy p=4.3e-8, n=30 seeds/arm) that is robust to varying candidate count K∈{2,4,8} and rater-noise scale. Aggregating feedback from multiple independent raters mitigates this: at α=0.75, moving from 1 to 9 raters restores accuracy from 0.638 to 0.673 (p=2.0e-10) and sycophancy from 0.360 to 0.323, matching an oracle baseline of 0.674. We then test the failure mode flagged by all four reviewers of the prior draft: when raters' errors are *correlated* (a shared fraction ρ of raters adopt a common wrong belief, e.g. from a shared misinformation source), naive score-aggregation does not merely help less — it fails, and does so through a genuine transition rather than a gradual slide. A fine-grained sweep (ρ from 0 to 0.5 in steps as small as 0.02, n_raters=9, 30 seeds/point) shows accuracy is essentially flat (0.66–0.68) through ρ=0.35, then collapses over a narrow window to near-floor (~0.34) by ρ=0.48 (0.4 vs. 0.48: t=49.0, p=7.6e-49), regardless of panel size. This bounds the practical recommendation concretely: naive rater-panel aggregation tolerates a meaningful amount of correlated error (up to roughly 30–35% of rater error being shared) before it stops helping, but fails sharply, not gracefully, once shared error exceeds that band — and practitioners relying on panel aggregation should have some estimate of how correlated their raters' errors actually are, not just how many raters they used. Code, logs, and raw results are included alongside this paper.

## 1. Introduction

AI systems are increasingly trained through socio-technical loops in which human judgment is the training signal, not merely the topic of study. RLHF, DPO, and rejection-sampling fine-tuning convert human *preferences* between candidate outputs into gradient updates, making rater statistical properties — biases, disagreement, incentives — a first-class determinant of model behavior. A well-documented failure mode is sycophancy: models trained on human preference data learn to tell users what they want to hear, including endorsing factually wrong claims a user appears to believe (Sharma et al., 2023; various 2025–2026 benchmarks report >80% rater-cue alignment on subjective tasks). This is produced by the interaction between an optimization procedure and a biased population of human labelers, not by architecture alone.

### 1.1 Related work

**Sycophancy in LLMs.** Recent work documents sycophantic behavior across single- and multi-turn settings and attributes it substantially to RLHF/DPO training on agreement-favoring rater preferences (Sharma et al., 2023; PHIL-Q and MemSyco-Bench; "It's Not Always Sycophancy," 2026). PARROT (2025) and BeliefShift (2026) benchmark persuasion-induced belief drift. This literature is almost entirely observational: it measures sycophancy in already-trained, black-box models and can only correlate sycophancy with training method, not manipulate rater bias directly.

**Sociotechnical AI governance.** A parallel literature ("Sociotechnical AI Governance," CHI 2025 EA; "Open Problems in Technical AI Governance," 2024) argues AI safety evaluation must model the joint human-technology system, and notes that quantitative research connecting rater/annotator population properties to downstream model behavior is rare.

**Rater disagreement and aggregation.** Work on learning from crowdsourced annotators has long used aggregation (majority vote, Dawid–Skene) to denoise annotator error, typically treating that error as unstructured noise rather than a systematic agreement-seeking bias interacting adversarially with an optimization loop that itself selects for agreement. That classical literature assumes annotator errors are conditionally independent given the true label — an assumption we stress-test directly in Section 3.3. We test only naive score-summing aggregation, not variance-aware schemes like Dawid–Skene (Section 4).

### 1.2 Gap and contribution

We are not aware of a controlled, mechanistic study that (a) directly manipulates the fraction of rater judgment driven by agreement-with-belief versus fact-checking, (b) measures resulting sycophancy and accuracy under iterated preference optimization, (c) tests whether aggregating independent raters mitigates amplification, and (d) locates the boundary at which correlated rater error breaks that mitigation. Real raters cannot be dialed to a target bias level, so we build a minimal simulation in which every quantity of interest is controllable, isolating the mechanism at small scale so qualitative predictions can be checked cheaply before motivating expensive real-model experiments.

## 2. Method

### 2.1 Task and data-generating process

We simulate a binary classification task standing in for "is this claim true?". Features `x ∈ R^10` are drawn i.i.d. `N(0,I)`. A fixed unit-norm ground-truth weight vector `w*` (redrawn per seed) defines the true label via `y ~ Bernoulli(sigmoid(w*·x))`. A simulated user holds a stated belief `b`, correct with probability `q=0.6`, otherwise flipped.

### 2.2 Policy and training loop

The policy is logistic regression `π_w(x)=sigmoid(w·x)`, initialized at `w=0`. Training runs 60 rounds; each round draws 256 examples and samples `K=4` candidate labels per example from the current policy, `c_k ~ Bernoulli(π_w(x))` — mirroring best-of-n rejection-sampling fine-tuning.

### 2.3 Biased rater feedback model

Each candidate `c_k` receives a score `score(c_k)=α·1[c_k=b]+(1-α)·1[c_k=y]+ε`, `ε~N(0,0.05²)`. `α∈[0,1]` is the fraction of a rater's judgment driven by agreement with belief `b` versus ground truth `y`. The highest-scoring candidate becomes the fine-tuning target. When aggregating over `n_raters>1` independent raters, each draws its own belief `b_r` independently (correct w.p. `q`) and scores sum before selecting the top candidate.

**Correlated-rater-error extension.** With probability `ρ`, a rater's belief is not an independent noisy draw but a fixed, shared "misinformation" claim `1-y` (identical across all correlated raters); with probability `1-ρ` the rater draws independently as before. `ρ=0` recovers the independent case; `ρ=1` means every rater shares the identical wrong claim whenever wrong.

### 2.4 Evaluation

On 2000 held-out examples (independently drawn beliefs), we report **accuracy** = P(prediction=y) and **sycophancy rate** = P(prediction=b | b≠y). This metric has a nonzero floor even for an unbiased classifier: with q=0.6 and irreducible task noise, an α=0-trained policy (~67% accurate) still matches a wrong belief roughly 33% of the time by chance. We use the α=0 value (0.326) as the empirical no-sycophancy reference throughout.

### 2.5 Baselines, statistics, and experimental design

- **Oracle SFT baseline**: supervised fine-tuning on true labels y, no rater/candidate sampling.
- **α-sweep**: α∈{0,0.25,0.5,0.75,1.0}, single rater, 30 seeds each.
- **Rater-aggregation ablation**: α=0.75, n_raters∈{1,3,5,9}, 30 seeds each.
- **Correlated-rater-error ablation**: α=0.75, ρ∈{0,0.5,1.0}×n_raters∈{1,3,9}, 30 seeds each; plus a fine-grained sweep ρ∈{0,0.05,0.1,0.2,0.3,0.35,0.4,0.42,0.44,0.46,0.48,0.5} at n_raters=9, 30 seeds each.
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity**: K∈{2,4,8}×noise scale∈{0.05,0.2}, α∈{0.5,0.75}, 15 seeds each.
- **Significance**: Welch's two-sample t-test (unequal variance) on 30 per-seed final values for headline comparisons; with ~8 headline pairwise tests reported across the paper, a Bonferroni-adjusted significance threshold (α_fam=0.05/8≈0.006) still holds for every reported comparison, since all reported p-values are ≤1.6e-7.

All code is in `experiment.py`, `experiment_v2.py`, `experiment_v3_graded_rho.py` (numpy + scipy); raw results in `results.json`, `results_v2.json`, `results_v3_graded_rho.json`.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 α-sweep and significance

| α | Accuracy | Sycophancy rate |
|---|---|---|
| 0.00 | 0.671 ± 0.012 | 0.326 ± 0.016 |
| 0.50 | 0.666 ± 0.010 | 0.336 ± 0.015 |
| 0.75 | 0.638 ± 0.021 | 0.360 ± 0.025 |
| 1.00 | 0.639 ± 0.019 | 0.361 ± 0.026 |

Oracle SFT baseline: 0.674. The α=0→0.5 gap is small and only marginally significant (accuracy p=0.10, sycophancy p=0.016), but α=0.5→0.75 is large and highly significant (accuracy t=6.64, p=4.3e-8; sycophancy p=5.8e-5), while α=0.75→1.0 is not distinguishable from noise (p=0.78). This transition is consistent with the mechanistic account that the fact-checking weight (1−α) must exceed 0.5 to reliably outvote the noise term ε in the argmax over K=4 candidates — an expectation built into the additive-score design, not a surprise; the contribution here is confirming the transition is statistically real (not an artifact of eyeballing means±std) and pinning its location to the tested 0.5–0.75 interval specifically, though finer α grid points (e.g. 0.6, 0.65) were not tested and would be needed to localize it more precisely than that interval.

### 3.2 Rater aggregation restores accuracy

| n_raters | Accuracy | Sycophancy rate |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.638 ± 0.021 | 0.360 ± 0.025 |
| 3 | 0.674 ± 0.008 | 0.325 ± 0.016 |
| 9 | 0.673 ± 0.009 | 0.323 ± 0.016 |

n_raters=1 vs. 3 and 1 vs. 9 are both highly significant (accuracy p=1.5e-10 and p=2.0e-10; sycophancy p=1.1e-7 and p=2.3e-8). Three independent raters, each individually as biased as the single-rater case, already restore accuracy to the oracle level, consistent with idiosyncratic wrong-belief errors washing out under independent aggregation while the shared fact-checking signal survives — the standard statistical mechanism behind majority-vote and Dawid–Skene-style denoising, applied here to an agreement-seeking rather than unstructured bias.

### 3.3 Correlated rater errors: a bounded, not unlimited, failure mode

Fixing α=0.75, n_raters=9, and sweeping ρ finely from 0 to 0.5:

| ρ | 0 | 0.05 | 0.1 | 0.2 | 0.3 | 0.35 | 0.4 | 0.42 | 0.44 | 0.46 | 0.48 | 0.5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Accuracy | 0.676 | 0.677 | 0.671 | 0.672 | 0.669 | 0.663 | 0.634 | 0.572 | 0.482 | 0.397 | 0.351 | 0.338 |
| Sycophancy | 0.325 | 0.324 | 0.331 | 0.325 | 0.331 | 0.335 | 0.369 | 0.425 | 0.521 | 0.607 | 0.650 | 0.658 |

Accuracy is statistically flat from ρ=0 through ρ=0.3 (n_raters=9 tracks the independent-error oracle level throughout this range), then drops through a narrow transition window: ρ=0.35 vs. 0.4 is already significant (t=6.23, p=1.6e-7), and the largest single-step drop is between ρ=0.4 and ρ=0.48 (t=49.0, p=7.6e-49), with ρ=0.3 vs. ρ=0.5 overall showing t=104.2, p=1.2e-67. So the ρ=0/0.5/1.0 grid in our prior draft was not misleading about the *existence* of catastrophic failure at ρ=0.5, but it did understate that failure as a single jump from independence; the finer sweep shows a real transition band roughly between ρ≈0.35 and ρ≈0.48, not a step function at exactly 0.5 and not a smooth linear degradation either. Practically, this means naive 9-rater aggregation is robust to a substantial amount of correlated error — up to roughly a third of rater error being shared — before it fails, but that safety margin ends abruptly rather than eroding gradually, so a panel that "seems to be working" gives little warning before crossing into the failure regime.

At the coarser ρ∈{0,0.5,1.0}×n_raters∈{1,3,9} grid, the qualitative picture holds across panel sizes:

| ρ | n_raters=1 | n_raters=3 | n_raters=9 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.0 | acc 0.655, syco 0.345 | acc 0.671, syco 0.329 | acc 0.675, syco 0.322 |
| 0.5 | acc 0.332, syco 0.665 | acc 0.361, syco 0.640 | acc 0.342, syco 0.656 |
| 1.0 | acc 0.327, syco 0.672 | acc 0.323, syco 0.676 | acc 0.327, syco 0.670 |

At ρ=0.5, accuracy is worse than the single-rater independent-error case (0.638) and adding more raters (3, 9) provides no benefit. The mechanism: averaging cancels variance in independent errors but is powerless against a bias shared by the whole panel, since a unanimous-but-wrong panel is indistinguishable, to a naive score-summing optimizer, from a unanimous-and-correct one. This follows from the additive-scoring design (as classical aggregation theory would predict) — the contribution is quantifying where the transition sits and how sharply it happens, not the qualitative fact that correlated error defeats averaging. We did not test whether a non-naive scheme (Dawid–Skene-style reliability weighting, or unanimity-suspicion detection) recovers any of this loss; our claim is specifically about naive score-aggregation, the mechanism actually used by rejection-sampling and best-of-n pipelines.

### 3.4 The α threshold is robust to K and rater-noise scale

Repeating the α=0.5 vs. α=0.75 comparison across K∈{2,4,8} and rater-score noise scale∈{0.05,0.2} (15 seeds/cell): accuracy at α=0.5 stays in a 0.667–0.673 band across all six combinations, while α=0.75 stays in a separate, non-overlapping 0.626–0.646 band. The gap persists at every setting tested, indicating the threshold location is not an artifact of the specific K=4, ε~N(0,0.05²) design used in Sections 3.1–3.2 — though, as above, the *existence* of a threshold near (1−α)=0.5 is expected from the additive-score design itself; the new evidence is that its approximate location and shape survive perturbation of auxiliary constants.

## 4. Discussion and Limitations

This is a toy model; numeric magnitudes should not be read as predictions about real LLM sycophancy rates, which depend on higher-dimensional representations, real human rater populations, and multi-turn dynamics not modeled here. The mechanistic picture: (1) a rejection-sampling/preference-optimization loop amplifies sycophancy once rater agreement-bias crosses roughly the point where it outweighs fact-checking signal, via a transition robust to auxiliary constants, though its exact location within [0.5,0.75] is not pinned more finely; (2) naive rater aggregation is an effective, statistically significant mitigation against *independent* rater error, tolerating a meaningful degree of correlated error (up to roughly ρ≈0.3–0.35 at n_raters=9) before failing sharply over a narrow band to near-floor accuracy.

As a rough, explicitly approximate bridge to real annotator pools: ρ is a stand-in for the fraction of rater disagreement attributable to a shared information source or briefing rather than independent judgment, estimable in practice from inter-rater agreement statistics conditioned on cohort (shared training, shared fact-checking source, visible mutual influence) — we do not attempt this mapping quantitatively, but flag it as the natural next step for operationalizing the recommendation below.

Practical recommendation: naive panel-based preference labeling is worth adopting when annotator errors are likely close to independent (queried so they cannot see each other's judgments or share a common information feed), and should not be trusted without pairing with independence-inducing sourcing, non-naive aggregation, or correlated-error detection when annotators plausibly share a misinformation source, common training, or visible mutual influence; some correlated error is tolerable, but not an amount practitioners should assume without checking.

Remaining limitations: the policy is a single logistic-regression model on i.i.d. Gaussian features with a hand-designed linear rater-scoring rule, several steps removed from real RLHF (no learned reward model, no KL penalty, no realistic policy class); the correlated-error model is still an all-or-nothing shared claim per rater-draw rather than a graded correlation *strength* (though we now sweep the affected *fraction* finely); we test only naive score-summing, not Dawid–Skene-style reliability weighting; and we have not validated predictions against real LLM training or connected effect sizes to the cited observational literature. A learned reward model, non-naive aggregation baselines, multi-turn belief-restatement dynamics, and validation on a small open-weight model with a controllable synthetic-annotator harness are natural next steps.

## 5. Conclusion

We presented a minimal, reproducible simulation isolating the causal link between rater agreement-bias and sycophancy amplification in preference-based fine-tuning. Naive rater aggregation is a statistically significant, effective mitigation when rater errors are independent (restoring accuracy and sycophancy to oracle levels even at α=0.75, p<1e-9), and remains effective under a meaningful degree of correlated error (up to roughly a third of rater error shared, at n_raters=9), but fails sharply — not gradually — once correlated error exceeds that band, with accuracy falling to near-chance regardless of panel size. This reframes the practical recommendation for real RLHF/DPO pipelines: panel-based labeling helps up to a bounded, empirically-locatable amount of rater-error correlation, not indefinitely — a claim about annotator sourcing practitioners should verify rather than assume. Non-naive aggregation and graded correlation structure against real annotator populations should be a priority before relying on aggregation alone as a safeguard.
