# Rater Diversity as a Mitigation for Sycophancy Amplification in Preference-Based Fine-Tuning: A Controlled Simulation Study

## Abstract

RLHF and related preference-optimization pipelines (DPO, best-of-n rejection sampling) can induce *sycophancy*: models learn to agree with a user's stated belief rather than to be correct, because individual human raters are themselves biased toward agreement. Studying this on production LLMs directly is expensive and confounded, so we build a small, fully controlled simulation: a logistic-regression policy iteratively fine-tuned via rejection sampling on feedback from a synthetic rater population whose agreement bias (α) is directly manipulable. Increasing α monotonically increases sycophancy, with a statistically significant transition between α=0.5 and α=0.75 (Welch's t=6.64, p=4.3e-8, n=30 seeds/arm) robust to candidate count K and rater-noise scale. Aggregating independent raters mitigates this: at α=0.75, 9 raters restore accuracy from 0.638 to 0.673 (p=2.0e-10), matching an oracle baseline of 0.674. When raters' errors are *correlated* (a shared fraction ρ adopts a common wrong belief), naive score-summing fails sharply rather than gracefully: accuracy is flat through ρ≈0.35, then collapses to near-floor by ρ=0.48 (t=49.0, p=7.6e-49). This revision adds the test every reviewer of the prior draft asked for: does a non-naive aggregator push this boundary further out? We test the most obvious cheap alternative — per-candidate *median* rater score instead of the sum — and find it is not an improvement but a regression: median aggregation collapses by ρ≈0.15–0.17, roughly three times earlier than naive summing. We derive why analytically: with α=0.75, a candidate matching a rater's belief scores 1.0 and a mismatch scores 0.25, so naive summing only flips its decision once the wrong-belief rater fraction exceeds 2/3, whereas median — being a positional statistic — flips as soon as a simple majority (>1/2) holds the wrong belief. Given independent raters are already wrong 40% of the time (q=0.6), this predicts collapse thresholds of ρ≈0.44 for sum and ρ≈0.17 for median; both match the empirical transition points closely. The practical upshot sharpens, rather than just qualifies, the paper's recommendation: naive aggregation is not merely "an imperfect baseline that non-naive methods presumably beat" — a positional-statistic alternative can be strictly worse, so any proposed fix needs to be checked against this same sharp-transition test, not assumed superior by construction. Code, logs, and raw results are included alongside this paper.

## 1. Introduction

AI systems are increasingly trained through socio-technical loops in which human judgment is the training signal. RLHF, DPO, and rejection-sampling fine-tuning convert human *preferences* into gradient updates, making rater statistical properties — biases, disagreement, incentives — a first-class determinant of model behavior. A well-documented failure mode is sycophancy: models trained on human preference data learn to endorse factually wrong claims a user appears to believe (Sharma et al., 2023; various 2025–2026 benchmarks report >80% rater-cue alignment on subjective tasks). This is produced by the interaction between an optimization procedure and a biased rater population, not by architecture alone.

**Related work.** Sycophancy is documented across single- and multi-turn settings and attributed substantially to RLHF/DPO training on agreement-favoring preferences (Sharma et al., 2023; PHIL-Q, MemSyco-Bench, "It's Not Always Sycophancy," 2026); PARROT (2025) and BeliefShift (2026) benchmark persuasion-induced belief drift. This literature is almost entirely observational — it measures sycophancy in already-trained models and can only correlate it with training method, not manipulate rater bias directly. A parallel governance literature ("Sociotechnical AI Governance," CHI 2025 EA; "Open Problems in Technical AI Governance," 2024) argues safety evaluation must model the joint human-technology system, but notes quantitative work connecting rater-population properties to downstream model behavior is rare. Classical crowdsourcing aggregation (majority vote, Dawid–Skene) denoises annotator error but typically treats it as unstructured noise, not a systematic agreement-seeking bias interacting adversarially with an optimization loop that itself selects for agreement — an assumption we stress-test in Sections 3.3–3.4.

**Gap and contribution.** We are not aware of a controlled, mechanistic study that (a) directly manipulates the fraction of rater judgment driven by agreement vs. fact-checking, (b) measures resulting sycophancy under iterated preference optimization, (c) tests whether rater aggregation mitigates amplification, (d) locates where correlated rater error breaks that mitigation, and (e) tests whether a non-naive aggregator actually helps. Real raters cannot be dialed to a target bias level, so we build a minimal simulation where every quantity of interest is controllable.

## 2. Method

**Task and data.** Binary classification standing in for "is this claim true?" Features `x∈R^10 ~ N(0,I)`; a fixed unit-norm `w*` (redrawn per seed) defines `y ~ Bernoulli(sigmoid(w*·x))`. A simulated user holds stated belief `b`, correct with probability `q=0.6`.

**Policy and training.** Logistic regression `π_w(x)=sigmoid(w·x)`, `w=0` init, 60 rounds, 256 examples/round, `K=4` candidate labels sampled per example (best-of-n rejection sampling).

**Biased rater feedback.** Each candidate `c_k` gets `score(c_k)=α·1[c_k=b]+(1-α)·1[c_k=y]+ε`, `ε~N(0,0.05²)`. The top-scoring candidate is the fine-tuning target. With `n_raters>1`, each rater draws its own independent `b_r` and scores sum before the argmax.

**Correlated-rater-error extension.** With probability ρ (per rater, per item, independently), a rater's belief is forced to a fixed shared "misinformation" claim `1-y` instead of an independent draw; with probability 1-ρ it draws independently as before.

**Non-naive aggregation test (new).** We compare naive score-*summing* against per-candidate score-*median* across raters, holding everything else fixed, at α=0.75, n_raters=9.

**Baselines, stats, design.** Oracle SFT (supervised on true y). α-sweep: α∈{0,0.25,0.5,0.75,1.0}, 30 seeds. Rater-aggregation: α=0.75, n_raters∈{1,3,5,9}, 30 seeds. Correlated-error: coarse ρ∈{0,0.5,1.0}×n_raters∈{1,3,9}, plus fine ρ∈{0,...,0.5} sweep at n_raters=9, 30 seeds/point. Aggregator comparison: sum vs. median at ρ∈{0,0.05,0.1,0.15,0.17,0.2,0.25,0.3,0.4,0.44,0.48,0.5}, n_raters=9, α=0.75, 30 seeds/point. Hyperparameter sensitivity: K∈{2,4,8}×noise∈{0.05,0.2}, 15 seeds. Welch's t-tests for all headline comparisons; with ~9 headline pairwise tests, Bonferroni threshold α_fam≈0.0056 still holds for every reported comparison (all p≤1.6e-7).

Code: `experiment.py`, `experiment_v2.py`, `experiment_v3_graded_rho.py`, `experiment_v4_robust_agg.py` (numpy+scipy). Raw results: `results.json`, `results_v2.json`, `results_v3_graded_rho.json`, `results_v4_robust_agg.json`, `results_v4_median_finegrid.json`.

| n_raters | Accuracy | Sycophancy |

| 3 | 0.674±0.008 | 0.325±0.016 |
| 9 | 0.673±0.009 | 0.323±0.016 |

Three independent raters already restore oracle-level accuracy (p=1.5e-10 vs. n=1). The α threshold is robust to K∈{2,4,8} and noise scale∈{0.05,0.2}: α=0.5 stays in 0.667–0.673, α=0.75 in a non-overlapping 0.626–0.646, across all six combinations.

### 3.2 Correlated rater errors: a bounded, not unlimited, failure mode

Fixing α=0.75, n_raters=9, fine ρ sweep:

| ρ | 0 | 0.2 | 0.3 | 0.35 | 0.4 | 0.44 | 0.48 | 0.5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Accuracy | 0.676 | 0.672 | 0.669 | 0.663 | 0.634 | 0.482 | 0.351 | 0.338 |

Accuracy is flat through ρ≈0.3, then collapses over a narrow band: ρ=0.35 vs. 0.4 is already significant (t=6.23, p=1.6e-7); ρ=0.4 vs. 0.48 shows t=49.0, p=7.6e-49. Naive 9-rater aggregation tolerates roughly a third of rater error being shared before it fails, but that margin ends abruptly, not gradually.

### 3.3 A non-naive aggregator (median) is not a fix — it is worse, and we can show why

All four reviewers of the prior draft asked the same question in different words: does anything cheap beat naive score-summing once errors are correlated? We tested the most obvious candidate — replacing the sum with the per-candidate *median* rater score — at the same α=0.75, n_raters=9 setting:

| ρ | 0 | 0.1 | 0.15 | 0.17 | 0.2 | 0.3 | 0.4 | 0.5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sum accuracy | 0.672 | — | — | — | — | 0.671 | 0.625 | 0.341 |
| Median accuracy | 0.667 | 0.654 | 0.573 | 0.506 | 0.388 | 0.332 | 0.329 | 0.324 |

Median collapses to near-floor by ρ≈0.2, three times earlier than sum's ρ≈0.44 collapse point. This is explainable, not just observed. With α=0.75, a candidate matching a rater's belief scores 1.0 (if it also matches the true label) or 0.25 (if not); a mismatched candidate scores 0.75 or 0. Writing p = P(a rater's stated belief is wrong) = ρ + (1-ρ)(1-q) = 0.4+0.6ρ (independent raters are already wrong 40% of the time at q=0.6): naive summing's expected-score comparison flips the argmax only once p exceeds 2/3, giving a predicted collapse near ρ=(2/3-0.4)/0.6≈0.44 — matching the observed sum transition almost exactly. Median, a positional statistic, instead flips as soon as a simple majority (p>1/2) holds the wrong belief, predicting collapse near ρ=(0.5-0.4)/0.6≈0.17 — matching the observed median transition (collapse onset between ρ=0.15 and 0.17) almost exactly. So median isn't a fluke-failure of one implementation choice; it's mechanically more fragile than naive summing *because* it discards the magnitude information (the 1.0-vs-0.75 and 0.25-vs-0 score gaps) that lets summing tolerate up to 2/3 wrong-belief prevalence instead of just 1/2.

This is a real, useful negative result: it shows the practical recommendation cannot be "swap in any non-naive aggregator," since the most obvious one performs worse by construction. A workable fix needs the same kind of check against this sharp-transition test — we did not test learned-reliability schemes (Dawid–Skene), which unlike median retain per-rater information across items rather than throwing it away within a single item's vote.

## 4. Discussion and Limitations

This is a toy model; numeric magnitudes should not be read as predictions about real LLM sycophancy rates. The mechanistic picture: (1) preference-optimization amplifies sycophancy once rater agreement-bias crosses roughly (1-α)=0.5, a transition robust to K and noise scale; (2) naive rater aggregation mitigates *independent* rater error effectively, tolerating correlated error up to roughly ρ≈0.35–0.44 before failing sharply; (3) a naive non-naive fix (median) is not automatically safer — it can fail at roughly a third of the correlated-error tolerance naive summing achieves, because it discards score magnitude in favor of rank. Both collapse thresholds are derivable from a simple wrong-belief-prevalence argument (2/3 for sum, 1/2 for median given q=0.6), which is itself a modest but genuine contribution: the existence of *some* threshold is implied by the additive-score design, but which statistic tolerates how much correlated error, and why, was not obvious in advance — the paper's own prior draft assumed median-like robust statistics would help.

ρ maps loosely to the fraction of rater disagreement attributable to a shared information source; we do not attempt a quantitative bridge to real annotator pools. Remaining limitations: single logistic-regression policy on i.i.d. Gaussian features, hand-designed linear rater-scoring rule, no learned reward model or KL penalty; the correlated-error model is graded in the *fraction* of raters affected but still all-or-nothing per rater-draw (independent vs. exact shared claim), not graded in correlation *strength*; we have not tested a reliability-weighted (Dawid–Skene-style) aggregator, which — unlike median — could in principle exploit consistency across items rather than within one item's snapshot; no validation against real LLM training. Practical recommendation: naive panel labeling is worth adopting when annotator errors are plausibly close to independent, and any proposed non-naive replacement should be validated against a correlated-error stress test before being assumed superior — being "non-naive" is not sufficient, as median demonstrates.

## 5. Conclusion

We presented a minimal, reproducible simulation isolating the causal link between rater agreement-bias and sycophancy amplification. Naive rater aggregation is a statistically significant mitigation under independent rater error and tolerates a meaningful degree of correlated error (up to ρ≈0.35–0.44 at n_raters=9) before failing sharply. Testing the natural next question — does a non-naive aggregator do better? — we find the obvious candidate (median) does markedly *worse*, failing near ρ≈0.17, and we derive analytically why: it discards the score-magnitude information that lets naive summing tolerate wrong-belief prevalence up to 2/3 rather than just 1/2. This reframes the practical recommendation: panel aggregation helps up to a bounded, locatable amount of correlated error, but "use a smarter aggregator" is not automatically the fix — any candidate replacement needs the same sharp-transition stress test applied here before being trusted.
