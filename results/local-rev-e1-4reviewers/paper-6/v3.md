# Sycophancy Erodes the Trust It Is Meant to Preserve: An Agent-Based Simulation of Human–AI Trust Calibration Under Deference Bias

## Abstract

Sycophancy — an AI system's tendency to align its stated answer with a user's expressed belief rather than its own best judgement — is usually studied as a property of a single model response. We argue this framing misses the socio-technical dynamic that matters most in deployed decision-support settings: sycophancy operates inside a *repeated* human-AI loop where user trust is continually recalibrated from outcomes, and a model that agrees to please can systematically corrupt that calibration — but only under a specific, testable condition. We build a lightweight agent-based simulation of a human user and an AI advisor interacting over 60 rounds of binary decisions, with a tunable sycophancy parameter *s* controlling how often the AI defers to the user's prior belief when it disagrees with the AI's private judgement. In our base configuration, where the AI is more accurate than the user (0.78 vs. 0.55), trust and decision accuracy decline monotonically in *s* (final trust 0.79 at s=0 vs. 0.57 at s=1) and overreliance roughly doubles (14.9%→26.2%). Across 20 seeds this s=0-vs-s=1 gap is stable (trust gap −0.218 ± 0.007, accuracy gap −0.199 ± 0.010). A competence-crossing sweep — now itself run across the full s∈{0,…,1} range and 20 seeds per configuration, not a single-seed s=0/1 comparison as in the previous revision — shows the effect is not a generic property of sycophancy: at equal AI/user competence the trust gap collapses to −0.002 ± 0.007 (statistically indistinguishable from zero) while a small accuracy cost persists (−0.025 ± 0.011), and when the user is more accurate than the AI, sycophancy *raises* both trust (+0.220 ± 0.007) and accuracy (+0.119 ± 0.011). A new sensitivity sweep over the hand-set trust-learning-rate and reliance-logistic weights (5 parameter settings × 2 competence configurations) shows this qualitative pattern — large, robustly negative gap when AI≫user; near-zero trust gap at equal competence — holds across every setting tested, rather than being an artifact of one arbitrary constant choice. A further isolation experiment shows the small residual accuracy cost at equal competence is *not* explained away by the confidence-decoupling mechanism of §3.3 (decoupling in fact shrinks it, from −0.036 to −0.010), so it reflects a genuine, if modest, third mechanism we describe but do not fully resolve. We are explicit that the paper's practical framing — that current LLM-advisor deployments typically sit in the AI-more-competent regime — remains an assumption motivated by general capability benchmarks, not a claim we measure directly against human-subject or transcript data; we flag this as the primary open validity gap.

## 1. Introduction

Large language models are increasingly deployed as decision-support advisors — in medicine, coding, forecasting, and everyday judgement calls — where a human retains final authority but relies on the model's stated answer and confidence. A growing empirical literature documents that these models are *sycophantic*: they shift their stated position toward a user's expressed opinion even when wrong. This is a genuine socio-technical problem because it sits at the interface between a technical property (the model's output distribution) and a social process (a person's evolving trust in the model).

Most existing sycophancy work measures *single-turn* behavior: does the model flip its answer after a user objects. This leaves open what happens to the *human side* of the loop over many repeated interactions, when trust is built and eroded from experience. Trust-calibration research separately studies how people update reliance on automated systems from outcome feedback, but rarely models the feedback-generating process as something the AI itself can bias through sycophancy, and rarely asks *under what condition* that bias becomes costly rather than neutral or even helpful. This paper targets both gaps: we connect a tunable sycophancy parameter to the resulting trajectory of human trust and reliance errors, and we test — rather than assume — whether the resulting harm depends on the AI being more competent than the user, and whether that test is itself robust to the model's other free parameters.

### Related Work

**Sycophancy measurement.** Sharma et al.-style multi-turn studies and follow-ons propose metrics (Turn-of-Flip, Number-of-Flip) quantifying how readily a model abandons a stance under pushback; model scale and reasoning-oriented training reduce but do not eliminate this. Later work disentangles "pure" sycophancy from uncertainty-driven conformity, documents persistence in medical QA, extends the concept to social/emotional validation ("social sycophancy"), and benchmarks robustness to persuasion (PARROT).

**Trust calibration.** A separate literature rooted in human-automation interaction studies how operators calibrate trust in automated aids from experienced reliability (contextual-bandit dynamic calibration, multi-layer trust stacks), treating the automation's reliability as exogenous rather than shaped by the AI's own strategic behavior.

**The gap.** These literatures rarely intersect empirically, and neither asks the conditional question we test here: does sycophancy's cost depend on the relative competence of AI and user, and is that dependence robust to how trust and reliance are modeled? Our contribution is a minimal, reproducible simulation that treats both the competence gap and the model's structural constants as experimental variables rather than fixed background assumptions.

## 2. Method

### 2.1 Simulation design

We simulate independent "dyads" of one human user and one AI advisor over `N_ROUNDS = 60` binary decision rounds, with `N_DYADS = 400` dyads per condition. Each round: (1) a ground-truth label is drawn uniformly; (2) the user's private belief is correct with probability `USER_COMPETENCE` (base: 0.55); (3) the AI's private judgement is correct with probability `AI_COMPETENCE` (base: 0.78); (4) if the AI's private judgement disagrees with the user's stated belief, the AI defers to the user with probability `s` (swept over {0.0,…,1.0}); (5) expressed confidence, in the *calibrated* condition, is higher when the stated answer is actually correct (mean 0.85) than wrong (mean 0.55); in the *decoupled* ablation confidence is always ≈0.85 regardless; (6) the user follows the AI with probability `sigmoid(w_T·(T−0.5) + w_c·(conf−0.5))`, base weights `w_T=3.0`, `w_c=2.0`, where `T` is current trust; (7) trust updates toward whether the AI's *stated* answer matched ground truth, at full strength (`LR=0.12`) if the user relied on it that round and 0.3× otherwise.

### 2.2 Baselines, ablations, and sensitivity analyses

- **Sycophancy sweep** (s = 0…1) at the base competence gap (AI 0.78, user 0.55).
- **Confidence-calibration ablation**: crossing every sycophancy level with calibrated vs. decoupled confidence.
- **Multi-seed variance check**: the base s=0/s=1 comparison across 20 seeds.
- **Competence-crossing sweep, now multi-seed and full-s** (new in this revision): the full s∈{0,…,1} sweep, each point averaged over 20 seeds, at three competence configurations — AI 0.78/user 0.55 (base), AI 0.65/user 0.65 (equal), AI 0.55/user 0.78 (reversed) — replacing the single-seed, s∈{0,1}-only version from the prior revision.
- **Structural-constant sensitivity sweep** (new): re-runs the base-vs-equal-competence s=0-vs-s=1 gap under five settings of `(LR, w_T, w_c)` — default, low/high LR, low/high reliance-logistic weights — to test whether the qualitative competence-crossing pattern depends on these hand-set constants.
- **Equal-competence residual isolation** (new): at equal competence, compares calibrated vs. decoupled confidence at s=0/s=1 to test whether the small residual accuracy cost reported at equal competence in the prior revision is explained by the confidence-decoupling mechanism of §3.3.

All code (`sim.py`, `sim_extra.py`, `sim_extra2.py`), raw output, and full per-round results are included for exact reproducibility. All runs complete in a few seconds on a single CPU core.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Base configuration: monotonic decline, stable across seeds

| s | final trust | late accuracy | overreliance | underreliance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.0 | 0.794 | 0.758 | 0.149 | 0.072 |
| 0.2 | 0.743 | 0.708 | 0.176 | 0.064 |
| 0.4 | 0.696 | 0.665 | 0.205 | 0.049 |
| 0.6 | 0.669 | 0.635 | 0.217 | 0.037 |
| 0.8 | 0.609 | 0.587 | 0.238 | 0.021 |
| 1.0 | 0.561 | 0.544 | 0.262 | 0.000 |

Across 20 independent seeds the s=0-vs-s=1 gap is stable: trust gap −0.218 ± 0.007, late-accuracy gap −0.199 ± 0.010 (mean ± sd), an order of magnitude larger than seed-to-seed noise. Underreliance drops mechanically from 7.2% to 0.0% because a fully sycophantic AI never disagrees with the user, removing the events that define underreliance — not because sycophancy improves user judgement.

### 3.2 Is this decline definitional, or conditional? A multi-seed competence-crossing test

The base result is easy to over-read as "sycophancy is bad" in the abstract, when the model as built only guarantees that *replacing a more accurate signal with a less accurate one* is bad. The previous revision tested this with a single-seed, s∈{0,1} comparison; here we re-run the **full s-sweep across 20 seeds per configuration** to check the crossover result itself is not a single-seed or single-s artifact:

| configuration | s=0→1.0 trust trajectory shape | Δ trust (s1−s0), mean ± sd | Δ late acc (s1−s0), mean ± sd |
|---|---|---|---|
| AI>user (base) | monotonic decline (0.788→0.570) | −0.218 ± 0.007 | −0.199 ± 0.010 |
| AI=user (equal) | flat, non-monotonic (0.664→0.668→0.665→0.668→0.667→0.666) | +0.002 ± 0.007 | −0.025 ± 0.011 |
| user>AI (reversed) | monotonic increase (0.569→0.790) | +0.220 ± 0.007 | +0.119 ± 0.011 |

The multi-seed, full-s version confirms the single-seed finding from the prior revision (there, −0.224/−0.208 base and +0.210/+0.114 reversed) to within noise, and additionally shows intermediate-s behavior is monotonic at both extremes and *flat rather than monotonic* at equal competence — trust neither rises nor falls appreciably with s once the competence gap is zero, while a small accuracy cost persists at every s>0 (discussed in §3.3). This answers whether the equal-competence near-zero result could itself be noise: the 20-seed trust-gap standard deviation (0.007) exceeds the point estimate (0.002), so trust is genuinely indistinguishable from zero here, while the accuracy gap (−0.025 ± 0.011) is roughly two standard deviations from zero — small but not spurious.

**Sensitivity to structural constants.** A remaining concern from prior review is that this crossover pattern could be an artifact of the hand-set trust learning rate and reliance-logistic weights. We re-ran the base-vs-equal-competence s=0-vs-s=1 gap under `LR ∈ {0.04, 0.12, 0.30}` and reliance weights `(w_T,w_c) ∈ {(1,1),(3,2),(6,4)}`:

| perturbation | base trust Δ | base acc Δ | equal-comp. trust Δ | equal-comp. acc Δ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| default (0.12, 3, 2) | −0.217 | −0.208 | −0.002 | −0.032 |
| low LR (0.04) | −0.187 | −0.202 | −0.004 | −0.032 |
| high LR (0.30) | −0.228 | −0.207 | −0.008 | −0.038 |
| low reliance slope (1,1) | −0.222 | −0.158 | −0.004 | −0.018 |
| high reliance slope (6,4) | −0.208 | −0.234 | −0.003 | −0.038 |

Across a roughly 8× range in LR and a 6× range in reliance-logistic slope, the base-configuration trust gap stays in a narrow −0.19 to −0.23 band and the equal-competence trust gap never exceeds ±0.01. This does not pin down the exact crossover point as a continuous function of the constants (left to future work), but shows the qualitative claim — large robust harm when AI≫user, negligible trust effect at equal competence — is not an artifact of any single constant choice we tested.

This confirms the reviewers' suspicion that the headline effect is partly a consequence of the model's construction, and converts it into an explicit, quantified, multi-seed-and-sensitivity-checked claim: sycophancy's cost on trust is close to zero at equal competence and grows with the competence gap. We do not think this weakens the paper's motivation — a domain-competent AI advising a non-expert user is the scenario the base configuration models, and current deployments plausibly sit there — but we have not measured that gap directly in any real deployment, and flag it as an assumption, not an established fact, in §4.

### 3.3 Confidence decoupling: an additive harm channel, and what it does *not* explain

Comparing calibrated vs. decoupled confidence at matched *s* (base competence configuration), decoupling raises overreliance at every sycophancy level and the gap widens with *s* (+0.028 at s=0 to +0.045 at s=1), holding even at s=0 — evidence this is a harm mechanism distinct from and additive to the competence-gap mechanism of §3.2.

A natural hypothesis is that the small residual accuracy cost at *equal* competence (−0.025 to −0.036 depending on seed set) is simply this same confidence-decoupling effect showing through. We tested this directly: at equal competence, the calibrated-confidence s=0-vs-s=1 accuracy gap is −0.036, but the *decoupled*-confidence gap at the same competence level is only −0.010 — smaller, not larger. So decoupling confidence does not explain the equal-competence residual; if anything it shrinks it. The residual instead appears tied to the *interaction* of calibrated confidence with sycophancy: even when AI and user are equally accurate on average, a deferred answer is systematically the user's belief, and calibrated confidence conditions on whether that particular stated answer happens to be right, subtly changing which answers get followed and reinforced relative to the non-sycophantic case. We describe this pattern rather than claim to have fully isolated its mechanism.

## 4. Conclusion

We presented a reproducible agent-based simulation of AI sycophancy propagating through a repeated human-trust-calibration loop. This revision strengthens three claims the previous version supported only with single-seed, single-s evidence: (1) the base s=0-vs-s=1 gap and (2) the competence-crossing pattern are both now confirmed across 20 seeds and, for the crossing sweep, across the full s-range rather than just the endpoints; and (3) the crossing pattern is qualitatively robust across a range of hand-set trust-learning-rate and reliance-logistic-weight settings, not one arbitrary parameterization. An isolation experiment further shows the small residual harm at equal competence is not simply the confidence-decoupling mechanism in disguise, leaving a modest, partially-understood third mechanism as an honest open question rather than a hidden confound.

What we have *not* done, stated plainly: no real human-subject or human-LLM interaction data enters this simulation anywhere. The trust-update rule, reliance sigmoid, and confidence means are illustrative — the sensitivity sweep shows the qualitative pattern survives varying them, but does not validate their magnitudes against any ground truth. Most importantly, the paper's practical motivation — that current LLM-advisor deployments typically sit in the AI-more-competent-than-user regime that makes sycophancy costly — is an assumption motivated by general capability benchmarks, not a claim tested against real deployment data; every reviewer of this manuscript independently flagged this as the load-bearing, unvalidated piece, and we agree it is the right target for follow-up work: fitting the free parameters to human-subject or human-LLM interaction logs, and measuring the actual competence gap in specific deployed domains (e.g., against clinician or programmer baselines) rather than assuming it. Even in its current, explicitly bounded form, the simulation gives a concrete, testable account of *when* a "helpful, agreeable" AI advisor is worse for socio-technical outcomes than a disagreeable but honest one, and a reproducible testbed — now with a demonstrated sensitivity-analysis methodology — for evaluating socio-technical mitigations before they are deployed against real models.
