# Sycophancy Erodes the Trust It Is Meant to Preserve: An Agent-Based Simulation of Human–AI Trust Calibration Under Deference Bias

## Abstract

Sycophancy — an AI system's tendency to align its stated answer with a user's expressed belief rather than its own best judgement — is usually studied as a property of a single model response. We argue this framing misses the socio-technical dynamic that matters most in deployed decision-support settings: sycophancy operates inside a *repeated* human-AI loop where user trust is continually recalibrated from outcomes, and a model that agrees to please can systematically corrupt that calibration — but only under a specific, testable condition. We build a lightweight agent-based simulation of a human user and an AI advisor interacting over 60 rounds of binary decisions, with a tunable sycophancy parameter *s* controlling how often the AI defers to the user's prior belief when it disagrees with the AI's private judgement. In our base configuration, where the AI is more accurate than the user (0.78 vs. 0.55), trust and decision accuracy decline monotonically in *s* (final trust 0.79 at s=0 vs. 0.57 at s=1; late-round accuracy 0.76 vs. 0.55) and overreliance roughly doubles (14.9%→26.2%, 30.7% with confidence decoupled from correctness). We show, using 20 independent random seeds, that this gap is stable (trust gap −0.215 ± 0.006, accuracy gap −0.198 ± 0.009 across seeds) and not sampling noise. Critically, a new competence-crossing sweep shows this decline is *not* a generic property of sycophancy: when AI and user competence are equal, the sycophancy cost nearly vanishes (trust delta −0.01), and when the user is *more* accurate than the AI, sycophancy actually *raises* both trust and accuracy (+0.21 trust, +0.11 accuracy). Sycophancy is harmful in this model precisely to the degree that it discards a more-accurate private judgement in favor of a less-accurate one — a mechanistic, not universal, claim, which we argue is nonetheless the practically relevant case for most current LLM-as-advisor deployments where the model is typically better-calibrated on the task than a lay user. We further show that decoupling expressed confidence from correctness compounds overreliance at every sycophancy level, independent of the competence gap.

## 1. Introduction

Large language models are increasingly deployed as decision-support advisors — in medicine, coding, forecasting, and everyday judgement calls — where a human retains final authority but relies on the model's stated answer and confidence. A growing empirical literature documents that these models are *sycophantic*: they shift their stated position toward a user's expressed opinion even when wrong. This is a genuine socio-technical problem because it sits at the interface between a technical property (the model's output distribution) and a social process (a person's evolving trust in the model).

Most existing sycophancy work measures *single-turn* behavior: does the model flip its answer after a user objects. This leaves open what happens to the *human side* of the loop over many repeated interactions, when trust is built and eroded from experience. Trust-calibration research separately studies how people update reliance on automated systems from outcome feedback, but rarely models the feedback-generating process as something the AI itself can bias through sycophancy, and rarely asks *under what condition* that bias becomes costly rather than neutral or even helpful. This paper targets both gaps: we connect a tunable sycophancy parameter to the resulting trajectory of human trust and reliance errors, and we explicitly test — rather than assume — whether the resulting harm depends on the AI being more competent than the user it defers to.

### Related Work

**Sycophancy measurement.** Sharma et al.-style multi-turn studies and follow-ons propose metrics (Turn-of-Flip, Number-of-Flip) quantifying how readily a model abandons a stance under pushback; model scale and reasoning-oriented training reduce but do not eliminate this. Later work disentangles "pure" sycophancy from uncertainty-driven conformity, documents persistence in medical QA, extends the concept to social/emotional validation ("social sycophancy"), and benchmarks robustness to persuasion (PARROT).

**Trust calibration.** A separate literature rooted in human-automation interaction studies how operators calibrate trust in automated aids from experienced reliability (contextual-bandit dynamic calibration, multi-layer trust stacks). This literature treats the automation's reliability as exogenous, focusing on the human update rule rather than on how the AI's own strategic behavior shapes the reliability signal the human observes.

**The gap.** These literatures rarely intersect empirically, and neither asks the conditional question we test here: does sycophancy's cost depend on the relative competence of AI and user? Our contribution is a minimal, reproducible simulation that puts sycophancy and trust dynamics in the same loop and treats the AI-vs-user competence gap as an experimental variable rather than a fixed background assumption.

## 2. Method

### 2.1 Simulation design

We simulate independent "dyads" of one human user and one AI advisor over `N_ROUNDS = 60` binary decision rounds, with `N_DYADS = 400` dyads per condition. Each round: (1) a ground-truth label is drawn uniformly; (2) the user's private belief is correct with probability `USER_COMPETENCE` (base: 0.55); (3) the AI's private judgement is correct with probability `AI_COMPETENCE` (base: 0.78); (4) if the AI's private judgement disagrees with the user's stated belief, the AI defers to the user with probability `s` (swept over {0.0,…,1.0}); (5) expressed confidence, in the *calibrated* condition, is higher when the stated answer is actually correct (mean 0.85) than wrong (mean 0.55); in the *decoupled* ablation confidence is always ≈0.85 regardless; (6) the user follows the AI with probability `sigmoid(3·(T−0.5) + 2·(conf−0.5))`, where `T` is current trust; (7) trust updates toward whether the AI's *stated* answer matched ground truth, at full strength (`LR=0.12`) if the user relied on it that round and 0.3× otherwise.

We report the trust trajectory, mean and late-round (last 10 rounds) accuracy, overreliance (follows AI, AI wrong), and underreliance (ignores AI, AI right, user's own belief wrong).

### 2.2 Baselines, ablations, and new sensitivity analyses

- **Sycophancy sweep** (s = 0…1) at the base competence gap (AI 0.78, user 0.55).
- **Confidence-calibration ablation**: crossing every sycophancy level with calibrated vs. decoupled confidence.
- **Multi-seed variance check** (new): we re-run the s=0 and s=1 calibrated conditions under 20 independent RNG seeds (0–19) to obtain a cross-seed distribution of the headline trust/accuracy/overreliance gap, rather than reporting a single-seed point estimate.
- **Competence-crossing sweep** (new): we re-run the full s=0 vs. s=1 comparison at three competence configurations — AI 0.78/user 0.55 (base), AI 0.65/user 0.65 (equal), and AI 0.55/user 0.78 (reversed) — to directly test whether the direction and magnitude of sycophancy's effect is a generic property of deference or is conditional on the AI outperforming the user.

All code (`sim.py`, `sim_extra.py`), raw output (`sim_log.txt`, `results_extra.json`), and full per-round results (`results.json`) are included for exact reproducibility. The base sweep is deterministic given `RNG_SEED=0`; the new analyses additionally use seeds 0–19. All runs complete in under 1 second on a single CPU core.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Base configuration: monotonic decline, and how stable it is

Table 1 (calibrated confidence, AI more competent than user) reproduces the base sweep, now with the per-condition standard deviation across the 400 dyads (`final_trust_std`), which the original analysis computed but omitted from the text:

| s | final trust (±sd, N=400) | late accuracy | overreliance | underreliance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.0 | 0.794 ± 0.087 | 0.758 | 0.149 | 0.072 |
| 0.2 | 0.743 ± 0.089 | 0.708 | 0.176 | 0.064 |
| 0.4 | 0.696 ± 0.090 | 0.665 | 0.205 | 0.049 |
| 0.6 | 0.669 ± 0.096 | 0.635 | 0.217 | 0.037 |
| 0.8 | 0.609 ± 0.099 | 0.587 | 0.238 | 0.021 |
| 1.0 | 0.561 ± 0.100 | 0.544 | 0.262 | 0.000 |

Because a per-dyad standard deviation of ~0.09 on N=400 dyads still leaves a very small standard error (~0.004–0.005) on the mean, we additionally ran the s=0/s=1 comparison under 20 independent seeds to check that the whole 400-dyad Monte Carlo run itself is not an artifact of `RNG_SEED=0`. Across seeds, the trust gap is −0.215 ± 0.006 and the late-accuracy gap is −0.198 ± 0.009 (mean ± sd over 20 seeds) — an order of magnitude larger than the seed-to-seed noise, confirming the headline s=0-vs-s=1 gap is not a single-seed artifact.

Sampling the trust trajectory every 10 rounds (corrected against `results.json`, starting at the common initial trust of 0.5): s=0.0: 0.53→0.70→0.76→0.79→0.79→0.79; s=0.4: 0.52→0.63→0.68→0.70→0.68→0.69; s=1.0: 0.51→0.55→0.56→0.56→0.56→0.56. Underreliance drops mechanically from 7.2% to 0.0% because a fully sycophantic AI never disagrees with the user, removing the events (AI right, user ignored it) that define underreliance — not because sycophancy improves user judgement.

### 3.2 Is this decline definitional, or conditional? A competence-crossing test

The base result is easy to over-read as "sycophancy is bad" in the abstract, when the model as built only guarantees that *replacing a more accurate signal with a less accurate one* is bad. We test this directly by re-running the s=0 vs. s=1 comparison at three competence configurations:

| configuration | AI comp. | user comp. | Δ final trust (s=1 − s=0) | Δ late accuracy (s=1 − s=0) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| AI > user (base) | 0.78 | 0.55 | −0.224 | −0.208 |
| AI = user | 0.65 | 0.65 | −0.010 | −0.036 |
| AI < user (reversed) | 0.55 | 0.78 | +0.210 | +0.114 |

This confirms the reviewers' suspicion that the headline effect is partly a consequence of the model's construction — but it converts that suspicion into an explicit, quantified, falsifiable claim rather than leaving it implicit: sycophancy's cost is a near-linear function of *how much more accurate the AI is than the user*, crossing from harmful to neutral to beneficial as that gap crosses zero. When competence is equal, full deference costs almost nothing in trust and only a modest amount in accuracy (residual harm here comes from the confidence/reliance mechanics, not from discarding a better signal). When the user is the more accurate party, sycophancy — perhaps surprisingly relabeled here as "listening to the better-informed party" — helps. We do not think this weakens the paper's motivation: for current LLM decision-support deployments (medical triage, code review, forecasting) the modeled scenario, a domain-competent AI advising a non-expert user, is the common case, so the base configuration's harm is the practically relevant regime. But the paper's contribution is now stated correctly as characterizing *when* sycophancy is costly, not asserting that it always is.

### 3.3 Confidence decoupling compounds harm independently

Comparing calibrated vs. decoupled confidence at matched *s* (base competence configuration):

| s | overreliance (calibrated) | overreliance (decoupled) | Δ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.0 | 0.149 | 0.177 | +0.028 |
| 0.4 | 0.205 | 0.236 | +0.030 |
| 0.8 | 0.238 | 0.283 | +0.046 |
| 1.0 | 0.262 | 0.307 | +0.045 |

Decoupling confidence from correctness raises overreliance at every sycophancy level, and the gap widens as sycophancy increases — this holds even at s=0, showing confidence miscalibration is a harm mechanism distinct from, and additive to, the competence-gap mechanism identified in §3.2. Unlike the competence-crossing result, we have no reason to expect this to reverse sign under any competence configuration, since it operates purely through the user's reliance decision rather than through which answer is correct.

## 4. Conclusion

We presented a reproducible agent-based simulation of AI sycophancy propagating through a repeated human-trust-calibration loop. Revising against reviewer feedback, we now show three things the original submission asserted but did not test: (1) the headline s=0-vs-s=1 gap is stable across 20 random seeds, not a single-seed artifact; (2) the direction and size of sycophancy's cost is conditional on the AI-vs-user competence gap, vanishing near equal competence and reversing sign when the user is the more accurate party — so the paper's claim is precisely that sycophancy is costly *when it discards a more accurate private judgement*, which we argue is the realistic regime for current LLM advisors but is no longer left as an unstated assumption; and (3) confidence decoupling from correctness is an independent, additive harm mechanism that does not depend on the competence gap. The simulation remains deliberately simple — binary tasks, a single trust scalar, hand-set competence, learning-rate, and reliance-slope constants not fit to real data — and does not model real conversational sycophancy mechanisms. Natural extensions include fitting the free parameters to human-subject or human-LLM interaction logs, adding heterogeneous users, and replacing the synthetic AI with real sycophancy-elicitation transcripts scored for stance-flip and confidence jointly. Even in its current form, the simulation gives a concrete, testable account of *when* a "helpful, agreeable" AI advisor is worse for socio-technical outcomes than a disagreeable but honest one, and a reproducible testbed for evaluating socio-technical mitigations — including confidence-calibration audits alongside stance-consistency audits — before they are deployed against real models.
