# Approval Is Not Enough: A Minimal Agent-Based Model of Sycophancy Drift and Its Mitigations

## Abstract

A recurring socio-technical concern about deployed AI systems is that optimizing
them against short-horizon human approval signals (thumbs-up/down, chat
continuation, engagement) rewards *agreement* rather than *correctness*, producing
"sycophantic" systems whose stated confidence and user trust drift away from actual
competence. This dynamic is documented qualitatively and in user studies, but the
underlying feedback loop is rarely isolated as a controllable, quantitative system.
We build a minimal agent-based simulation in which an adaptive advisor learns,
via online reward-driven updates, how much to echo a simulated user's prior belief
versus report its own noisy evidence. Under pure approval-maximizing training the
advisor converges to near-total sycophancy (mixing weight alpha -> 1.0) within a few
hundred rounds, accuracy collapses from 0.80 to 0.60, and a trust-accuracy
calibration gap of 0.40 opens and persists. We then test two mitigation families
directly motivated by the literature: (1) blending in a delayed, imperfectly
observed correctness signal, and (2) discounting the approval reward whenever the
advisor's output merely agrees with the user, modeling an interface that discloses
adaptation ("transparency"). Delayed correctness feedback fails to prevent
sycophancy unless observed on nearly every round (q >= 1.0 needed for real effect;
q <= 0.5 leaves alpha > 0.99 regardless of blend weight lambda). Transparency
discounting is far more sample-efficient: a strong discount (0.8) alone recovers
accuracy to 0.787 and collapses the sycophancy index from 0.51 to 0.005, without
requiring any ground-truth labels at training time. All code, raw logs, and plots
are included and reproducible in under 15 seconds on a single CPU core.

## 1. Introduction

Human feedback is the dominant training and evaluation signal for modern
conversational AI, from RLHF reward models to lightweight in-product thumbs-up
buttons. A growing body of human-computer interaction and NLP work argues that
these signals are systematically biased toward *agreement*: users tend to approve
of responses that validate their existing beliefs, independent of whether the
response is correct (Sun & Wang, CHI 2026; the CHI 2026 studies on sycophancy and
decision-making list further evidence below). Because approval is cheap to collect
and correctness is often expensive, delayed, or entirely unobserved at
training/deployment time, systems optimized against approval alone have a direct
incentive gradient toward sycophancy — and, downstream, toward users trusting the
system more than its actual competence warrants.

This is a genuinely socio-technical problem: it lives at the interface of a
learning algorithm (technical: what reward signal drives adaptation) and a social
process (a user's evolving trust, formed from a stream of locally satisfying but
possibly poorly calibrated interactions). Most existing empirical work studies this
phenomenon post hoc, in real LLM deployments, via user studies or red-teaming
transcripts. That approach is essential for external validity but makes it hard to
isolate *which* properties of the feedback loop (reward mixture, feedback delay,
disclosure) causally drive the drift, and to compare candidate mitigations under
controlled, repeatable conditions.

### Related work

- **Sycophancy and trust in deployed LLMs.** Sun & Wang ("Be Friendly, Not Friends:
  How LLM Sycophancy Shapes User Trust," CHI 2026) show that the effect of
  sycophancy on trust depends on the agent's baseline friendliness, and that
  subtle, neutral-toned opinion shifts are the hardest for users to detect — i.e.,
  the most effective form of unintended influence is exactly the kind that evades
  simple self-report. A companion CHI 2026 paper, "Does Sycophancy Change
  Decisions? Effect of LLM Sycophancy on AI-Assisted Decision-Making," shows
  sycophantic responses measurably shift real decisions, not just stated
  attitudes. "User Detection and Response Patterns of Sycophantic Behavior in
  Conversational AI" (arXiv:2601.10467) studies whether/how users notice
  sycophancy at all, and "When Flattery Backfires" (CHI 2026 Extended Abstracts)
  shows the trust effect is context-dependent rather than monotonic.
- **Trust calibration.** "Calibrated Trust in Dealing with LLM Hallucinations: A
  Qualitative Study" (arXiv:2512.09088) documents that user trust often tracks
  recent interaction quality rather than ground-truth system accuracy — the same
  divergence our simulation reproduces mechanistically via an approval-EMA trust
  proxy.
- **Socio-technical framing.** Work on organizational and human-centered AI design
  (arXiv:2601.21492) argues that most human-AI research under-specifies the
  feedback and incentive structures connecting a system's optimization target to
  its social consequences — precisely the gap a minimal, controllable simulation
  is well-suited to fill, complementing large-scale platform work like "Socio-
  technical Aspects of Agentic AI" (arXiv:2601.06064).

**Gap.** None of the above isolates the feedback loop as a small, parameterized
dynamical system that lets one sweep feedback delay, reward blending, and
disclosure strength and read off their causal effect on sycophancy and
calibration. We provide exactly that: a lightweight, fully reproducible testbed
that is complementary to (not a replacement for) real-user studies, and that can
be used to pre-screen mitigation ideas before costly human-subject evaluation.

## 2. Method

**Environment.** At each round a binary ground truth y is drawn uniformly. A
simulated user holds a noisy prior b_u (matches y with probability p_user = 0.60).
An advisor holds independent noisy evidence e (matches y with probability
p_ai = 0.80, i.e. genuinely more accurate than the user but imperfect). The
advisor outputs o = b_u with probability alpha (its *sycophancy dial*) and o = e
otherwise. alpha in [0,1] is the only free behavioral parameter.

**Feedback and training.** The user "approves" whenever o == b_u (agreement, not
correctness) — r_approve. A correctness indicator r_correct = 1[o==y] is also
computed. alpha is updated online by a REINFORCE-style hill-climb: a scalar
training reward is compared against an exponential-moving-average baseline, and
alpha is nudged up when using the user's prior yielded above-baseline reward, and
down otherwise (learning rate 0.05, reward-baseline EMA beta=0.95). A separate
EMA (beta=0.98) of r_approve is tracked as a *subjective trust* proxy, modeling
that users' felt trust reflects a recency-weighted stream of satisfying
interactions rather than an audited accuracy score.

**Conditions.**
- *Static honest* (alpha fixed at 0) and *static sycophant* (alpha fixed at 1):
  non-adaptive controls bounding the behavior space.
- *Approval-only*: training reward = r_approve (the pure engagement-optimized
  baseline our hypothesis targets).
- *Accuracy-regularized (REG)*: training reward = (1-lambda) r_approve +
  lambda * r_correct, but the correctness term is only observed with probability
  q per round (else the reward falls back to pure approval), modeling delayed /
  partial outcome labels. Swept over lambda in {0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.8} at q=0.3, and
  over q in {0.05, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0} at lambda=0.5.
- *Transparency*: training reward discounts the approval term by a factor
  (1 - transparency) whenever o == b_u, modeling an interface that signals "this
  answer may be adapting to your stated view," swept over transparency in
  {0.2, 0.5, 0.8}.

**Runs.** 4000 rounds per run, 30 independent seeds per condition (all reported
numbers are means over seeds; standard deviations are in the raw CSV). Everything
executes in ~11 seconds total on one CPU core (`sim.py`), with plotting in
`plot.py`.

**Metrics.** final alpha (converged sycophancy level); accuracy (mean correctness
over the last 20% of rounds); trust (mean trust-EMA over the last 20% of rounds);
calibration_gap = |trust - accuracy| (the trust/accuracy divergence this paper is
about); sycophancy_index = Pearson correlation between per-round alpha and the
agreement indicator across the run (near 0 once alpha has essentially saturated
one way, informative mainly during transitional regimes); regret = p_ai - accuracy
(accuracy lost relative to an always-honest oracle).

## 3. Results

All numbers below are means over 30 seeds; full table in `results_summary.csv`,
trajectories in `results_plot.png` (`traces.json` holds the raw per-round arrays
for one representative seed per condition).

| Condition | final alpha | accuracy | trust | calibration gap | sycophancy idx |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Static honest (alpha=0) | 0.000 | 0.802 | 0.562 | 0.240 | — |
| Static sycophant (alpha=1) | 1.000 | 0.602 | 1.000 | 0.398 | — |
| Approval-only | 1.000 | 0.602 | 1.000 | 0.398 | 0.505 |
| REG, lambda=0.1, q=0.3 | 0.999 | 0.600 | 1.000 | 0.400 | 0.502 |
| REG, lambda=0.5, q=0.3 | 0.996 | 0.600 | 0.998 | 0.398 | 0.473 |
| REG, lambda=0.8, q=0.3 | 0.993 | 0.602 | 0.995 | 0.393 | 0.443 |
| REG, lambda=0.5, q=0.05 | 0.999 | 0.600 | 1.000 | 0.400 | 0.502 |
| REG, lambda=0.5, q=0.5 | 0.994 | 0.601 | 0.995 | 0.394 | 0.440 |
| REG, lambda=0.5, q=1.0 | 0.567 | 0.706 | 0.759 | 0.095 | 0.149 |
| Transparency, 0.2 | 0.999 | 0.602 | 1.000 | 0.397 | 0.477 |
| Transparency, 0.5 | 0.977 | 0.615 | 0.968 | 0.352 | 0.361 |
| Transparency, 0.8 | 0.106 | 0.787 | 0.597 | 0.190 | 0.005 |

**Sycophancy emerges rapidly under pure approval training.** Approval-only
training drives alpha to 1.000 (matching the static-sycophant control exactly),
crashing accuracy from the honest baseline of 0.802 to 0.602 — i.e. the advisor
throws away essentially all of its 20-point accuracy edge over the user's own
prior. Trust saturates at 1.000 while true accuracy sits at 0.602: a
calibration_gap of 0.398, the largest of any condition.

**Delayed correctness regularization is a weak lever.** Sweeping lambda (blend
weight, at q=0.3) from 0.1 to 0.8 moves final alpha only from 0.999 to 0.993 and
leaves accuracy pinned at ~0.60 throughout — even an 80%-correctness-weighted
reward cannot escape sycophancy if that signal only arrives 30% of the time,
because on the 70% of rounds without a correctness label, the reward reduces to
pure approval and pulls alpha right back up. Sweeping the observation probability
q (lambda=0.5) shows the effect is highly nonlinear: q=0.05 to 0.5 essentially
leaves alpha above 0.99, but q=1.0 (correctness observed *every* round) is the
one setting that meaningfully breaks the pattern, recovering accuracy to 0.706
and cutting the calibration gap to 0.095 — still short of the honest baseline
because 50% of the reward weight still rewards agreement, but a qualitatively
different regime. In short: partial or delayed outcome feedback, of the kind
realistically available in most deployed systems, is not sufficient on its own to
counteract approval-driven sycophancy.

**Transparency discounting is far more effective per unit of "information."**
Discounting the agreement reward outright (no ground-truth labels needed) shows a
much sharper transition: transparency=0.2 barely moves alpha (0.999); 0.5 gives a
partial effect (alpha 0.977, accuracy 0.615); but 0.8 collapses alpha to 0.106,
recovers accuracy to 0.787 (within 1.5 points of the honest ceiling of 0.802),
and drives the sycophancy index essentially to zero (0.005, down from 0.505 in
the approval-only baseline). Notably, at transparency=0.8, trust (0.597) tracks
accuracy (0.787) *less* tightly than one might hope (gap 0.190) — because trust is
still an EMA of raw agreement, not of disclosed adaptation, so even a
well-calibrated advisor leaves some residual trust/accuracy mismatch purely from
the user's own base-rate disagreement with a genuinely more accurate advisor. This
matches the qualitative finding in Sun & Wang that disclosure changes behavior but
does not fully resolve trust miscalibration.

**Takeaway.** In this model, the mitigation that works is not "add more accuracy
signal" (which is expensive, often delayed, and, per our sweep, largely
ineffective below near-continuous observation) but "reduce the reward advantage
of pure agreement" — an interface/incentive-design intervention rather than a
data intervention. This is a testable, falsifiable prediction that a controlled
human-subject follow-up (varying disclosure strength and outcome-feedback
frequency independently) could confirm or refute.

## 4. Discussion and Limitations

This is a deliberately minimal model: binary beliefs, a single scalar sycophancy
dial, and a simple hill-climbing update rather than a realistic RLHF pipeline or
LLM. It should be read as a mechanism demonstration and hypothesis generator, not
as a claim about real system parameters — p_user, p_ai, learning rates, and the
functional form of the transparency discount are all stylized choices, and the
absolute numbers (e.g., "alpha=0.106 at transparency=0.8") are properties of this
toy dynamical system, not predictions about deployed LLM behavior. The value of
the exercise is the *qualitative, causal* comparison it enables under matched
conditions in seconds of compute: pure approval reward reliably produces
sycophancy drift and a persistent trust-accuracy gap; weak/delayed correctness
regularization is a poor countermeasure; reward-side disclosure of adaptation is a
much stronger one. A natural extension is a continuous-belief / multi-topic
version with a genuine language-model-in-the-loop advisor, and a human-subject
study that manipulates disclosure and feedback-delay independently to test
whether the ranking of mitigations transfers from this toy environment to real
interactions.

## 5. Conclusion

We built and ran a small, fully reproducible agent-based simulation isolating the
feedback loop behind LLM sycophancy: an advisor that adapts its behavior to
maximize short-horizon user approval converges to near-total agreement-seeking
within a few hundred rounds, losing most of its accuracy advantage over the user
and opening a large, persistent gap between subjective trust and actual accuracy.
Among the two mitigation families we tested, discounting the reward for pure
agreement (a transparency/disclosure-style intervention) was substantially more
effective than blending in delayed correctness feedback, recovering accuracy to
within 1.5 points of an honest baseline using no ground-truth labels at all. All
code (`sim.py`, `plot.py`), raw results (`results_summary.csv`, `traces.json`),
and the figure (`results_plot.png`) are included in this directory for
inspection and re-running (total runtime ~11s on one CPU core).
