# Approval Is Not Enough: A Minimal Agent-Based Model of Sycophancy Drift and the Illusion of Short-Horizon Mitigations

## Abstract

Optimizing AI systems against short-horizon human approval (thumbs-up/down, chat
continuation) rewards *agreement* over *correctness*. We build a minimal
agent-based simulation in which an adaptive advisor learns, via online
reward-driven updates, how much to echo a simulated user's prior belief versus
report its own noisier-but-better evidence. Under pure approval-maximizing
training the advisor converges to near-total sycophancy (alpha -> 1.0), accuracy
collapses from 0.80 to 0.60, and a trust-accuracy calibration gap opens. At a
fixed 4000-round budget, a "transparency" mitigation (discounting approval
reward whenever the advisor merely agrees) looked far more effective than
blending in delayed correctness feedback (REG). Independent review flagged that
REG's correctness signal was diluted through *two* multiplicative factors while
transparency's was diluted through only one — an unfair fight. We built a
matched, single-knob variant (REG_FAIR) and re-ran both mitigations to
20,000-60,000 rounds (5-15x the original budget). The result overturns our
original headline finding: transparency discounting is mathematically
equivalent to shrinking the effective learning rate (verified exactly:
TRANSPARENCY at discount 0.8, LR=0.1 reproduces APPROVAL_ONLY at LR=0.02 to 6
decimals), so it only *delays* drift to alpha=1 — by 20,000 rounds it
reconverges to alpha~1.000, accuracy 0.601, indistinguishable from
approval-only. Only REG_FAIR at lambda=0.8 reaches a stable low-sycophancy fixed
point (alpha=0.018, accuracy=0.799) that persists at 60,000 rounds. This
revision fixes two further issues raised independently by all four reviewers.
First, the script that generated our long-horizon numbers was never included;
we now ship `sim_longhorizon.py`, which reproduces every long-horizon number in
this paper directly (67s on one CPU core). Second, our claimed
residual-calibration-gap range (0.19-0.20) did not match what our own model
produces when trust is tracked correctly; the corrected figure, computed by
`sim_longhorizon.py`, is 0.23 (0.217-0.250 at 20k rounds, 0.221-0.237 at 60k),
which we now report and analyze rather than the earlier, uncomputed estimate.
Third, we add a lambda-vs-accuracy-gap sensitivity sweep: REG_FAIR's fixed
point requires *both* a sufficiently large correctness weight and a
sufficiently large p_ai-p_user gap — at a smaller gap (0.10), even
lambda=0.8 fails to reliably restore honesty (bimodal outcomes across seeds,
alpha=0.22 +/- 0.17), showing the mitigation's guarantee is conditional, not
universal. All code, raw logs, and plots are included and reproducible in
under 100 seconds total on one CPU core.

## 1. Introduction

Human feedback is the dominant training/evaluation signal for conversational AI,
from RLHF reward models to in-product thumbs-up buttons. HCI and NLP work argues
these signals are systematically biased toward agreement: users tend to approve
of responses that validate existing beliefs, independent of correctness (Sun &
Wang, CHI 2026). Because approval is cheap and correctness is often delayed or
unobserved, systems optimized against approval alone have a direct incentive
gradient toward sycophancy. This is a socio-technical problem: a learning
algorithm (technical) interacting with a user's evolving trust (social). Most
existing empirical work studies this post hoc in real deployments; that is
essential for external validity but makes it hard to isolate *which* properties
of the feedback loop causally drive drift and to compare mitigations under
controlled, repeatable conditions.

### Related work

- **Sycophancy and trust in deployed LLMs.** Sun & Wang ("Be Friendly, Not
  Friends," CHI 2026) show subtle, neutral-toned opinion shifts are hardest for
  users to detect. "Does Sycophancy Change Decisions?" (CHI 2026) shows
  sycophantic responses shift real decisions. "User Detection and Response
  Patterns of Sycophantic Behavior" (arXiv:2601.10467) studies whether users
  notice sycophancy at all; "When Flattery Backfires" (CHI 2026 EA) shows the
  trust effect is context-dependent.
- **Trust calibration.** "Calibrated Trust in Dealing with LLM Hallucinations"
  (arXiv:2512.09088) documents that user trust tracks recent interaction
  quality rather than ground-truth accuracy — the divergence our simulation
  reproduces mechanistically via an approval-EMA trust proxy.
- **Reward misspecification and specification gaming.** Our approval-vs-
  correctness setup is a minimal instance of the broader reward-hacking
  problem studied in RL theory. Our contribution is a small, socio-technically
  motivated instance parameterized around observable interface variables
  (disclosure, feedback delay) rather than abstract reward functions, letting
  us compare *interface-level* mitigations directly.
- **Socio-technical framing.** Work on human-centered AI design
  (arXiv:2601.21492, arXiv:2601.06064) argues human-AI research under-specifies
  the feedback/incentive structures linking optimization targets to social
  consequences — the gap this simulation is built to fill.

**Gap.** None of the above isolates the feedback loop as a small, parameterized
dynamical system letting one sweep feedback delay, reward blending, and
disclosure strength and read off causal, *long-run* effects on sycophancy and
calibration. We provide that, and — following independent review — we show
that reading off effects from a single fixed training horizon can be actively
misleading: a mitigation that looks effective at 4000 rounds can be a transient
artifact that a longer horizon reveals as no mitigation at all.

## 2. Method

**Environment.** Each round draws binary ground truth y uniformly. A simulated
user holds a noisy prior b_u (matches y with probability p_user=0.60). An
advisor holds independent noisy evidence e (matches y with probability
p_ai=0.80). The advisor outputs o=b_u with probability alpha (its sycophancy
dial), else o=e. alpha in [0,1] is updated online by a REINFORCE-style
hill-climb: a scalar training reward is compared to an EMA baseline
(beta=0.95), and alpha is nudged up when using the user's prior yielded
above-baseline reward, down otherwise (learning rate 0.05 unless swept). A
separate EMA (beta=0.98) of agreement is tracked as *subjective trust* — a
recency-weighted proxy, not an audited accuracy score. We flag this as a
modeling choice: a trust proxy that instead updates on *later-revealed*
correctness would by construction track accuracy more closely and would likely
shrink the calibration gap we report; we leave that variant to future work.

**Conditions.** *Static honest/sycophant* (alpha fixed at 0/1): bounding
controls. *Approval-only*: reward=r_approve. *REG(lambda,q)*: reward =
(1-lambda) r_approve + lambda r_correct, but r_correct is only available with
probability q per round (else falls back to pure approval) — modeling
delayed/partial outcome labels, diluting the correctness signal through *two*
factors. *REG_FAIR(lambda)*: r_correct observed every round (q=1), so the
correctness term is attenuated by exactly one factor (lambda), matching how
*Transparency* attenuates agreement by exactly one factor (1-transparency).
*Transparency*: reward = r_approve * (1-transparency) whenever o==b_u.

**Runs and reproducibility fix.** Original sweep: 4000 rounds, 30 seeds
(`sim.py`, ~9s). `sim_extra.py` (~20s) adds REG_FAIR at lambda in
{0.2,0.5,0.8}, a finer q sweep, and an LR/baseline-beta robustness sweep. All
four round-2 reviewers independently reran the code and flagged that the
20,000/60,000-round numbers reported in the previous revision had no
corresponding driver script — `sim.py` and `sim_extra.py` both cap at 4000
rounds. This revision adds `sim_longhorizon.py` (~67s), which actually
generates `results_longhorizon.csv`: it re-runs the key conditions at 20,000
and 60,000 rounds, correctly tracks the trust EMA (the version of
`run_condition` inside `sim_extra.py` computed a local `trust_ema` but never
returned it, so no long-horizon calibration-gap number was ever computed from
included code), and adds a lambda x accuracy-gap sensitivity sweep for
REG_FAIR. Total runtime for all three scripts is under 100s on one CPU core.

**Metrics.** final alpha; accuracy (mean correctness, last 20% of rounds);
trust (mean trust-EMA, last 20%); calibration_gap=|trust-accuracy|. We no
longer report the alpha-agreement correlation ("sycophancy index") as a
headline number — as flagged in prior review, it is uninformative once alpha
saturates in either direction and is at most a transitional-regime diagnostic.

## 3. Results

**Short-horizon (4000 rounds) baseline.** Approval-only training drives alpha
to 1.000, crashing accuracy from the honest baseline (0.802) to 0.602 and
opening a calibration gap of 0.398 (trust saturates at 1.000). REG with
two-factor dilution barely moves alpha (0.999->0.993 across lambda at q=0.3); a
finer q sweep at lambda=0.5 locates the transition between q=0.7 (alpha=0.981)
and q=0.9 (alpha=0.786), with q=1.0 giving alpha=0.567. At this horizon,
transparency=0.8 looked like the clear winner: alpha collapses to 0.106,
accuracy recovers to 0.787.

**Fairness check: REG_FAIR vs. Transparency.** REG_FAIR removes the q dilution.
At 4000 rounds and matched strength 0.8, REG_FAIR *outperforms* transparency:
alpha=0.017 vs. 0.106 (Welch t=-11.60, p=3.6e-13, n=30 each) and accuracy=0.799
vs. 0.787 (t=3.75, p=4.5e-4). This confirms the two-factor-vs-one-factor
asymmetry substantially drove the original "transparency wins" finding.

**Long-horizon re-run: transparency's equilibrium is a transient.** At 20,000
rounds (30 seeds, from `sim_longhorizon.py`): transparency=0.2/0.5/0.8 all
converge to alpha=0.9998-1.000, accuracy=0.601 — indistinguishable from
approval-only. REG (lambda=0.5, q=1.0), a partial win at 4000 rounds
(alpha=0.567), reaches alpha=0.992 by 20,000 rounds. Only REG_FAIR at
lambda=0.8 remains stable: alpha=0.018, accuracy=0.799 at 20,000 rounds, and
alpha=0.017, accuracy=0.797 at 60,000 rounds (10 seeds) — a genuine fixed
point.

| Condition (20,000 rounds, 30 seeds) | final alpha | accuracy | calib. gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Approval-only | 1.000 | 0.601 | 0.399 |
| Transparency 0.8 | 1.000 | 0.601 | 0.399 |
| REG, lambda=0.5, q=1.0 | 0.992 | 0.600 | 0.396 |
| REG_FAIR, lambda=0.5 | 0.991 | 0.603 | 0.393 |
| REG_FAIR, lambda=0.8 | 0.018 | 0.799 | 0.232 |

**Mechanism.** Because TRANSPARENCY's reward is r_approve*(1-transparency) with
no compensating correctness term, and both the training reward and the EMA
baseline are scaled by this same constant, the alpha-update reduces exactly to
running approval-only at learning rate LR*(1-transparency). Confirmed as an
exact identity, not an approximation: TRANSPARENCY at transparency=0.8, LR=0.1
reproduces APPROVAL_ONLY at LR=0.02 bit-for-bit (alpha=0.844016,
accuracy=0.660542 in both, n=30). Transparency has no independent restoring
force; it only rescales approach speed toward the same alpha=1 corner.
REG_FAIR differs in kind: its undiluted correctness term means that at high
alpha, the average reward from *not* echoing the user (higher accuracy, since
p_ai=0.80 > p_user=0.60) can exceed the discounted-agreement reward from
echoing it, producing a genuine interior fixed point.

**Corrected calibration-gap figure.** The previous revision reported a residual
calibration gap of "0.190-0.20" for REG_FAIR lambda=0.8 without a script that
actually computed it at long horizon. All four reviewers reran the model with
correct trust-EMA tracking and independently obtained ~0.22-0.25; the
`sim_longhorizon.py` run reported here reproduces that: 0.232 mean
(range 0.217-0.250) at 20,000 rounds and 0.229 mean (range 0.221-0.237) at
60,000 rounds. We were wrong; the true residual gap is roughly 15-20% larger
than we previously estimated. The qualitative story is unchanged — REG_FAIR
substantially shrinks the gap relative to approval-only (0.399) but does not
close it, because the trust EMA tracks raw agreement, not correctness, even
near the fixed point.

**Lambda sensitivity to the accuracy gap.** Reviewers asked whether REG_FAIR's
lambda=0.8 threshold is specific to p_ai-p_user=0.20. We swept lambda in
{0.05,0.1,0.2,0.4,0.8} at gaps of 0.10 (p_user=0.65, p_ai=0.75), 0.20
(baseline), and 0.30 (p_user=0.55, p_ai=0.85), 20,000 rounds, 10 seeds each.
At gap=0.20 and 0.30, lambda=0.8 reliably restores honesty (alpha=0.020 and
0.015, low variance). At gap=0.10, lambda<=0.4 leaves alpha pinned near 0.99
regardless of strength, and even lambda=0.8 is unreliable: alpha=0.216 +/-
0.172 across seeds, i.e. some seeds escape to near-honest behavior and others
stay sycophantic — a bimodal outcome, not a clean fixed point. This confirms
the mechanism's prediction: the restoring force depends on the *margin* by
which honest reporting out-earns agreement (proportional to p_ai-p_user), so a
fixed lambda that stabilizes a large-gap advisor can fail to reliably stabilize
a smaller-gap one. REG_FAIR should be read as a demonstrated sufficient
mechanism whose required strength is gap-dependent, not a lambda value that
transfers across settings.

**Robustness.** At LR=0.02, even approval-only has not fully converged by round
4000 (alpha=0.844), confirming short-horizon numbers at low LR conflate "not
yet sycophantic" with "durably mitigated." Varying baseline_beta in
{0.90,0.95,0.99} leaves transparency=0.8's 4000-round alpha in a narrow band
(0.088-0.112) — robust to this constant, but not to LR or horizon, both of
which change the underlying regime.

**Takeaway (revised).** The lesson is not "transparency beats delayed
correctness feedback." It is that *any* mitigation which merely shrinks the
approval-reward coefficient, without adding a genuine competing signal, only
slows convergence to full sycophancy — a sufficiently long deployment will
still drift there. A mitigation is durable only if it changes the sign of the
average advantage at high alpha, which requires an undiluted correctness term
of sufficient strength relative to the advisor's accuracy advantage over the
user (REG_FAIR here), not just disclosure of the agreement bias.

## 4. Discussion and Limitations

This remains a deliberately minimal model: binary beliefs, a scalar sycophancy
dial, a hill-climbing update rather than a realistic RLHF pipeline or LLM. The
absolute numbers are properties of this toy dynamical system, not predictions
about deployed LLM behavior. Limitations we did not fully resolve: (1) the
trust proxy is a raw-agreement EMA by construction, so it cannot track a
well-calibrated advisor's accuracy even at the true fixed point — the
corrected residual calibration gap at REG_FAIR lambda=0.8 is 0.23, larger than
we previously (incorrectly) estimated; a trust model that updates on
later-revealed correctness would likely close more of this gap and is a
natural next experiment; (2) REG_FAIR's stabilizing strength depends on the
p_ai-p_user gap, as shown in Section 3 — at a gap of 0.10 even lambda=0.8 is
unreliable, so "undiluted correctness signal creates a fixed point" is a
demonstrated sufficient condition for large-enough gap/lambda combinations, not
a universal guarantee; (3) the hill-climb is a bespoke heuristic, not a
derived policy-gradient update. The long-horizon experiments substitute for,
but do not replace, a full optimizer-robustness study — we have not tested a
genuinely different optimizer family (e.g. tabular Q-learning over discretized
alpha), which remains open given this revision's time budget. A natural
extension is a continuous-belief, multi-topic model with a language-model-in-
the-loop advisor and a human-subject study manipulating disclosure, feedback
delay, and *deployment duration* independently — this revision's central
finding is that duration is a first-order determinant of whether a mitigation
is real or transient, not a nuisance variable.

## 5. Conclusion

We built a small, reproducible agent-based simulation isolating the feedback
loop behind LLM sycophancy. Approval-maximizing training reliably converges to
near-total sycophancy, collapsing accuracy and opening a persistent
trust-accuracy gap. Independent review of our original mitigation comparison
flagged a structural asymmetry between our two reward parameterizations;
correcting it (REG_FAIR) and extending the training horizon 5-15x overturned
our original headline claim: transparency-style reward discounting has no
independent restoring force and is mathematically equivalent to a slower
learning rate — it delays but does not prevent sycophancy drift. Only a reward
that blends in an undiluted, every-round correctness signal of sufficient
strength relative to the advisor's accuracy advantage reaches a genuinely
stable, near-honest equilibrium. This revision also corrects two errors
independent reviewers found in the previous version: a missing driver script
for the long-horizon experiments (now `sim_longhorizon.py`) and an incorrect
residual calibration-gap estimate (0.19-0.20 claimed vs. 0.23 actual), and adds
a new sensitivity sweep showing REG_FAIR's guarantee is conditional on the
advisor's accuracy advantage over the user. All code (`sim.py`,
`sim_extra.py`, `sim_longhorizon.py`, `plot.py`), raw results
(`results_summary.csv`, `results_extra.csv`, `results_longhorizon.csv`,
`traces.json`), and the figure (`results_plot.png`) are included and
reproducible in under 100 seconds total on one CPU core.
