# Cheap Uncertainty Signals for Error Prediction in Language Models: A Controlled Comparison on a Synthetic Arithmetic Task

## Abstract

Detecting when a language model's output is likely wrong is a central open problem for reliable deployment. A growing literature proposes uncertainty signals of varying cost: token-level predictive entropy (nearly free, one forward pass), self-consistency voting, and semantic entropy (both requiring K stochastic samples). Prior work reports semantic entropy outperforms naive token entropy on open-domain QA with large pretrained models, but these comparisons are entangled with pretraining artifacts, retrieval, and expensive human-labeled correctness judgments. We isolate the comparison in a fully controlled setting: a small Transformer decoder is trained from scratch on a synthetic arithmetic task with programmatically verifiable ground truth, deliberately under-trained so it produces a realistic mix of correct and incorrect answers. On 400 held-out arithmetic problems (seed 0), we compute all three signals from the same K=10 sampled completions plus one greedy decode per problem, and measure each signal's AUROC for predicting whether the greedy answer is correct. Point estimates replicate the qualitative ordering reported for large pretrained LLMs — semantic entropy (0.668) and self-consistency (0.673) both exceed token entropy (0.646) — but paired bootstrap confidence intervals (5000 resamples) on this run show all three pairwise gaps are **not** statistically distinguishable from zero at the 95% level. A 5-seed sweep finds the ordering is *not* consistent (token entropy wins in 2/5 seeds), and a second sweep holding the 400-problem eval set fixed across seeds finds the same 2/5 flip rate — on different seeds (2, 3 vs. 0, 1), so the matching rate itself should not be read as replication at n=5. **New in this revision**, in response to all four round-2 and round-3 reviewers asking whether K=10 undersampling explains the fragility, we re-run the fixed-eval sweep at K=20 for all 5 seeds: the flip persists identically on seeds 2 and 3. We also compute a within-seed bootstrap CI (2000 resamples) at K=20 for every seed: four of five seeds' semantic-entropy-vs-token-entropy gaps still straddle zero, but seed 3's does not — its CI is entirely negative ([-0.141, -0.013]), meaning token entropy is *significantly better* than semantic entropy on that seed alone. We revise our conclusion accordingly: this controlled setting supports only a weak, seed-sensitive version of the literature's qualitative claim, and at least one seed shows a statistically significant reversal, not just noise. We release all training, evaluation, and statistical-analysis code and raw per-example outputs, including all three seed sweeps.

## 1. Introduction

Large language models frequently produce fluent but incorrect statements ("hallucinations"), and distinguishing confident-and-correct outputs from confident-sounding-but-wrong ones is a practical necessity for any deployed system that must decide whether to answer, abstain, or ask for clarification. Because ground-truth correctness labels are expensive to obtain at scale, a large body of work studies *unsupervised* uncertainty signals extracted from the model itself.

**Related work.** The simplest signal is token-level predictive entropy, computed from the softmax distribution of a single greedy decode. Malinin & Gales (2021) formalized this class of "sequence-level uncertainty" measures. Kuhn et al. (2023) argued that token-level entropy conflates *lexical* variability with genuine *semantic* uncertainty, and proposed **semantic entropy**: cluster K sampled generations by bidirectional entailment (or exact semantic equivalence), then compute the Shannon entropy of the resulting cluster-frequency distribution. Farquhar et al. (2024, *Nature*) extended this to open-domain QA, reporting large gains over token entropy and self-consistency. Manakul et al. (2023) proposed SelfCheckGPT, a closely related sampling-consistency signal for reference-free hallucination detection — the self-consistency baseline we compare against is conceptually in this family. Follow-up work has sought cheaper approximations: Semantic Entropy Probes (Kossen et al., 2024) predict semantic entropy from a single generation's hidden states; Semantic Energy (2025) operates on penultimate-layer logits. A 2025 KDD survey (Xiao et al.) notes no single uncertainty metric dominates across tasks and that most reported comparisons are run on large pretrained models where the *source* of a model's uncertainty is hard to disentangle from the *signal's* intrinsic ability to track correctness.

**Gap.** This entanglement makes it hard to know whether semantic entropy's reported advantage reflects something fundamental about the two signal classes, or artifacts of specific pretrained models and QA benchmarks. We are not aware of prior work replicating this comparison in a fully controlled, from-scratch setting with exact ground truth and exact semantic clustering — nor of prior work checking how sensitive the comparison is to training seed, eval-set draw, and sampling budget K, which turns out to matter a great deal (Section 3).

**Contribution.** We provide such a controlled replication, together with a statistical robustness analysis the original single-run comparison lacked. We train a small (≈300K-parameter) decoder-only Transformer from random initialization on synthetic two-digit addition/subtraction, deliberately stopping training early. Because answers are integers, semantic equivalence is *exactly* decidable, removing entailment-model noise. On the originally reported seed, point estimates replicate the literature's qualitative ordering; but a paired bootstrap, two 5-seed sweeps (independent vs. fixed eval set), a K=10→K=20 sweep, and per-seed bootstrap CIs together show this ordering is fragile — and in one seed, significantly reversed. We view this as the paper's main finding: at this model/task scale, the well-known ordering is a real but weak trend, not a robust effect, and single-run comparisons should be treated with corresponding caution.

## 2. Method

**Task.** Each example is `a<op>b=` where `a, b ∈ [1, 99]`, `op ∈ {+, -}` (subtraction ordered non-negative), target is the decimal result — exactly checkable ground truth.

**Model.** Character-level decoder-only Transformer (`TinyGPT`): token + positional embeddings, 3 pre-norm layers used causally, d_model=64, 4 heads, GELU MLPs, ≈300K parameters, trained entirely from random initialization (no pretraining, no external data).

**Training.** AdamW, lr=3e-3, batch size 64, 1500 steps, teacher-forced cross-entropy, fresh random examples each batch. Training was deliberately kept short so the model is *informatively* wrong on a meaningful fraction of problems.

**Signals compared.** For each held-out problem: (1) **token entropy** — mean per-step Shannon entropy of the greedy decode's softmax; (2) **semantic entropy** — draw K samples at temperature 0.9, cluster by exact parsed-integer equality, compute Shannon entropy of cluster frequencies; (3) **self-consistency** — fraction of the K samples agreeing with the greedy answer. All three are computed from the same generations per problem. K=10 throughout except the new K-sweep (Section 3), which also reports K=20.

**Evaluation.** Each signal scores predicted correctness of the greedy answer (label 1 = correct); we report AUROC (Mann–Whitney U, ties at 0.5).

**Statistical robustness.** Round-1 and round-2 reviewers (unanimously) flagged that a single run cannot support claims about a 2–3 point AUROC gap, and that our original multi-seed sweep confounded initialization variance with eval-set variance. Round-3 reviewers (three of four) additionally flagged that per-seed AUROC point estimates have no within-seed variance estimate, and all four asked whether the K=10 sampling budget itself explains the seed-to-seed fragility. We now run five checks: (1) **paired bootstrap** on the seed-0 run (5000 resamples); (2) **multi-seed sweep, independent eval per seed** (5 seeds, fresh 400-problem draw each); (3) **multi-seed sweep, fixed eval set** (one 400-problem set, seed 999, reused across 5 training seeds, isolating init/training-stream variance); (4) **K-sweep (new)** — for the same 5 fixed-eval-set seeds, draw 20 samples per problem instead of 10 and recompute AUROC at K=10 (first 10 draws) and K=20 (all 20); (5) **per-seed bootstrap (new)** — within each seed's 400-problem eval set, resample with replacement 2000 times and compute a CI on each signal's AUROC gap at K=20. Code for all five analyses (`bootstrap_original.py`, `multiseed_experiment.py`, `fixed_eval_experiment.py`, `k_sweep_and_seed_bootstrap.py`) is included.

## 3. Results

**Original run (seed 0).** Greedy accuracy 58.5% (234/400).

| Signal | AUROC |
|---|---|
| Token-level predictive entropy | 0.646 |
| Semantic entropy | 0.668 |
| Self-consistency | 0.673 |

Point estimates replicate the qualitative ordering from Kuhn et al. (2023) and Farquhar et al. (2024): both sampling-based signals exceed token entropy, by 2.2 and 2.7 AUROC points respectively.

**Paired bootstrap (5000 resamples, seed-0 run).** All three 95% CIs straddle zero (semantic − token: [−0.036, +0.076]; self-consistency − token: [−0.029, +0.082]; self-consistency − semantic: [−0.028, +0.040]), though the resample distributions lean positive (76–83% of resamples favor the sampling-based signal). None of the three pairwise gaps is significant on this single run.

**Multi-seed sweeps (5 seeds each).** Independent-eval sweep: token entropy wins outright in seeds 0 and 1. Fixed-eval sweep (same 400 problems, drawn once at seed 999, reused across all 5 training seeds): token entropy wins in seeds 2 and 3.

| Seed | Correct/Incorrect | Token entropy | Semantic entropy | Self-consistency |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 159/241 | 0.663 | **0.680** | 0.670 |
| 1 | 287/113 | 0.801 | 0.821 | **0.836** |
| 2 | 63/337 | **0.607** | 0.544 | 0.577 |
| 3 | 314/86 | **0.644** | 0.584 | 0.634 |
| 4 | 323/77 | 0.730 | **0.762** | 0.772 |

Both sweeps show a 2/5 flip rate, but on *different* seeds — this rules out eval-set redraw as the sole source of instability (the fixed-eval sweep alone already shows 2/5 flips with only init/training-stream variance present), but the matching *rate* across sweeps should be read as a coincidence of n=5, not as independent replication of which seeds flip.

**K-sweep, K=10 → K=20 (new).** For the same 5 fixed-eval-set seeds, we drew 20 samples per problem and recomputed AUROC using only the first 10 (reproducing the table above up to a different sampling stream) versus all 20:

| Seed | Token entropy | Semantic entropy (K=10→20) | Self-consistency (K=10→20) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0.663 | 0.687 → 0.692 | 0.700 → 0.711 |
| 1 | 0.801 | 0.800 → 0.817 | 0.826 → 0.831 |
| 2 | **0.607** | 0.604 → 0.565 | 0.497 → 0.537 |
| 3 | **0.644** | 0.594 → 0.566 | 0.640 → 0.627 |
| 4 | 0.730 | 0.733 → 0.768 | 0.768 → 0.808 |

Doubling K sharpens most gaps (as expected — less finite-sample noise in the cluster/vote estimates) but does not change which signal wins in any seed: token entropy still wins outright in seeds 2 and 3 at K=20, and by a larger margin than at K=10 in seed 3. This is evidence *against* the hypothesis (raised independently by all four round-3 reviewers) that the flips are simply K=10 undersampling noise in the sampling-based estimators — at least up to K=20, more samples make the sampling-based signals more stable, not more competitive, on the seeds where they lose.

**Per-seed bootstrap CIs (new, K=20, 2000 resamples within each seed's 400 problems).** Semantic entropy − token entropy gap:

| Seed | 95% CI | Significant? |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | [−0.007, +0.065] | No |
| 1 | [−0.024, +0.055] | No |
| 2 | [−0.155, +0.068] | No |
| 3 | **[−0.141, −0.013]** | **Yes — token entropy wins** |
| 4 | [−0.008, +0.083] | No |

Four of five seeds' gaps are indistinguishable from noise even at K=20, confirming most of the apparent seed-to-seed variation is within the range sampling noise can produce at n=400. But seed 3 is an exception: its CI excludes zero entirely, meaning token entropy is significantly *better* than semantic entropy on that model/eval combination, not merely tied. This directly answers the round-3 reviewers' shared request — a genuine, statistically supported reversal exists in this small sweep, not just an unresolved-noise story. Self-consistency − token-entropy CIs are wider and cross zero in all 5 seeds (full table in `k_sweep_and_seed_bootstrap_results.json`).

**Revised interpretation.** Point estimates on the original run replicate the literature's ordering, and the trend across seeds leans the same direction, but four independent analyses — paired bootstrap, two seed-sweep designs, and now a K-sweep plus per-seed bootstrap — converge on the same qualified picture: the ordering is a real, weak, seed-sensitive tendency, robust to eval-set redraw and to doubling K, but not something that holds in every model instance we trained. Seed 3 shows this is not merely "insufficient power to detect a positive effect" — it is a genuine, significant case where the literature's claimed direction is wrong.

**Cost.** Token entropy costs 1 generation; semantic entropy and self-consistency cost K+1 generations (11x at K=10, 21x at K=20), consistent with the 5–10x figure reported for semantic entropy on larger models. One K=10 run (train + 400-problem eval) takes ≈75–90s on CPU; the K=20 sweep with per-seed bootstrap over 5 seeds took 563.5s; combined with the earlier bootstrap and both multi-seed sweeps, total robustness-analysis compute is under 20 minutes on CPU.

## 4. Conclusion

We provide a controlled, from-scratch replication of the semantic-entropy-vs-token-entropy comparison, removing pretraining and entailment-clustering confounds present in prior large-model studies. Beyond a single-run comparison, we quantify how much to trust the result five ways: a paired bootstrap shows none of the three pairwise AUROC gaps is significant at 95% on the original run; a 5-seed sweep with independent eval draws and a second with a fixed eval set both show the ordering flips in 2/5 seeds (on different seeds, so this match is not itself evidence of a replicated phenomenon); a K=10→K=20 sweep shows the flips are not resolved by more samples; and per-seed bootstrap CIs show that while most seed-level "flips" are statistically indistinguishable from noise, one seed's reversal is significant. The honest summary is that sampling-based signals show a real but weak and seed-sensitive advantage over single-pass token entropy in this setting — robust to eval-set redraw and moderate increases in K, but not universal — and that comparisons of these signals should routinely report seed and per-seed variance, including at larger scale.

**Limitations.** Still a single small model architecture and a single synthetic task; only 5 seeds per sweep (enough to show the ordering is fragile and to find one significant reversal, not enough to precisely characterize the flip rate's distribution). K was only checked up to 20 and temperature was not varied; it remains possible that much larger K or different temperatures shift the picture further, though our K=10→20 result suggests the direction of each seed's ordering is already stable by K=20. The task's small, exactly-clusterable answer space still does not directly generalize to open-ended NLG, where clustering itself is a hard sub-problem. We did not evaluate cheaper single-pass approximations (e.g., Semantic Entropy Probes) or SelfCheckGPT-style multi-sample-consistency variants beyond our self-consistency baseline.

**Future work.** Extend the K-sweep further (e.g., K=50–100) to check whether seed 3's reversal persists or shrinks; extend the seed sweep (more seeds, both fixed- and variable-eval) to get a real interval on the flip rate itself rather than a single point at n=5; investigate whether seed 3's significant reversal correlates with an observable model property (e.g., degenerate or overconfident sample distributions) or looks like unstructured noise; test whether a linear probe on hidden states recovers most of the sampling-based signals' (weak) advantage at 1x inference cost.
