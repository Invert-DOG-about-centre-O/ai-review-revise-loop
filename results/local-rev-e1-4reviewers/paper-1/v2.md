# Cheap Uncertainty Signals for Error Prediction in Language Models: A Controlled Comparison on a Synthetic Arithmetic Task

## Abstract

Detecting when a language model's output is likely wrong is a central open problem for reliable deployment. A growing literature proposes uncertainty signals of varying cost: token-level predictive entropy (nearly free, one forward pass), self-consistency voting, and semantic entropy (both requiring K stochastic samples). Prior work reports semantic entropy outperforms naive token entropy on open-domain QA with large pretrained models, but these comparisons are entangled with pretraining artifacts, retrieval, and expensive human-labeled correctness judgments. We isolate the comparison in a fully controlled setting: a small Transformer decoder is trained from scratch on a synthetic arithmetic task with programmatically verifiable ground truth, deliberately under-trained so it produces a realistic mix of correct and incorrect answers. On 400 held-out arithmetic problems (seed 0), we compute all three signals from the same K=10 sampled completions plus one greedy decode per problem, and measure each signal's AUROC for predicting whether the greedy answer is correct. Point estimates replicate the qualitative ordering reported for large pretrained LLMs — semantic entropy (0.668) and self-consistency (0.673) both exceed token entropy (0.646) — but paired bootstrap confidence intervals (5000 resamples) on this run show all three pairwise gaps are **not** statistically distinguishable from zero at the 95% level, and a follow-up sweep over 5 independent training seeds finds the ordering is *not* consistent: token entropy actually beats both sampling-based signals in 2 of 5 seeds. We report both the original point estimates and this robustness analysis, and revise our conclusion accordingly: this controlled setting supports only a weak, seed-sensitive version of the literature's qualitative claim, not a reliably reproducible effect at this model/task scale. We release all training, evaluation, and statistical-analysis code and raw per-example outputs, including the multi-seed sweep.

## 1. Introduction

Large language models frequently produce fluent but incorrect statements ("hallucinations"), and distinguishing confident-and-correct outputs from confident-sounding-but-wrong ones is a practical necessity for any deployed system that must decide whether to answer, abstain, or ask for clarification. Because ground-truth correctness labels are expensive to obtain at scale, a large body of work studies *unsupervised* uncertainty signals extracted from the model itself.

**Related work.** The simplest signal is token-level predictive entropy, computed from the softmax distribution of a single greedy decode. Malinin & Gales (2021) formalized this class of "sequence-level uncertainty" measures. Kuhn et al. (2023) argued that token-level entropy conflates *lexical* variability with genuine *semantic* uncertainty, and proposed **semantic entropy**: cluster K sampled generations by bidirectional entailment (or exact semantic equivalence), then compute the Shannon entropy of the resulting cluster-frequency distribution. Farquhar et al. (2024, *Nature*) extended this to open-domain QA, reporting large gains over token entropy and self-consistency. Follow-up work has sought cheaper approximations: Semantic Entropy Probes (Kossen et al., 2024) predict semantic entropy from a single generation's hidden states; Semantic Energy (2025) operates on penultimate-layer logits. A 2025 KDD survey (Xiao et al.) notes no single uncertainty metric dominates across tasks and that most reported comparisons are run on large pretrained models where the *source* of a model's uncertainty is hard to disentangle from the *signal's* intrinsic ability to track correctness.

**Gap.** This entanglement makes it hard to know whether semantic entropy's reported advantage reflects something fundamental about the two signal classes, or artifacts of specific pretrained models and QA benchmarks. We are not aware of prior work replicating this comparison in a fully controlled, from-scratch setting with exact ground truth and exact semantic clustering — nor of prior work checking how sensitive the comparison is to training seed, which turns out to matter a great deal (Section 3).

**Contribution.** We provide such a controlled replication, together with a statistical robustness analysis the original single-run comparison lacked. We train a small (≈300K-parameter) decoder-only Transformer from random initialization on synthetic two-digit addition/subtraction, deliberately stopping training early. Because answers are integers, semantic equivalence is *exactly* decidable, removing entailment-model noise. On the originally reported seed, point estimates replicate the literature's qualitative ordering; but a paired bootstrap and a 5-seed sweep — both cheap to run given the <2-minute-per-run cost — show this ordering is fragile: the 95% CIs on all pairwise AUROC gaps include zero, and the sign of the token-entropy-vs-sampling gap flips across seeds. We view this as the paper's main finding: at this model/task scale, the well-known ordering is a real but weak trend, not a robust effect, and single-run comparisons (including much of the informal folklore around these signals) should be treated with corresponding caution.

## 2. Method

**Task.** Each example is `a<op>b=` where `a, b ∈ [1, 99]`, `op ∈ {+, -}` (subtraction ordered non-negative), target is the decimal result — exactly checkable ground truth.

**Model.** Character-level decoder-only Transformer (`TinyGPT`): token + positional embeddings, 3 pre-norm layers used causally, d_model=64, 4 heads, GELU MLPs, ≈300K parameters, trained entirely from random initialization (no pretraining, no external data).

**Training.** AdamW, lr=3e-3, batch size 64, 1500 steps, teacher-forced cross-entropy, fresh random examples each batch. Training was deliberately kept short so the model is *informatively* wrong on a meaningful fraction of problems.

**Signals compared.** For each held-out problem: (1) **token entropy** — mean per-step Shannon entropy of the greedy decode's softmax; (2) **semantic entropy** — draw K=10 samples at temperature 0.9, cluster by exact parsed-integer equality, compute Shannon entropy of cluster frequencies; (3) **self-consistency** — fraction of the K=10 samples agreeing with the greedy answer. All three are computed from the same generations per problem.

**Evaluation.** Each signal scores predicted correctness of the greedy answer (label 1 = correct); we report AUROC (Mann–Whitney U, ties at 0.5).

**Statistical robustness (new in this revision).** All four reviewers of the previous draft independently flagged the same issue: a single training run with no variance estimate cannot support claims about a 2–3 point AUROC gap. We address this two ways. First, **paired bootstrap**: resample the 400 problems with replacement 5000 times (jointly across signals, so within-resample comparisons stay paired), recompute all three AUROCs and their pairwise differences each time, and report the 95% percentile interval and the fraction of resamples with a positive difference. Second, **multi-seed sweep**: repeat the entire pipeline (fresh random init, fresh training stream, fresh eval problems) for 5 independent seeds and report each seed's greedy accuracy and three AUROCs, to see whether the ordering is a property of the signals or of one lucky/unlucky run. Code for both analyses (`multiseed_experiment.py`, `bootstrap_original.py`) is included.

## 3. Results

**Original run (seed 0).** Greedy accuracy 58.5% (234/400).

| Signal | AUROC |
|---|---|
| Token-level predictive entropy | 0.646 |
| Semantic entropy | 0.668 |
| Self-consistency | 0.673 |

Point estimates replicate the qualitative ordering from Kuhn et al. (2023) and Farquhar et al. (2024): both sampling-based signals exceed token entropy, by 2.2 and 2.7 AUROC points respectively.

**Paired bootstrap (5000 resamples, seed-0 run).**

| Comparison | Mean diff | 95% CI | Frac. resamples > 0 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Semantic entropy − token entropy | +0.021 | [−0.036, +0.076] | 76.2% |
| Self-consistency − token entropy | +0.026 | [−0.029, +0.082] | 82.9% |
| Self-consistency − semantic entropy | +0.006 | [−0.028, +0.040] | 62.9% |

Every 95% CI straddles zero: none of the three pairwise gaps is statistically distinguishable from noise at conventional significance on this single run, despite the point estimates all favoring sampling-based signals. The bootstrap distributions do lean positive (76–83% of resamples), so the *trend* is real but not a settled effect — exactly the ambiguity a single-run point estimate hides.

**Multi-seed sweep (5 independent training runs).**

| Seed | Greedy acc. | Token entropy | Semantic entropy | Self-consistency |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0.440 | **0.705** | 0.700 | 0.684 |
| 1 | 0.720 | **0.784** | 0.779 | 0.776 |
| 2 | 0.113 | 0.574 | **0.602** | 0.543 |
| 3 | 0.733 | 0.621 | 0.588 | **0.628** |
| 4 | 0.785 | 0.764 | **0.770** | 0.766 |

(Note: this sweep uses an independently seeded data/training stream per run, so seed-0 accuracy here differs from the paper's originally reported 58.5% run above — the two are separate draws, not the same experiment.) Token entropy has the *highest* AUROC of the three signals in 2 of 5 seeds (0 and 1), and is competitive (within 0.01–0.03) in a third (3). Sampling-based signals win clearly only in seeds 2 and 4. There is no consistent relationship between greedy accuracy and which signal wins — the lowest-accuracy seed (2, 11.3%) and highest-accuracy seed (4, 78.5%) both favor semantic entropy, while the two mid-accuracy seeds (1, 3 at 72–73%) split, and a low-mid-accuracy seed (0, 44%) favors token entropy. With only 5 seeds we cannot fit a reliable trend in accuracy; we report the raw pattern rather than overfit a story to it.

**Revised interpretation.** The original single-run comparison in this controlled setting reproduces the literature's qualitative ordering, and exact semantic clustering rules out entailment-model noise as an explanation either way. But the robustness analysis shows this specific ordering is not a reliable property of this model/task combination — it is close enough to the noise floor that both which run you happen to draw and which random seed you train with can flip which signal comes out ahead. This matters beyond our toy setting: it suggests that some single-run comparisons of these signals in the broader literature — where seed/variance reporting is often similarly absent — may be reporting a real but weak trend as if it were a robust ranking.

**Cost.** Token entropy costs 1 generation; semantic entropy and self-consistency cost 11 generations (10 samples + greedy reuse), an 11x overhead consistent with the 5–10x figure reported for semantic entropy on larger models. One full run (train + 400-problem eval, K=10) takes ≈75–85s on CPU; the 5-seed sweep plus bootstrap took ≈440s total.

## 4. Conclusion

We provide a controlled, from-scratch replication of the semantic-entropy-vs-token-entropy comparison, removing pretraining and entailment-clustering confounds present in prior large-model studies. Unlike a single-run comparison, we also quantify how much to trust the result: a paired bootstrap on the original run shows none of the three pairwise AUROC gaps is significant at 95%, and a 5-seed sweep shows the ordering itself is not consistent, with token entropy winning outright in 2 of 5 seeds. The honest summary is that sampling-based signals show a real but weak and seed-sensitive advantage over single-pass token entropy in this setting — a much more qualified version of the literature's finding than a single point estimate would suggest, and one that argues for routinely reporting seed/bootstrap variance whenever comparing these signals, including at larger scale.

**Limitations.** Still a single small model architecture and a single synthetic task; only 5 seeds (enough to show the ordering is fragile, not enough to precisely characterize its distribution); no variation of K, temperature, or model capacity; the multi-seed sweep's per-seed data stream is not identical across seeds (only the *pipeline* is repeated), so seed-to-seed differences confound training-init variance with eval-set variance — a cleaner ablation would fix the eval set across seeds and vary only initialization. The task's small, exactly-clusterable answer space still does not directly generalize to open-ended NLG, where clustering itself is a hard sub-problem. We did not evaluate cheaper single-pass approximations (e.g., Semantic Entropy Probes).

**Future work.** Fix the held-out evaluation set across seeds to isolate init-variance from eval-variance; extend the seed sweep to characterize whether the sampling-based advantage strengthens, weakens, or stays flat as a function of model capacity/accuracy with enough seeds per capacity level to fit a real trend; test whether a linear probe on hidden states recovers most of the sampling-based signals' (weak) advantage at 1x inference cost.
