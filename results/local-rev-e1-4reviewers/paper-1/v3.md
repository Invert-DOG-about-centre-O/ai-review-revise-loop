# Cheap Uncertainty Signals for Error Prediction in Language Models: A Controlled Comparison on a Synthetic Arithmetic Task

## Abstract

Detecting when a language model's output is likely wrong is a central open problem for reliable deployment. A growing literature proposes uncertainty signals of varying cost: token-level predictive entropy (nearly free, one forward pass), self-consistency voting, and semantic entropy (both requiring K stochastic samples). Prior work reports semantic entropy outperforms naive token entropy on open-domain QA with large pretrained models, but these comparisons are entangled with pretraining artifacts, retrieval, and expensive human-labeled correctness judgments. We isolate the comparison in a fully controlled setting: a small Transformer decoder is trained from scratch on a synthetic arithmetic task with programmatically verifiable ground truth, deliberately under-trained so it produces a realistic mix of correct and incorrect answers. On 400 held-out arithmetic problems (seed 0), we compute all three signals from the same K=10 sampled completions plus one greedy decode per problem, and measure each signal's AUROC for predicting whether the greedy answer is correct. Point estimates replicate the qualitative ordering reported for large pretrained LLMs — semantic entropy (0.668) and self-consistency (0.673) both exceed token entropy (0.646) — but paired bootstrap confidence intervals (5000 resamples) on this run show all three pairwise gaps are **not** statistically distinguishable from zero at the 95% level. A 5-seed sweep finds the ordering is *not* consistent (token entropy wins in 2/5 seeds), and — new in this revision — we re-run that sweep holding the 400-problem evaluation set fixed across seeds so only model initialization varies: the same 2/5 flip rate persists, ruling out eval-set redraw as the source of the instability. We revise our conclusion accordingly: this controlled setting supports only a weak, seed-sensitive version of the literature's qualitative claim, not a reliably reproducible effect at this model/task scale. We release all training, evaluation, and statistical-analysis code and raw per-example outputs, including both seed sweeps.

## 1. Introduction

Large language models frequently produce fluent but incorrect statements ("hallucinations"), and distinguishing confident-and-correct outputs from confident-sounding-but-wrong ones is a practical necessity for any deployed system that must decide whether to answer, abstain, or ask for clarification. Because ground-truth correctness labels are expensive to obtain at scale, a large body of work studies *unsupervised* uncertainty signals extracted from the model itself.

**Related work.** The simplest signal is token-level predictive entropy, computed from the softmax distribution of a single greedy decode. Malinin & Gales (2021) formalized this class of "sequence-level uncertainty" measures. Kuhn et al. (2023) argued that token-level entropy conflates *lexical* variability with genuine *semantic* uncertainty, and proposed **semantic entropy**: cluster K sampled generations by bidirectional entailment (or exact semantic equivalence), then compute the Shannon entropy of the resulting cluster-frequency distribution. Farquhar et al. (2024, *Nature*) extended this to open-domain QA, reporting large gains over token entropy and self-consistency. Manakul et al. (2023) proposed SelfCheckGPT, a closely related sampling-consistency signal for reference-free hallucination detection — the self-consistency baseline we compare against is conceptually in this family. Follow-up work has sought cheaper approximations: Semantic Entropy Probes (Kossen et al., 2024) predict semantic entropy from a single generation's hidden states; Semantic Energy (2025) operates on penultimate-layer logits. A 2025 KDD survey (Xiao et al.) notes no single uncertainty metric dominates across tasks and that most reported comparisons are run on large pretrained models where the *source* of a model's uncertainty is hard to disentangle from the *signal's* intrinsic ability to track correctness.

**Gap.** This entanglement makes it hard to know whether semantic entropy's reported advantage reflects something fundamental about the two signal classes, or artifacts of specific pretrained models and QA benchmarks. We are not aware of prior work replicating this comparison in a fully controlled, from-scratch setting with exact ground truth and exact semantic clustering — nor of prior work checking how sensitive the comparison is to training seed and eval-set draw, which turns out to matter a great deal (Section 3).

**Contribution.** We provide such a controlled replication, together with a statistical robustness analysis the original single-run comparison lacked. We train a small (≈300K-parameter) decoder-only Transformer from random initialization on synthetic two-digit addition/subtraction, deliberately stopping training early. Because answers are integers, semantic equivalence is *exactly* decidable, removing entailment-model noise. On the originally reported seed, point estimates replicate the literature's qualitative ordering; but a paired bootstrap and two 5-seed sweeps — one with a fresh eval draw per seed, one with a fixed eval set isolating pure init variance — show this ordering is fragile under both designs. We view this as the paper's main finding: at this model/task scale, the well-known ordering is a real but weak trend, not a robust effect, and single-run comparisons should be treated with corresponding caution.

## 2. Method

**Task.** Each example is `a<op>b=` where `a, b ∈ [1, 99]`, `op ∈ {+, -}` (subtraction ordered non-negative), target is the decimal result — exactly checkable ground truth.

**Model.** Character-level decoder-only Transformer (`TinyGPT`): token + positional embeddings, 3 pre-norm layers used causally, d_model=64, 4 heads, GELU MLPs, ≈300K parameters, trained entirely from random initialization (no pretraining, no external data).

**Training.** AdamW, lr=3e-3, batch size 64, 1500 steps, teacher-forced cross-entropy, fresh random examples each batch. Training was deliberately kept short so the model is *informatively* wrong on a meaningful fraction of problems.

**Signals compared.** For each held-out problem: (1) **token entropy** — mean per-step Shannon entropy of the greedy decode's softmax; (2) **semantic entropy** — draw K=10 samples at temperature 0.9, cluster by exact parsed-integer equality, compute Shannon entropy of cluster frequencies; (3) **self-consistency** — fraction of the K=10 samples agreeing with the greedy answer. All three are computed from the same generations per problem.

**Evaluation.** Each signal scores predicted correctness of the greedy answer (label 1 = correct); we report AUROC (Mann–Whitney U, ties at 0.5).

**Statistical robustness.** All four round-1 reviewers flagged that a single run with no variance estimate cannot support claims about a 2–3 point AUROC gap; round-2 reviewers (all four, independently) further flagged that our original 5-seed sweep confounded initialization variance with eval-set variance, since each seed redrew its own 400 eval problems. We now run three checks: (1) **paired bootstrap** on the seed-0 run — resample 400 problems with replacement 5000 times (jointly across signals), recompute AUROC differences each time; (2) **multi-seed sweep, independent eval per seed** (as before) — repeat the full pipeline for 5 seeds; (3) **multi-seed sweep, fixed eval set (new)** — draw one 400-problem eval set once (seed 999) and reuse it unchanged for all 5 training seeds, so only model initialization and the training stream vary. We also now report per-seed correct/incorrect counts so readers can judge how much low-accuracy seeds' AUROC estimates are limited by minority-class sample size. Code for all three analyses (`multiseed_experiment.py`, `bootstrap_original.py`, `fixed_eval_experiment.py`) is included.

## 3. Results

**Original run (seed 0).** Greedy accuracy 58.5% (234/400).

| Signal | AUROC |
|---|---|
| Token-level predictive entropy | 0.646 |
| Semantic entropy | 0.668 |
| Self-consistency | 0.673 |

Point estimates replicate the qualitative ordering from Kuhn et al. (2023) and Farquhar et al. (2024): both sampling-based signals exceed token entropy, by 2.2 and 2.7 AUROC points respectively.

**Paired bootstrap (5000 resamples, seed-0 run).**

| Comparison | Mean diff | 95% CI | Frac. resamples > 0 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Semantic entropy − token entropy | +0.021 | [−0.036, +0.076] | 76.2% |
| Self-consistency − token entropy | +0.026 | [−0.029, +0.082] | 82.9% |
| Self-consistency − semantic entropy | +0.006 | [−0.028, +0.040] | 62.9% |

Every 95% CI straddles zero: none of the three pairwise gaps is statistically distinguishable from noise on this single run, despite point estimates favoring sampling-based signals. The bootstrap distributions lean positive (76–83%), so the *trend* is real but not settled.

**Multi-seed sweep, independent eval per seed.** Token entropy has the highest AUROC in seeds 0 and 1, and is competitive in seed 3; sampling-based signals win clearly only in seeds 2 and 4 (full table in the released `multiseed_results.json`, unchanged from the prior revision).

**Multi-seed sweep, fixed eval set (new).** Same 400 problems (drawn once, seed 999) evaluated for all 5 training seeds, isolating init/training-stream variance from eval-draw variance:

| Seed | Correct/Incorrect | Greedy acc. | Token entropy | Semantic entropy | Self-consistency |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 159/241 | 0.398 | 0.663 | **0.680** | 0.670 |
| 1 | 287/113 | 0.718 | 0.801 | 0.821 | **0.836** |
| 2 | 63/337 | 0.158 | **0.607** | 0.544 | 0.577 |
| 3 | 314/86 | 0.785 | **0.644** | 0.584 | 0.634 |
| 4 | 323/77 | 0.808 | 0.730 | **0.762** | 0.772 |

Token entropy still wins outright in 2 of 5 seeds (2 and 3) — the same flip rate as the independent-eval sweep — even with the eval set held fixed. This directly answers the round-2 reviewers' shared request: the seed-sensitivity finding is not an artifact of redrawing the eval set each seed; it survives when only initialization/training-stream variance is present. Seeds 2 and 3 also have the most imbalanced label splits (63 and 86 minority-class examples respectively), which the per-seed counts above make explicit — AUROC estimates on those seeds carry wider sampling noise than the roughly-balanced seeds (0, 1), a caveat readers should weigh alongside the point estimates.

**Revised interpretation.** The original single-run comparison reproduces the literature's qualitative ordering, and exact semantic clustering rules out entailment-model noise as an explanation either way. But two independent seed-sweep designs — one redrawing the eval set per seed, one holding it fixed — both show the ordering is not a reliable property of this model/task combination, and give the same 2/5 flip rate. This strengthens rather than weakens the original finding: the fragility is attributable to model/training variance, not eval-set noise. It suggests some single-run comparisons of these signals in the broader literature, where seed/variance reporting is often absent, may be reporting a real but weak trend as if it were a robust ranking.

**Cost.** Token entropy costs 1 generation; semantic entropy and self-consistency cost 11 generations (10 samples + greedy reuse), an 11x overhead consistent with the 5–10x figure reported for semantic entropy on larger models. One run (train + 400-problem eval, K=10) takes ≈75–90s on CPU; the fixed-eval 5-seed sweep took 435s total; combined with the earlier bootstrap and independent-eval sweep, total robustness-analysis compute is under 15 minutes on CPU.

## 4. Conclusion

We provide a controlled, from-scratch replication of the semantic-entropy-vs-token-entropy comparison, removing pretraining and entailment-clustering confounds present in prior large-model studies. Beyond a single-run comparison, we quantify how much to trust the result three ways: a paired bootstrap shows none of the three pairwise AUROC gaps is significant at 95%; a 5-seed sweep with independent eval draws shows the ordering flips in 2/5 seeds; and a second 5-seed sweep with a fixed eval set — added in this revision at all four round-2 reviewers' request — shows the identical 2/5 flip rate, confirming the instability comes from model/training variance rather than eval-set redraw. The honest summary is that sampling-based signals show a real but weak and seed-sensitive advantage over single-pass token entropy in this setting, and that this advantage is robust to at least one plausible confound (eval-set variance) while remaining sensitive to another (initialization/training variance) — a qualified version of the literature's finding that argues for routinely reporting seed/bootstrap variance whenever comparing these signals, including at larger scale.

**Limitations.** Still a single small model architecture and a single synthetic task; only 5 seeds per sweep (enough to show the ordering is fragile, not enough to precisely characterize its distribution or to reliably attribute per-seed AUROC swings to specific label-imbalance levels); no variation of K or temperature — since self-consistency and semantic entropy both derive from the same K stochastic draws, their estimators may simply be noisier at K=10, and we have not checked whether the ordering stabilizes at larger K or lower temperature. The task's small, exactly-clusterable answer space still does not directly generalize to open-ended NLG, where clustering itself is a hard sub-problem. We did not evaluate cheaper single-pass approximations (e.g., Semantic Entropy Probes) or SelfCheckGPT-style multi-sample-consistency variants beyond our self-consistency baseline.

**Future work.** Sweep K and temperature to test whether sampling-based signals' apparent fragility is an artifact of undersampling; extend the seed sweep (more seeds, both fixed- and variable-eval) to characterize whether the sampling-based advantage strengthens, weakens, or stays flat as a function of model capacity/accuracy; test whether a linear probe on hidden states recovers most of the sampling-based signals' (weak) advantage at 1x inference cost.
