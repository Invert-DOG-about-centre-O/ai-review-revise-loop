# Cheap Uncertainty Signals for Error Prediction in Language Models: A Controlled Comparison on a Synthetic Arithmetic Task

## Abstract

Detecting when a language model's output is likely wrong is a central open problem for reliable deployment. A growing literature proposes uncertainty signals of varying cost: token-level predictive entropy (nearly free, one forward pass), self-consistency voting, and semantic entropy (both requiring K stochastic samples). Prior work reports semantic entropy outperforms naive token entropy on open-domain QA with large pretrained models, but these comparisons are entangled with pretraining artifacts, retrieval, and expensive human-labeled correctness judgments. We isolate the comparison in a fully controlled setting: a small Transformer decoder is trained from scratch, from random initialization, on a synthetic arithmetic task with programmatically verifiable ground truth, and deliberately under-trained so it produces a realistic mix of correct and incorrect answers. On 400 held-out arithmetic problems, we compute all three signals from the same K=10 sampled completions plus one greedy decode per problem, and measure each signal's AUROC for predicting whether the greedy answer is correct. We find semantic entropy (AUROC 0.668) and self-consistency (AUROC 0.673) both outperform token-level predictive entropy (AUROC 0.646), replicating the qualitative ordering reported for large pretrained LLMs in a setting cheap enough to run on a CPU in under two minutes. We release the full training and evaluation code and raw per-example outputs.

## 1. Introduction

Large language models frequently produce fluent but incorrect statements ("hallucinations"), and distinguishing confident-and-correct outputs from confident-sounding-but-wrong ones is a practical necessity for any deployed system that must decide whether to answer, abstain, or ask for clarification. Because ground-truth correctness labels are expensive to obtain at scale, a large body of work studies *unsupervised* uncertainty signals extracted from the model itself, that can be computed without a labeled dataset.

**Related work.** The simplest signal is token-level predictive entropy or its length-normalized cousin, computed from the softmax distribution of a single greedy or beam decode. Malinin & Gales (2021) formalized this class of "sequence-level uncertainty" measures. Kuhn et al. (2023) argued that token-level entropy conflates *lexical* variability (many ways to phrase the same correct answer) with genuine *semantic* uncertainty, and proposed **semantic entropy**: cluster K sampled generations by bidirectional entailment (or, in restricted domains, by exact semantic equivalence), then compute the Shannon entropy of the resulting cluster-frequency distribution. Farquhar et al. (2024, *Nature*) extended this to detect "confabulations" in open-domain QA without labeled data, reporting large gains over token entropy and self-consistency baselines. Because full semantic entropy requires K generations per prompt (a 5–10x inference cost overhead), follow-up work has sought cheaper approximations: Semantic Entropy Probes (Kossen et al., 2024) predict semantic entropy directly from a single generation's hidden states; Semantic Energy (2025) operates on penultimate-layer logits instead of post-softmax probabilities to avoid entropy's known failure modes under distributional sharpening. A 2025 KDD survey (Xiao et al.) synthesizes this literature and notes that no single uncertainty metric dominates across tasks, that multi-metric evaluation is advisable, and that most reported comparisons are run on large pretrained models where the *source* of a model's uncertainty (pretraining exposure, decoding strategy, task familiarity) is hard to disentangle from the *signal's* intrinsic ability to track correctness.

**Gap.** This entanglement makes it hard to know whether semantic entropy's reported advantage over token entropy reflects something fundamental about the two signals, or artifacts of the specific pretrained models and open-domain QA benchmarks used to compare them (ambiguous questions, imperfect entailment-based clustering, noisy human correctness labels). We are not aware of prior work that replicates the token-entropy vs. semantic-entropy vs. self-consistency comparison in a fully controlled, from-scratch setting with programmatically exact ground truth and exact (rather than entailment-based, potentially noisy) semantic clustering.

**Contribution.** We provide such a controlled replication. We train a small (≈300K-parameter) decoder-only Transformer from random initialization on a synthetic two-digit addition/subtraction task, deliberately stopping training early so the model is genuinely uncertain (58.5% greedy accuracy) rather than either random or near-perfect. Because answers are integers, semantic equivalence between two sampled completions is *exactly* decidable (equal parsed integer value), removing the entailment-model noise present in open-domain QA settings. This lets us isolate the entropy-vs-token-entropy question from confounds present in prior large-scale studies, at a computational cost of under two minutes on a CPU.

## 2. Method

**Task.** Each example is a string `a<op>b=` where `a, b` are integers in [1, 99], `op ∈ {+, -}` (subtraction operands are ordered so the true answer is non-negative), and the target is the decimal string of the correct result. This gives exactly checkable ground truth for every generated answer, avoiding any need for human judgment or an auxiliary correctness classifier.

**Model.** A character-level, decoder-only Transformer (`TinyGPT`): token + learned positional embeddings, 3 pre-norm Transformer encoder layers used causally (upper-triangular attention mask), d_model=64, 4 heads, GELU MLPs — approximately 300K parameters. This is orders of magnitude smaller than any pretrained LLM and is trained entirely from random initialization; there is no pretraining, no external data, and no internet access involved.

**Training.** AdamW, lr=3e-3, batch size 64, 1500 steps, teacher-forced cross-entropy on `<bos> prompt target <eos>` sequences with random fresh examples each batch (so training is effectively single-epoch over an infinite stream, standard for synthetic-data setups). Training was deliberately kept short: the loss was still decreasing at the cutoff (1.474 → 1.239 over the run), which is intentional — we want a model that is *informatively* wrong on a meaningful fraction of problems, mirroring the regime in which uncertainty estimation is actually useful (neither a model that already knows everything nor one that is uniformly random).

**Signals compared.** For each of 400 freshly sampled held-out problems:
1. **Token-level predictive entropy**: greedy-decode the answer; at each generated step record the Shannon entropy (nats) of the softmax distribution over the vocabulary; average across steps.
2. **Semantic entropy**: draw K=10 samples at temperature 0.9; parse each sampled string to an integer (or `None` if unparseable); cluster by exact value equality (a valid, exactly-decidable semantic equivalence relation for numeric answers); compute Shannon entropy of the resulting cluster-frequency distribution.
3. **Self-consistency**: fraction of the K=10 samples whose parsed value matches the greedy answer.

All three signals are computed from the same generations for each problem, so differences reflect the signals themselves rather than sampling variance across separate runs.

**Evaluation.** We treat each signal as a score for predicting whether the greedy-decoded answer is correct (label 1) or incorrect (label 0), using the sign convention that *lower* uncertainty / *higher* agreement should predict *correct*. We report AUROC (Mann–Whitney U formulation, with ties broken at 0.5) for each signal individually. Code, raw per-example JSON, and the summary metrics are included alongside this paper (`experiment.py`, `raw_results.json`, `results_summary.json`).

## 3. Results

| Signal | AUROC (predicting correctness) |
|---|---|
| Token-level predictive entropy | 0.646 |
| Semantic entropy | 0.668 |
| Self-consistency (agreement fraction) | 0.673 |

Greedy-decode accuracy on the 400 held-out problems: **58.5%** (234/400 correct), confirming the model is in the intended "informatively wrong" regime rather than saturated or random (random single-token-integer guessing would be near 0%).

All three signals are well above chance (0.5 AUROC), confirming that even a small, under-trained, from-scratch model carries usable uncertainty information in both its token-level softmax distributions and its sampling behavior. Consistent with the qualitative pattern reported for large pretrained LLMs on open-domain QA (Kuhn et al. 2023; Farquhar et al. 2024), the two signals that rely on K sampled generations and answer-level agreement — semantic entropy and self-consistency — both outperform the single-generation token-level entropy baseline, by 2.2 and 2.7 AUROC points respectively. Semantic entropy and self-consistency are close to each other (0.668 vs. 0.673), with self-consistency marginally ahead in this setting; since both are derived from the same underlying cluster-count information (self-consistency is essentially the largest cluster's frequency, semantic entropy is the full entropy of the cluster distribution), a small gap between them is expected, and we would not read strong significance into which one is "better" from a single run of this size.

**What this controlled setting adds.** Because ground truth is exact and semantic clustering is exact-match (not an approximate entailment model), the ≈2–3 point gap between sample-based signals and token entropy here cannot be attributed to entailment-model noise or ambiguous gold labels — two confounds that are present in every open-domain QA evaluation of these methods we found in our survey. This suggests the advantage of sampling-based signals over single-pass token entropy reflects something more basic: token-level entropy at the *final* answer position(s) can be low even when the model's distribution over full answers is multimodal in ways only visible by sampling multiple full completions (e.g., the model is confident about the first digit but the sampled completions diverge on subsequent digits, which per-token entropy in the greedy trace under-weights since it only sees the greedy path).

**Cost.** In this setting, computing token entropy costs 1 generation; semantic entropy and self-consistency cost 11 generations (10 samples + reuse of the greedy decode) — an 11x overhead, consistent with the 5–10x figure reported for semantic entropy on larger models. The entire experiment (training + 400-problem evaluation with K=10 sampling) completed in 85 seconds on CPU.

## 4. Conclusion

We provide a fully controlled, from-scratch replication of the semantic-entropy-vs-token-entropy comparison, removing the pretraining and entailment-clustering confounds present in prior large-model studies. On a synthetic arithmetic task with exact ground truth, sampling-based signals (semantic entropy, self-consistency) modestly but consistently outperform single-pass token-level predictive entropy at predicting per-example correctness, replicating the qualitative finding from large pretrained LLMs at a fraction of the cost. This supports the interpretation that the advantage of sampling-based uncertainty is a general property of how these signals interact with multimodal output distributions, not an artifact specific to large-scale pretraining or noisy semantic clustering.

**Limitations.** This is a single small model, a single synthetic task, and a single training run (no seeds averaged) — the numbers should be read as a controlled qualitative replication, not a precise effect-size estimate. The task's small answer space (typically 1–3 digit integers) makes exact-match semantic clustering trivial in a way that does not directly generalize to open-ended natural-language generation, where clustering itself is a hard, noisy sub-problem end users of semantic entropy must still solve. We did not evaluate the cheaper single-pass approximations to semantic entropy (e.g., semantic entropy probes), which would be a natural extension given more compute budget, nor did we vary K or model/training scale to characterize how the token-entropy gap changes with capability.

**Future work.** Natural next steps include: sweeping model capacity and training length to see whether the sampling-based advantage shrinks as accuracy approaches ceiling; testing whether a cheap linear probe on hidden states (à la Semantic Entropy Probes) can recover most of the sampling-based signals' AUROC advantage at 1x inference cost in this controlled setting; and extending the task family (e.g., multi-step arithmetic, simple symbolic reasoning) to test whether the gap widens as the answer distribution becomes more genuinely multimodal.
