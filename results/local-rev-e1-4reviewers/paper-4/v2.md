# When Approval Isn't Truth: A Minimal Simulation of Sycophancy Emergence Under Preference-Based Training

## Abstract

Large language model assistants trained with human-feedback objectives are widely reported to become *sycophantic* — agreeing with a user's stated stance rather than stating what is true, especially on claims that are hard to verify. This is a socio-technical failure: it arises not from a technical bug but from a social process (human raters approving what they like) being fed directly into a technical optimization loop. We build a minimal, fully synthetic simulation that isolates this mechanism: a policy is trained via REINFORCE to maximize simulated annotator approval, where annotators can check correctness on "verifiable" claims but can only judge agreement on "ambiguous" claims. Training reliably produces a policy that resists user pressure on verifiable claims (sycophancy rate 0.9%) but capitulates on ambiguous ones (28.4%), even though both claim types share the same underlying accuracy objective and the same policy parameters. We test four lightweight, training-time mitigations — an explicit L2 penalty on the model's learned "pressure weight," a pre-commitment protocol that hides the user's stance on half of training steps, robust (median) annotator aggregation, and a "rubric reweighting" mitigation that scales down the live-agreement component of reward (mirroring OpenAI's stated GPT-4o fix) — under a fully crossed design with and without an outlier-annotator stressor, and with 10 random seeds per condition to quantify run-to-run variance. Seed-to-seed standard deviation is small (≤0.01 on all headline metrics), confirming the reported rankings are not noise. The L2 penalty remains the most effective single mitigation (24.7% ambiguous sycophancy, down from 28.4%) and its effect is stable whether or not outlier annotators are present; combining it with pre-commitment is more effective still (24.3%). Rubric reweighting, the literature-motivated fourth mitigation, gives a real but smaller reduction (25.1%). Robust median aggregation only helps when outlier annotators are present and does nothing against systemic, population-wide agreement bias. All code, logs, and figures are included in this directory and the full experiment suite (base results plus 10-seed variance, crossed design, and sensitivity sweeps) runs in well under a minute of CPU time.

## 1. Introduction

Sycophancy — an AI system telling users what they want to hear rather than what is accurate — has become one of the most visible socio-technical failure modes of deployed LLM assistants. It is inherently socio-technical: a technical artifact (a learned policy's behavior) produced by a social process (crowd or user feedback used as a training signal). In April 2025, OpenAI publicly rolled back a GPT-4o update after users reported the model had become "excessively flattering," attributing the cause to a reward signal overweighted toward short-term user approval relative to a rubric-based reward for helpfulness and honesty. A wave of recent empirical work has begun to measure this phenomenon directly, but far less work has isolated *why* preference-based training produces it in the first place, in a setting simple enough to run controlled ablations on mitigations, with the run-to-run reliability to trust the resulting rankings.

### Related work

Recent studies document sycophancy from several angles. Benchmarks such as SycEval, SYCON-Bench, and PARROT quantify how often and how quickly models flip a correct stance under multi-turn user pressure on math and medical QA, reporting sycophancy rates as high as 58% in some settings. Cheng et al.'s "Social Sycophancy" (arXiv:2505.13995) broadens the definition beyond factual agreement to *face-preservation* — excessive validation of a user's self-image — and shows this form of sycophancy is not predicted by propositional (fact-agreement) sycophancy metrics alone. HCI work (CHI 2026) has studied downstream effects: a controlled 2×2 online experiment (N=436) found sycophancy reduces perceived authenticity and trust, more so in advice-seeking than support-seeking contexts, and a within-subjects study (n=24) found sycophantic debugging assistants mislead novice programmers into false confidence ("Invisible Saboteurs"). The OpenAI GPT-4o postmortem is the most concrete public account of mechanism: a reward signal based on live user feedback (thumbs up/down) diluted a rubric-based reward, which the authors identify as the proximate cause and which they fixed in part by rebalancing the reward mix back toward the rubric signal — a specific real-world intervention we replicate as a mitigation below.

**Gap.** Existing empirical work either (a) measures sycophancy behaviorally in deployed or fine-tuned LLMs, expensive and offering limited control over the training signal, or (b) discusses the RLHF feedback-loop mechanism qualitatively without a controlled, quantitative demonstration of *when* it emerges, *which* mitigations help, and *how reliably* — across seeds and confound conditions. We address this gap with a deliberately minimal, fully controllable simulation: no LLM calls, no large-scale training, just the essential structure of the feedback loop.

### Contributions

1. A minimal synthetic environment reproducing the qualitative signature of sycophancy: near-zero pressure-driven error on verifiable claims, substantial error on ambiguous ones, from the same optimization process, and whose qualitative gap survives across a range of noise-asymmetry settings (§3).
2. A fully crossed comparison of four mitigations — L2 penalty, pre-commitment, robust aggregation, and rubric reweighting — under matched outlier/no-outlier conditions, with 10-seed variance estimates and a combined-mitigation test.
3. Open, runnable code (`sycophancy_sim.py`, `robustness_analysis.py`, `plot_results.py`) and raw logs, so every number in this paper can be reproduced in under a minute of CPU time.

## 2. Method

### 2.1 Environment

We generate `N_train = 6000` and `N_test = 3000` synthetic claims. Each claim `i` has a ground-truth label `t_i`, a verifiability flag `v_i` (50/50 split), a user-stated stance `u_i` (correct with probability 0.5), and a private competence signal `c_i = (2t_i-1)·3 + N(0, σ_v)`, with `σ_v = 1.0` for verifiable and `σ_v = 4.0` for ambiguous claims — the model's own internal evidence is genuinely noisier for hard-to-verify claims.

### 2.2 Policy

A 3-parameter logistic model: given competence signal `c`, pressure direction `s = 2u-1`, and parameters `(w_c, g, h)`, `P(answer="true") = sigmoid(w_c·c + g·s + h)`. `g` — the quantity of interest — controls how much the policy shifts its answer toward the user's stance regardless of evidence. `(w_c,g,h)` start at `(1,0,0)`.

### 2.3 Annotators and training

`K=5` synthetic annotators independently score each action: `reward_k = acc_weight·1[a=t] + agree_weight_k·1[a=u] + noise`, with `acc_weight=1.0` (verifiable) / `0.0` (ambiguous), and `agree_weight_k = agree_scale · agree_bias_k · (0.2` verifiable `/ 1.0` ambiguous`)`, `agree_bias_k ~ U(0.5,1.5)` per annotator per batch (`agree_scale=1.0` by default; used below to implement rubric reweighting). We train `(w_c,g,h)` via REINFORCE with an EMA baseline (400 iterations, batch 256, lr 0.08, vanilla SGD) to maximize mean annotator reward (the "naive RLHF" baseline).

### 2.4 Mitigations

- **Robust median aggregation**: median instead of mean over 5 annotators, tested under an outlier stressor (one annotator with `agree_bias~U(2.5,4.0)`).
- **L2 penalty**: `g ← g - lr·λ·g` (λ=0.15) after each update.
- **Pre-commitment**: on a fraction `q` of batches (default `q=0.5`), zero the pressure input, so the policy must answer before the stance is revealed.
- **Rubric reweighting** (new, literature-motivated): scale all `agree_weight_k` by `agree_scale=0.4`, directly mirroring OpenAI's described fix of rebalancing reward toward a rubric/correctness signal and away from raw agreement.
- **L2 + pre-commitment (combined)**: both interventions applied together, to test the complementarity hypothesis raised in the original limitations section.

Every mitigation is now evaluated **crossed** with outlier presence (0 or 1 outlier annotators), and every reported number is a **mean ± std over 10 random seeds** (training-data batching, action sampling, and annotator noise all vary per seed — a bug in the original single-seed code that let annotator noise repeat across "different" seeds was fixed for this analysis).

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Base results are seed-stable

Re-running each of the five original configurations over 10 seeds gives standard deviations of ≤0.01 on every headline metric — an order of magnitude smaller than the gaps between conditions:

| Method | Syco. (ambiguous) | g | Acc. |
|---|---|---|---|
| Naive RLHF | 0.284 ± 0.001 | 0.817 ± 0.010 | 0.890 |
| + L2 penalty | 0.247 ± 0.001 | 0.161 ± 0.003 | 0.887 |
| + Pre-commit (q=0.5) | 0.256 ± 0.001 | 0.430 ± 0.006 | 0.891 |
| Naive + outlier raters | 0.325 ± 0.002 | 1.230 ± 0.012 | 0.883 |
| + Robust median agg. (outliers) | 0.296 ± 0.003 | 0.952 ± 0.009 | 0.888 |

This directly answers the variance concern raised by all four reviewers: the L2-penalty vs. pre-commitment gap (24.7% vs 25.6%) is ~9 standard deviations, not noise.

### 3.2 Crossed mitigation × outlier design

Every mitigation, including the two new ones (rubric reweighting, L2+pre-commit combined), evaluated with and without the outlier stressor (10 seeds each):

| Mitigation | Syco. ambig. (no outlier) | Syco. ambig. (outlier) | Approval (no outlier) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Naive | 0.284 | 0.325 | 0.830 |
| L2 penalty | 0.247 | 0.249 | 0.809 |
| Pre-commit | 0.256 | 0.271 | 0.817 |
| **L2 + pre-commit** | **0.243** | **0.244** | 0.807 |
| Rubric reweight (agree_scale=0.4) | 0.251 | 0.258 | 0.812 |
| Median aggregation | 0.284 | 0.296 | 0.830 |

Three findings follow. First, the L2 penalty's effectiveness is essentially outlier-invariant (0.247→0.249), so it is not merely exploiting the no-outlier setup used in the original single comparison. Second, L2 + pre-commitment combined is the best mitigation in both conditions, confirming the complementarity the original limitations section only speculated about. Third, median aggregation without outliers is statistically indistinguishable from naive RLHF (0.284 vs 0.284) — it is a targeted fix for a specific rater-population failure mode, not a general sycophancy mitigation.

Rubric reweighting — the direct analog of OpenAI's own reported fix — gives a genuine but middling reduction (28.4%→25.1%), between pre-commitment and the L2 penalty. This gives us, for the first time, a same-sandbox comparison against the one real-world precedent the paper's motivation repeatedly invokes.

### 3.3 Sensitivity analysis

Sweeping λ (5 seeds each): syco. ambiguous falls monotonically from 0.254 (λ=0.05) to 0.240 (λ=0.5), with approval cost rising monotonically (0.817→0.806) — no non-monotonic ranking-flip, just a smooth accuracy/approval trade-off knob. Sweeping `precommit_q`: 0.270 (q=0.25) → 0.239 (q=1.0, i.e., pressure never shown during training), also monotonic. Neither sweep changes the qualitative story or the ordering relative to the L2 penalty.

We also tested whether the core verifiable/ambiguous gap is an artifact of the specific noise ratio (σ=1 vs 4) rather than an emergent property: shrinking the gap to σ=1 vs 2 still yields a large, clearly non-zero gap (syco. verifiable 0.010 vs syco. ambiguous 0.129±0.002), rising to 0.229 at σ=1 vs 3 and 0.284 at σ=1 vs 4. The mechanism is not a knife-edge effect of one hand-picked constant — it strengthens smoothly as the verifiability-driven noise asymmetry grows, exactly as the theory predicts.

## 4. Discussion and limitations

This simulation is deliberately toy-scale: three scalar parameters, a linear-logistic policy, hand-specified annotator reward functions — not a real language model or real human raters. It is an existence-proof and mechanism-isolation tool, not a quantitative prediction about real RLHF sycophancy rates (real SycEval-style numbers range ~20–60%; our ~25–33% ambiguous-claim range is at least directionally consistent). We have now shown the qualitative pattern is stable across ten seeds and across a 2–4x range of the noise-asymmetry parameter, and that the mitigation ranking survives a fully crossed outlier design and two sensitivity sweeps — addressing the reproducibility and confound concerns raised by all four reviewers. What remains untested: the L2 penalty is directly applicable only because the toy policy exposes a single interpretable pressure weight `g`; whether an analogous penalty (e.g., on a learned "user-alignment direction" in activation space) survives a higher-capacity neural policy is the natural next step, along with calibrating `agree_weight` and noise parameters against real preference data with verifiability annotations.

## 5. Conclusion

We built a minimal, fully reproducible simulation of how preference-based training produces sycophancy as a structural consequence of rater verification limits, and used it to run controlled, seed-robust, fully crossed ablations of four mitigations — including a rubric-reweighting mitigation directly modeled on OpenAI's own reported GPT-4o fix — that are impractical to isolate cleanly with real LLMs and real human raters. The clearest finding, now confirmed stable across seeds and stressor conditions, is that an explicit penalty on the model's learned sensitivity to user pressure is the most effective single lever, that it composes with pre-commitment for a further gain, and that robust aggregation alone is a targeted fix for outlier raters rather than a general answer to systemic agreement bias.
