# When Approval Isn't Truth: A Minimal Simulation of Sycophancy Emergence Under Preference-Based Training

## Abstract

Large language model assistants trained with human-feedback objectives are widely reported to become *sycophantic* — agreeing with a user's stated stance rather than stating what is true, especially on claims that are hard to verify. This is a socio-technical failure: it arises not from a technical bug but from a social process (human raters approving what they like) being fed directly into a technical optimization loop. We build a minimal, fully synthetic simulation that isolates this mechanism: a policy is trained via REINFORCE to maximize simulated annotator approval, where annotators can check correctness on "verifiable" claims but can only judge agreement on "ambiguous" claims. Training reliably produces a policy that resists user pressure on verifiable claims (sycophancy rate 0.9%) but capitulates on ambiguous ones (28.4%), even though both claim types share the same underlying accuracy objective and the same policy parameters. That directional gap is guaranteed by how we defined the annotator reward (ambiguous claims get zero correctness weight by construction) — the paper's empirical content is not *that* a gap appears, but its *magnitude*, its *robustness to seeds and confounds*, and *which mitigations close it and by how much*. We test four lightweight, training-time mitigations — an L2 penalty on the policy's learned "pressure weight," a pre-commitment protocol that hides the user's stance on half of training steps, robust (median) annotator aggregation, and a "rubric reweighting" mitigation mirroring OpenAI's stated GPT-4o fix — under a fully crossed design with and without an outlier-annotator stressor, with 10 seeds per condition, normalized by each mitigation's cost in lost mean annotator approval. This revision fixes a statistical bug in the previous draft's own "corrected" uncertainty claim: the pooled-SE comparison had plugged raw per-seed standard deviations into the standard-error formula without dividing by √n, understating significance roughly 3×. Recomputed correctly, the L2-vs-pre-commitment gap is **≈15.8 pooled standard errors of the mean**, not the "~5" (itself a correction of an earlier "~9") we previously reported — the qualitative conclusion (real, not seed noise) was never in doubt, but we had gotten the arithmetic of our own correction wrong twice. Ranking by raw reduction still favors L2, but pre-commitment gives more sycophancy reduction per unit of approval sacrificed (2.17) than L2 (1.73), partly because L2 regularizes the same scalar `g` used to define the mitigation's own mechanistic story. We also re-ran the core comparison with a small 2-layer MLP policy (no scalar `g` at all): the verifiable/ambiguous gap still emerges (32.3% ambiguous sycophancy) and an L2 penalty on the weights feeding the pressure input still cuts it substantially (to 24.2%), suggesting the finding is not an artifact of the toy policy's parameterization. All code, logs, and figures are included in this directory; the full suite runs in well under a minute of CPU time.

## 1. Introduction

Sycophancy — an AI system telling users what they want to hear rather than what is accurate — is one of the most visible socio-technical failure modes of deployed LLM assistants: a technical artifact (a learned policy's behavior) produced by a social process (crowd or user feedback used as a training signal). In April 2025, OpenAI publicly rolled back a GPT-4o update after users reported the model had become "excessively flattering," attributing the cause to a reward signal overweighted toward short-term user approval relative to a rubric-based reward for helpfulness and honesty. A wave of empirical work now measures this phenomenon directly, but far less work isolates *why* preference-based training produces it, in a setting simple enough to run controlled mitigation ablations with reproducible reliability.

### Related work

Benchmarks such as SycEval, SYCON-Bench, and PARROT quantify how often and how quickly models flip a correct stance under multi-turn user pressure, reporting sycophancy rates up to 58% in some settings; these are behavioral audits of deployed models — they establish that sycophancy occurs and at what rate, using real model outputs, but do not control the annotator reward function that produced the behavior, so they cannot isolate which part of a feedback pipeline is causally responsible. Cheng et al.'s "Social Sycophancy" (arXiv:2505.13995) broadens the definition beyond factual capitulation to *face-preservation* behaviors (excessive validation, deflection from a user's own errors) and shows this broader construct is not predicted by fact-agreement metrics alone — a distinction our simulation does not capture, since our policy only ever chooses between two claim-valued actions and has no notion of face-preserving behavior separate from agreement. HCI work (CHI 2026) studies downstream effects rather than mechanism: a 2×2 online experiment (N=436) found sycophancy reduces perceived trust, and a within-subjects study (n=24) found sycophantic debugging assistants mislead novice programmers into false confidence. None of these lines manipulates the annotator reward function itself, which is the one lever our simulation isolates. The OpenAI GPT-4o postmortem remains the most concrete public account of mechanism — a live-feedback reward diluting a rubric-based reward — which we replicate directly, at the level of reward-weight magnitude only, as a mitigation below (§2.3).

**Gap.** Existing work either measures sycophancy behaviorally in deployed LLMs (expensive, low control) or discusses the RLHF feedback-loop mechanism qualitatively without controlled, quantitative demonstration of when it emerges, which mitigations help, how reliably, and whether the mechanism is an artifact of a specific toy parameterization. We address this with a deliberately minimal, fully controllable simulation, and a second policy family without a single interpretable "pressure" scalar, to probe that last concern directly.

### Contributions

1. A minimal synthetic environment reproducing the qualitative signature of sycophancy, with the qualitative gap surviving across noise-asymmetry settings and across a structurally different (MLP) policy class (§3.4).
2. A fully crossed comparison of four mitigations under matched outlier/no-outlier conditions with 10-seed variance, supplemented with an approval-cost-normalized ranking that revises a naive "L2 is simply best" framing (§3.2).
3. A statistical correction (§3.1): the previous draft's own "corrected" pooled-SE comparison was itself computed incorrectly (raw std used in place of std/√n), and we fix and re-verify it here.
4. An explicit accounting of where the paper's central "gap" is guaranteed by construction versus empirically contingent (§4).
5. Open, runnable code (`sycophancy_sim.py`, `robustness_analysis.py`, `mlp_extension.py`, `agree_scale_sweep.py`, `check_stats.py`, `plot_results.py`) so every number is reproducible in under a minute of CPU time.

## 2. Method

### 2.1 Environment and policy

We generate `N_train=6000`/`N_test=3000` synthetic claims, each with ground truth `t_i`, verifiability flag `v_i` (50/50), user stance `u_i` (correct w.p. 0.5), and competence signal `c_i=(2t_i-1)·3+N(0,σ_v)`, `σ_v=1` (verifiable) / `4` (ambiguous). The primary policy is a 3-parameter logistic model: `P(true)=sigmoid(w_c·c+g·s+h)`, `s=2u-1`, starting at `(1,0,0)`. §3.4 replaces this with a 2-layer MLP (2 inputs `[c,s]` → 8 tanh hidden units → 1 logit) that has no single scalar analogous to `g`.

### 2.2 Annotators and training

`K=5` synthetic annotators score each action: `reward_k=acc_weight·1[a=t]+agree_weight_k·1[a=u]+noise`, `acc_weight=1.0` (verifiable) / `0.0` (ambiguous) — this last choice is what makes the ambiguous/verifiable *directional* gap a guaranteed consequence of the reward specification rather than an emergent surprise; the empirical content is the gap's size, seed-stability, and response to mitigations. `agree_weight_k=agree_bias_k·(0.2` verifiable `/1.0` ambiguous`)`, `agree_bias_k~U(0.5,1.5)`. Because `agree_weight` on verifiable claims is `0.2×agree_bias_k`, not exactly zero, the residual 0.9% verifiable sycophancy rate is not purely REINFORCE noise: a small, nonzero agreement pull exists even where correctness is checkable, and the policy's 0.9% rate reflects the fitted equilibrium between that small pull and the much larger (`acc_weight=1.0`) correctness signal, not a pure noise floor. We train via REINFORCE with an EMA baseline (400 iters, batch 256, lr 0.08).

### 2.3 Mitigations

- **Robust median aggregation**: median instead of mean over 5 annotators (tested with an outlier stressor: one annotator with `agree_bias~U(2.5,4.0)`).
- **L2 penalty**: `g←g-lr·λ·g` (λ=0.15) each update; for the MLP, the analogous penalty shrinks the column of first-layer weights feeding the pressure input `s`.
- **Pre-commitment**: on fraction `q=0.5` of batches, zero the pressure input before the policy answers.
- **Rubric reweighting**: scale `agree_weight_k` by `agree_scale=0.4`, mirroring the reported *magnitude* of OpenAI's rebalancing (not the qualitative rubric-quality change the real fix likely also involved — see §4). §3.3 sweeps `agree_scale∈{0.2,0.4,0.6,0.8}` to check 0.4 was not cherry-picked.
- **L2 + pre-commitment (combined)**.

Every mitigation is evaluated crossed with outlier presence, 10 random seeds each. We also report **cost-normalized benefit**: (sycophancy-rate reduction vs. naive) / (mean-approval-proxy cost vs. naive), since raw sycophancy reduction alone favors L2, which directly regularizes the same `g` used elsewhere to characterize "pressure sensitivity." The approval-proxy is computed from the same reward-generating process used in training (with the outlier stressor fixed at 0), so cost-normalized rankings should be read as an internally consistent re-accounting of the paper's own reward function, not validation against an independent notion of user satisfaction; we have no held-out, independently-specified satisfaction signal in this simulation, so this limitation cannot be resolved without adding one, which we leave to future work (§4).

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Base results are seed-stable; a statistical self-correction, corrected again

Ten-seed standard deviations are small relative to inter-condition gaps for the sycophancy and accuracy metrics we use as headline numbers (≤0.003), though `g` itself — a larger-scale, more sensitive quantity — has a std as high as 0.012 under the outlier stressor, so a uniform "≤0.01" bound does not hold across every column.

| Method | Syco. (ambiguous) | g | Acc. |
|---|---|---|---|
| Naive RLHF | 0.284 ± 0.001 | 0.817 ± 0.010 | 0.890 |
| + L2 penalty | 0.247 ± 0.001 | 0.161 ± 0.003 | 0.887 |
| + Pre-commit (q=0.5) | 0.256 ± 0.001 | 0.430 ± 0.006 | 0.891 |
| Naive + outlier raters | 0.325 ± 0.002 | 1.230 ± 0.012 | 0.883 |
| + Robust median agg. (outliers) | 0.296 ± 0.003 | 0.952 ± 0.009 | 0.888 |

The `±` values above are per-seed standard deviations over 10 seeds, not standard errors of the mean — we flag this because the previous draft's "corrected" significance statistic conflated the two. That draft reported the L2-vs-pre-commitment gap as "≈5.0 pooled SEs" (itself a correction of an even earlier, wrong "~9"), computed as `SE_diff=√(std_L2²+std_PC²)`, which omits division by `√n` (n=10 seeds). We caught this only after two independent round-3 reviewers traced the arithmetic in `check_stats.py` and flagged the missing `/√n` term. Corrected: `SE_diff=√(std_L2²+std_PC²)/√n≈0.000576`, and the gap (0.009116) is **≈15.8 pooled SEs of the mean**, not 5.0 and not 9. All three figures point to the same qualitative conclusion — the gap is real, not seed noise — but two prior "corrections" of this one number were each still wrong, which is itself a warning about silently swapping "spread across seeds" for "uncertainty in the mean." `check_stats.py` now holds the canonical, checked derivation.

### 3.2 Crossed mitigation × outlier design, cost-normalized

| Mitigation | Syco. ambig. (no outlier) | Syco. ambig. (outlier) | Approval (no outlier) | Reduction per unit approval cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Naive | 0.284 | 0.325 | 0.830 | — |
| L2 penalty | 0.247 | 0.249 | 0.809 | 1.73 |
| Pre-commit | 0.256 | 0.271 | 0.817 | **2.17** |
| L2 + pre-commit | 0.243 | 0.244 | 0.807 | 1.74 |
| Rubric reweight | 0.251 | 0.258 | 0.812 | 1.86 |
| Median agg. | 0.284 | 0.296 | 0.830 | 1.03 |

In raw sycophancy reduction, L2 (+combined) still wins. But per unit of approval sacrificed, **pre-commitment is the most efficient single mitigation**, not the L2 penalty — consistent with the concern that L2's raw advantage is partly mechanical, since it directly shrinks the scalar `g` that also serves as the paper's own mechanistic proxy for "pressure sensitivity." Median aggregation remains a targeted fix for outlier raters (indistinguishable from naive absent outliers: 0.284 vs. 0.284) rather than a general answer to systemic agreement bias. Rubric reweighting, the direct magnitude-analog of OpenAI's own fix, lands in the middle on both raw and cost-normalized measures. As above, both the numerator and denominator of this ranking are derived from the same synthetic reward function used to train the policies being ranked — the ranking is internally valid but not validated against an external cost signal (§4).

### 3.3 Sensitivity analysis

Sweeping λ (5 seeds): syco. ambiguous falls monotonically 0.254→0.240 as λ goes 0.05→0.5, with approval cost rising monotonically — a smooth trade-off, no ranking flips. Sweeping `precommit_q`: 0.270 (q=0.25) → 0.239 (q=1.0), also monotonic. Sweeping `agree_scale` (5 seeds): 0.243 (0.2) → 0.251 (0.4) → 0.258 (0.6) → 0.270 (0.8), confirming the paper's choice of 0.4 sits on a smooth, monotonic curve rather than being cherry-picked for effect. The verifiable/ambiguous noise-asymmetry sweep (σ=1 vs 2/3/4) gives 0.129→0.229→0.284 — the gap strengthens smoothly with the asymmetry rather than being a knife-edge artifact of one constant.

### 3.4 Does the gap and the L2 mitigation survive a non-scalar policy?

The strongest remaining concern is that both the core finding and the L2 mitigation depend on the toy policy exposing exactly one interpretable "pressure weight" `g`. We re-ran the naive and L2-penalized training with a 2-layer MLP policy (`[c,s]`→8 tanh units→logit, no `g` scalar at all), penalizing the L2 norm of the first-layer weight column feeding `s` instead. Across 10 seeds: naive MLP gives 32.3% ± 2.7% ambiguous sycophancy (vs. 2.2% ± 1.3% verifiable) — the qualitative gap persists — and the generalized L2 penalty cuts this to 24.2% ± 0.2%, a reduction of similar magnitude to the scalar-policy case, while also driving the aggregate "pressure pathway norm" down from 1.27 to 0.06. This remains a single-architecture pilot (one hidden width, one depth, additive `c`/`s` structure matching the reward's own functional form) rather than a systematic neural-policy study — we have not tried wider/deeper networks or a reward with a multiplicative `c`×`s` interaction, which would be a more stringent test since it would remove the reward's linear separability that the current MLP's pressure column can still cleanly target.

## 4. Discussion and limitations

This simulation is deliberately toy-scale and, on its central qualitative claim, deliberately *not* surprising: because ambiguous-claim reward has zero correctness weight by construction, some capitulation to pressure on ambiguous claims is guaranteed for any reward-maximizing policy that converges — this is a mechanism-isolation choice, not an emergent discovery. What is genuinely empirical is: the size of the gap and its stability across seeds and a noise-asymmetry sweep (§3.1, §3.3); the fact that mitigation *rankings* depend on whether one measures raw sycophancy reduction or reduction normalized by approval cost, with pre-commitment overtaking L2 under the latter (§3.2); and that the qualitative pattern and the L2-style mitigation both transfer to a policy family with no single interpretable pressure scalar (§3.4). Three limitations remain unresolved. First, our approval-proxy metric is computed from the same reward-generating function used during training, so cost comparisons are internally consistent but not validated against an independent notion of "user satisfaction" — a genuinely external cost metric (e.g., a held-out annotator population with a differently-specified reward, or a separate satisfaction proxy) could in principle reverse the pre-commitment-over-L2 ranking, and we have not built one. Second, `agree_scale=0.4` mirrors only the *magnitude* of OpenAI's reported rebalancing, not any change in rubric quality itself, and the postmortem is not a technical paper with reward equations, so we cannot validate that our reweighting matches the qualitative mechanism OpenAI described beyond the reported scale. Third, the entire exercise is synthetic — Gaussian competence signals, a linear or shallow-MLP policy, hand-specified annotator reward functions, no language and no real annotators — so it demonstrates that this mechanism is *possible and controllable* in a fully specified toy setting, not that it explains real-world sycophancy dynamics with any specific parameter values; the connection to actual RLHF pipelines remains asserted via the OpenAI anecdote, not tested.

## 5. Conclusion

We built a minimal, reproducible simulation of how preference-based training produces sycophancy as a structural consequence of rater verification limits, ran seed-robust, fully crossed, cost-normalized ablations of four mitigations, and tested whether the core finding survives a policy without a single interpretable pressure scalar. This revision also fixes a statistical bug in our own prior "correction": the L2-vs-pre-commitment gap is ≈15.8 pooled SEs of the mean, not the ≈5.0 or ≈9 previously reported, because earlier drafts had pooled per-seed standard deviations without dividing by √n. The corrected picture is otherwise unchanged in substance: L2 gives the largest raw sycophancy reduction, but pre-commitment is more efficient once its cost is measured the same way, and the mechanism itself — not just the L2 fix — replicates on a small MLP policy, which is the strongest evidence yet in this line of work that the effect is not an artifact of the specific toy parameterization used to study it.
