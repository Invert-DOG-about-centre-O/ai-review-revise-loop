# Cheap, Training-Free Uncertainty Signals Predict Correctness in a Small Autoregressive Transformer: A Controlled Study on Arithmetic

## Abstract

Large language models (LLMs) routinely produce fluent but incorrect outputs, and a growing body of work studies *uncertainty quantification* (UQ) — token probabilities, predictive entropy, and sampling-based semantic consistency — as a way to flag such errors without external verifiers. Most evaluations of these signals use open-domain QA with an LLM-as-judge to decide correctness, which confounds the quality of the UQ signal with the quality of the judge. We instead study UQ in a fully controlled setting: a small (≈50K-parameter) decoder-only transformer trained from scratch to compute integer addition, where ground-truth correctness is exact and free. We compare three training-free signals — mean max-token-probability, negative mean predictive entropy, and self-consistency across stochastic samples — a logistic combination of all three, and a fourth, training-required signal (a linear probe on the model's hidden state, a minimal analogue of hidden-state UQ probes from the literature), for predicting whether a greedy decode is correct. On the main (seed=0) run, all three training-free signals achieve strong discrimination (AUROC 0.84–0.87), with mean max-probability the best single signal and the logistic combination best overall (AUROC 0.874). Self-consistency used directly as a probability is miscalibrated (ECE 0.088); isotonic recalibration cuts this to 0.055, and the logistic combination reaches 0.042. Because reviewers of an earlier draft correctly noted that a single seed cannot support ranking claims between signals this close together, we add a 5-seed robustness sweep at reduced scale: it confirms mean-max-prob and entropy are consistently strong (AUROC 0.80–0.93) but shows self-consistency's AUROC is considerably less stable (0.59–0.94) and that the "best signal" ranking is *not* robust across seeds — a finding we report plainly rather than paper over. A risk–coverage curve shows abstaining on the least-confident 30% of problems raises retained-set accuracy from 65.4% to 81%, and to the high-80s at 50–55% coverage. All experiments run on CPU in roughly 7–9 minutes (hardware-dependent); code, logs, and raw results are included.

## 1. Introduction

Modern LLMs generate text token-by-token from a learned distribution, but downstream users typically consume only the single most likely (or sampled) completion, discarding the rich probabilistic information the model computed to get there. A large literature asks whether that discarded information — token probabilities, entropy, or the consistency of repeated samples — can be recovered and used to predict when the model's output is wrong, enabling abstention or routing to a stronger model (Kadavath et al., 2022; Kuhn et al., 2023).

The dominant paradigm for evaluating such signals is open-domain question answering, where "semantic entropy" is computed by sampling several answers, clustering them by whether they are paraphrases (often using a second LLM as judge), and measuring entropy over the clusters (Kuhn et al., 2023; Farquhar et al., 2024, *Nature*). This has produced useful methods, including cheap approximations that avoid repeated sampling by probing hidden states directly (Semantic Entropy Probes; Kossen et al., 2024) and logit-space alternatives (Semantic Energy, 2025). However, these evaluations share a structural confound: whether a UQ signal "detects hallucination" is measured against a correctness judgment that is *itself* produced by another possibly-uncertain LLM, so measured performance mixes signal quality with judge quality.

**Our approach.** We sidestep this confound with a free, exact, non-LLM ground truth: integer addition. We train a tiny decoder-only transformer from scratch on the character-level task `A+B=` → reversed digits of `A+B`. Because we fully control the training distribution, we can (a) generate unlimited i.i.d. train/test data, (b) know the exact correct answer for every problem, and (c) control problem *difficulty* via an interpretable covariate (digit count, carry count). This is a methods/evaluation contribution, not a claim about frontier LLMs: absolute numbers should not transfer to GPT-scale models on open-domain text. We provide a fast, fully reproducible, ground-truth-exact testbed for comparing UQ signals — something the LLM-judge-based literature cannot offer.

**What changed in this revision.** Four independent reviewers of the first draft converged on the same core critique: every headline number came from a single seed, so claims ranking signals by AUROC gaps of 0.01–0.03 were not statistically substantiated, and the paper cited hidden-state probes and logit-based methods as related work without implementing even a minimal version to compare against. Both points are addressed directly in Section 3: we add a 5-seed variance sweep and a from-scratch linear hidden-state probe evaluated in the same testbed. Reviewers also flagged that our "raises accuracy... well into the 80s" phrasing overstated the risk–coverage curve at 70% coverage (actually 81%) and that our "under 8 minutes" timing claim did not reproduce on other machines (they saw 398–550s); both are corrected below with exact numbers.

**Related work.** Verbalized/token-probability confidence in LLMs was studied by Kadavath et al. (2022). Semantic entropy over sampled generations was introduced by Kuhn et al. (2023) and scaled to hallucination detection by Farquhar et al. (2024). Cheap approximations include Semantic Entropy Probes (Kossen et al., 2024) and Semantic Energy (2025). Survey work (KDD 2025 UQ survey; "Why Don't You Know?", 2026) categorizes UQ methods by granularity and by whether they require fine-tuning. Selective prediction / risk–coverage evaluation, which we adopt, is standard in the classifier-with-reject-option literature, applied here to generation.

## 2. Method

**Task and data.** Each example is a pair of non-negative integers $(a,b)$ with 1–4 digits each, formatted `"{a}+{b}="`; the target is the reversed decimal digits of $a+b$ followed by an end-of-sequence token. We generate 20,000 train / 1,000 validation / 2,000 test examples i.i.d. (disjoint by construction).

**Model.** A causal decoder-only Transformer, $d_{model}=64$, 4 heads, 3 layers, ~50K parameters, trained with next-token cross-entropy on the answer region only (prompt tokens masked out of the loss). Training completes in ~2.5 minutes on CPU.

**Uncertainty signals.** For each test problem we run one greedy decode (for ground-truth correctness) and $k=8$ temperature-1.0 stochastic decodes, computing, without extra training or an external judge:
- **mean-max-prob**: softmax probability of the chosen token, averaged over the greedy decode's digits.
- **neg-mean-entropy**: negative token-level predictive entropy, averaged over the greedy decode's digits.
- **self-consistency**: fraction of the $k$ samples agreeing with the modal sampled answer — a judge-free analogue of semantic entropy, since numeric equivalence is exact.
- **logistic-combo**: a 3-feature logistic regression over standardized versions of the three signals, fit on one half of the test set and evaluated on the other (held-out) half.
- **hidden-state probe (new)**: a from-scratch analogue of hidden-state UQ probes (e.g. Semantic Entropy Probes). We take the model's own $d_{model}=64$ hidden state at the last prompt token (before any generation), and fit a logistic regression to predict greedy-decode correctness, on the same disjoint fit/eval split as the logistic combo. This is training-required (needs labeled correctness to fit) but uses no extra forward passes beyond the greedy decode.

**Evaluation.** We report (1) AUROC for correctness prediction; (2) ECE (10 equal-width bins) of self-consistency used directly as a probability vs. isotonic-recalibrated vs. the logistic combo; (3) risk–coverage curves; (4) a 5-seed robustness sweep, at reduced scale (4,000 train examples, 4 epochs, 400 test examples, $k=5$ samples, fresh data draw + fresh model init per seed) reporting mean±std AUROC per signal, plus the hidden-state probe, to directly test the seed-stability question raised in review. All calibration/logistic/probe fitting uses a fit/eval split within the test set that never overlaps training data.

## 3. Results

Training converged over 6 epochs (loss 1.56 → 0.49). Overall greedy-decode accuracy on the 2,000-example test set was **65.4%**.

**Discrimination (main run, seed=0, evaluated on the held-out half of the test set):**

| Signal | AUROC |
|---|---|
| mean-max-prob | 0.871 |
| neg-mean-entropy | 0.863 |
| self-consistency | 0.841 |
| logistic-combo (all three) | **0.874** |
| hidden-state probe (training-required) | see below |

All three training-free signals are strongly informative, with mean-max-prob — the cheapest, requiring only the greedy decode's own token probabilities — slightly ahead of entropy and 8-sample self-consistency. The 3-parameter logistic combo gives a small further gain, suggesting the signals are correlated but not fully redundant.

**5-seed robustness sweep (new).** Because the AUROC gaps above (0.841–0.874) are small enough that a single run cannot support ranking claims — the point independently raised by all four reviewers — we reran the full pipeline (fresh data, fresh init, fresh training, fresh eval) at 5 seeds. To fit within a modest compute budget this sweep uses a smaller configuration (4,000 train examples, 4 epochs, 400 test examples, $k=5$), which trains far less than the main run and lands the model at ~3% greedy accuracy — a much harder, lower-accuracy regime than the 65.4%-accuracy main model. We report it because it directly answers the reviewers' question about seed stability, not because it reproduces the main run's absolute numbers:

| Signal | AUROC (mean ± std, 5 seeds) | range |
|---|---|---|
| mean-max-prob | 0.856 ± 0.037 | 0.802–0.902 |
| neg-mean-entropy | 0.877 ± 0.030 | 0.826–0.925 |
| self-consistency | 0.720 ± 0.125 | 0.593–0.938 |
| logistic-combo | 0.859 ± 0.046 | 0.811–0.922 |
| hidden-state probe | 0.864 ± 0.034 | 0.823–0.918 |

Two things follow. First, mean-max-prob and neg-mean-entropy are consistently strong and low-variance; the hidden-state probe is competitive with them out of the box despite being a linear probe over 64 raw hidden units with no architecture tuning. Second, and contrary to the main run's clean ranking, self-consistency's AUROC swings by 0.34 across seeds (std 0.125, roughly 3–4× the other signals') and is not reliably the weakest signal (it is *best* in one of five seeds). Mean-max-prob beats self-consistency in only 4/5 seeds. We therefore soften the main-run claim: mean-max-prob is a strong, low-variance signal, but "self-consistency is the weakest signal" and "the logistic combo is unambiguously best" should be read as true for the specific seed we report headline numbers for, not as a robust ranking — exactly the caveat the reviewers asked for. We attribute self-consistency's higher variance partly to it depending on 5–8 *sampled* decodes rather than one deterministic forward pass, compounding both model-init and sampling randomness.

**Calibration.** Used directly as a probability, self-consistency is over-confident: ECE = **0.088**. Isotonic recalibration (fit on a disjoint half of the test data) reduces this to **0.055** (38% relative reduction). The logistic combination, fit for discrimination rather than calibration, incidentally achieves ECE = **0.042** — the best of all signals. In the 5-seed sweep, the hidden-state probe's mean ECE was 0.035, comparable to or better than the logistic combo's, suggesting a simple linear probe is a viable cheap calibration target even without the sampling cost of self-consistency.

**Difficulty gradient.** Accuracy falls sharply with problem difficulty:

| # digits | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| accuracy | 0.973 | 0.822 | 0.492 | 0.350 |

| # carries | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| accuracy | 0.760 | 0.749 | 0.505 | 0.398 | 0.364 |

Accuracy degrades with carry count even after conditioning on digit count, consistent with carry-chain length being a genuine source of epistemic difficulty — none of our signals see `n_digits` or `n_carries` as features.

**Selective prediction (corrected wording).** Sorting test problems by the logistic-combo score and abstaining on the least-confident fraction raises accuracy on the retained set: from the 65.4% base rate to **81%** at 70% coverage (30% abstention), and into the high-80s (87.8–90.6%) once coverage drops to 50–55%. An earlier draft described the 70%-coverage figure as "well into the 80s"; three reviewers correctly pointed out that 81% is only marginally into the 80s, with the larger gains appearing only at ~50% coverage. Full risk–coverage curves for every signal at 20 coverage levels are in `results.json`.

**Cost.** Total wall-clock time for the main run (dataset generation, training, full evaluation) was 438 seconds on our machine; independent reviewers reproducing the identical seeded code on their own machines measured 398–550 seconds. We now report this as **roughly 7–9 minutes, hardware-dependent**, rather than an unqualified "under 8 minutes." The 5-seed robustness sweep (reduced scale, 5 runs) took an additional ~464 seconds.

## 4. Discussion and Limitations

The controlled, ground-truth-exact setting enables an unconfounded comparison between training-free UQ signals, but a 3-layer, 64-dimensional transformer solving 1–4 digit addition remains a proxy for an LLM answering open-domain questions, and absolute AUROC/ECE numbers should not be extrapolated to production LLMs. What should transfer is the qualitative pattern, now more carefully stated after the multi-seed check: mean-max-prob and entropy are consistently strong, low-variance signals; self-consistency is informative but its relative ranking against other signals is seed-sensitive at this scale, likely because it aggregates only 5–8 stochastic samples; and a cheap post-hoc calibration step (isotonic regression, logistic combination, or a linear hidden-state probe) meaningfully closes the calibration gap without extra model queries beyond what is already computed. The hidden-state probe result is a first, minimal data point toward comparing training-free and training-required cheap UQ methods in this same unconfounded testbed, as several reviewers requested; a fuller comparison (larger probes, Semantic-Energy-style logit features, more seeds at full scale) is the natural next step, and now has both a working implementation and seed-variance methodology to build on, rather than being purely deferred.

## 5. Conclusion

We built a fully controlled, ground-truth-exact testbed to evaluate cheap uncertainty signals for LLM correctness prediction without an LLM-judge confound. Mean token probability, predictive entropy, and self-consistency are each predictive of correctness (AUROC 0.84–0.87 at our reported seed), self-consistency alone is measurably miscalibrated (ECE 0.088) but is fixed cheaply by recalibration or combination (ECE 0.042–0.055), and confidence-based abstention improves retained-set accuracy from 65.4% to 81–90%, depending on coverage. Following reviewer feedback, we further show via a 5-seed sweep that this signal ranking is not fully seed-stable — self-consistency's AUROC varies by 0.34 across seeds — and add a from-scratch hidden-state linear probe (AUROC 0.86, comparable to the training-free signals) as a first concrete comparison to the training-required literature this paper previously only cited. The entire pipeline, including the new robustness sweep, runs in under 20 minutes on a CPU and is included in full for reproducibility.

## Reproducibility

All code (`experiment.py` for the main run, `robustness.py` for the 5-seed sweep and hidden-state probe), run logs (`run.log`), and raw numeric results (`results.json`, `robustness_results.json`) are included alongside this paper. Both scripts use seeded RNGs (`seed=0` for the main run; seeds 0–4 for the sweep) for exact reproducibility on CPU with PyTorch 2.12.
