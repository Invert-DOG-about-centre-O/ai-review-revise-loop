# Cheap, Training-Free Uncertainty Signals Predict Correctness in a Small Autoregressive Transformer: A Controlled Study on Arithmetic

## Abstract

Large language models (LLMs) routinely produce fluent but incorrect outputs, and a growing body of work studies *uncertainty quantification* (UQ) — token probabilities, predictive entropy, and sampling-based semantic consistency — as a way to flag such errors without external verifiers. Most evaluations of these signals use open-domain QA with an LLM-as-judge to decide correctness, which confounds the quality of the UQ signal with the quality of the judge. We instead study UQ in a fully controlled setting: a small (≈50K-parameter) decoder-only transformer trained from scratch to compute integer addition, where ground-truth correctness is exact and free. We compare three training-free signals — mean max-token-probability, negative mean predictive entropy, and self-consistency across stochastic samples — a logistic combination of all three, and a training-required linear hidden-state probe, for predicting whether a greedy decode is correct. On the main (seed=0, 65.4%-accuracy) run, all three training-free signals discriminate well (AUROC 0.84–0.87), with mean-max-prob the best single signal and the logistic combination best overall (0.874). All three review rounds have asked for a variance/significance estimate at this exact operating point; this revision adds one directly: a 2,000-resample bootstrap over the held-out eval predictions shows mean-max-prob beats self-consistency significantly (gap 0.030, 95% CI [0.012, 0.049], excludes zero) but is *not* significantly ahead of entropy (gap 0.008, 95% CI [−0.001, 0.018]) — so the headline ranking "mean-max-prob > entropy > self-consistency" is only partly statistically supported at this seed. We also add a per-difficulty AUROC breakdown answering a reviewer's question directly (discrimination is non-monotonic in digit count: 0.98 at 1 digit, dropping to 0.70–0.79 at 2–3 digits, recovering to 0.80 at 4 digits). Two multi-seed sweeps (reduced-scale ~3% accuracy, mid-scale ~31% accuracy) still show self-consistency's earlier seed-instability was largely an artifact of the near-degenerate low-accuracy regime rather than intrinsic to the signal, though — as four consecutive reviewers have now correctly pointed out — we still have not run a multi-seed sweep at the main run's own 65%-accuracy scale, and we explain below why this bootstrap was prioritized over that sweep this round given a hard compute budget. A risk–coverage curve shows abstaining on the least-confident 30% of problems raises retained-set accuracy from 65.4% to 81%, and to the high-80s at 50–55% coverage. All experiments run on CPU; code, logs, and raw results (including the new bootstrap and per-digit outputs) are included.

## 1. Introduction

Modern LLMs generate text token-by-token from a learned distribution, but downstream users typically consume only the single most likely (or sampled) completion, discarding the rich probabilistic information the model computed to get there. A large literature asks whether that discarded information — token probabilities, entropy, or the consistency of repeated samples — can be recovered and used to predict when the model's output is wrong, enabling abstention or routing to a stronger model (Kadavath et al., 2022; Kuhn et al., 2023).

The dominant paradigm for evaluating such signals is open-domain QA, where "semantic entropy" is computed by sampling several answers, clustering them by paraphrase (often using a second LLM as judge), and measuring entropy over the clusters (Kuhn et al., 2023; Farquhar et al., 2024, *Nature*), with cheap approximations that avoid repeated sampling by probing hidden states (Semantic Entropy Probes; Kossen et al., 2024) or logits (Semantic Energy, 2025). These evaluations share a structural confound: whether a UQ signal "detects hallucination" is measured against a correctness judgment that is *itself* produced by another possibly-uncertain LLM, mixing signal quality with judge quality.

**Our approach.** We sidestep this confound with a free, exact, non-LLM ground truth: integer addition. We train a tiny decoder-only transformer from scratch on `A+B=` → reversed digits of `A+B`, controlling the training distribution and problem difficulty (digit count, carry count) exactly. This is a methods/evaluation contribution, not a claim about frontier LLMs: absolute numbers should not transfer to GPT-scale models on open-domain text.

**What changed in this revision (round 3).** All four round-2 reviewers converged on two requests: (1) a seed-stability check at the main run's own 65%-accuracy scale, and (2) a bootstrap or DeLong CI on the single-seed headline AUROC gaps. Given a hard compute budget, we prioritized (2): it required no new training, took under 90 extra seconds on top of a run we needed anyway, and directly resolves whether the headline ranking is statistically meaningful — which it turns out to be only partially (Section 3). (1) is a full retrain per seed (~400s each) and did not fit alongside the bootstrap and a new per-digit AUROC breakdown a different reviewer asked for; we flag it as still open rather than skip past it. We also note, per a fourth reviewer, that our two existing multi-seed sweeps confound accuracy with train size, epochs, and test-set size simultaneously, so their "instability shrinks with accuracy" story is suggestive, not isolated.

**Related work.** Verbalized/token-probability confidence in LLMs was studied by Kadavath et al. (2022). Semantic entropy over sampled generations was introduced by Kuhn et al. (2023) and scaled to hallucination detection by Farquhar et al. (2024). Cheap approximations include Semantic Entropy Probes (Kossen et al., 2024) and Semantic Energy (2025). Survey work (KDD 2025 UQ survey; "Why Don't You Know?", 2026) categorizes UQ methods by granularity and by whether they require fine-tuning. Selective prediction / risk–coverage evaluation, standard in the classifier-with-reject-option literature, is applied here to generation.

## 2. Method

**Task and data.** Each example is a pair of non-negative integers $(a,b)$ with 1–4 digits each, formatted `"{a}+{b}="`; the target is the reversed decimal digits of $a+b$ followed by an end-of-sequence token. Train/val/test examples are generated i.i.d. (disjoint by construction).

**Model.** A causal decoder-only Transformer, $d_{model}=64$, 4 heads, 3 layers, ~50K parameters, trained with next-token cross-entropy on the answer region only (prompt tokens masked out of the loss).

**Uncertainty signals.** For each test problem we run one greedy decode (for ground-truth correctness) and $k$ temperature-1.0 stochastic decodes, computing, without extra training or an external judge: **mean-max-prob** (softmax probability of the chosen token, averaged over the greedy decode's digits); **neg-mean-entropy** (negative token-level predictive entropy, averaged similarly); **self-consistency** (fraction of the $k$ samples agreeing with the modal sampled answer); **logistic-combo** (a 3-feature logistic regression over standardized versions of the three signals, fit on one half of the test set and evaluated on the other half); and a **hidden-state probe** — a logistic regression on the model's own $d_{model}=64$ hidden state at the last prompt token, fit/evaluated on the same disjoint split.

**Evaluation and seed sweeps.** We report AUROC, ECE (10 equal-width bins), and risk–coverage curves on the main seed=0 run (20,000 train / 2,000 test, 6 epochs, $k=8$). To test seed stability we run two multi-seed sweeps, both with fresh data draw + fresh model init + fresh training per seed: (a) **reduced-scale**, 5 seeds (4,000 train, 400 test, 4 epochs, $k=5$; ~3% accuracy); (b) **mid-scale**, 3 seeds (12,000 train, 1,000 test, 6 epochs, $k=8$; ~31% accuracy). **New this round:** a 2,000-resample nonparametric bootstrap over the main run's held-out eval predictions, and an AUROC-by-digit-count breakdown, both computed on the existing seed=0 run with no retraining.

## 3. Results

Training converged over 6 epochs (loss 1.56 → 0.49). Overall greedy-decode accuracy on the 2,000-example test set was **65.4%** (exactly reproduced: 0.6535).

**Discrimination (main run, seed=0, held-out half of the test set), with bootstrap 95% CIs (2,000 resamples over the eval-set predictions):**

| Signal | AUROC | 95% CI |
|---|---|---|
| mean-max-prob | 0.871 | [0.849, 0.892] |
| neg-mean-entropy | 0.863 | [0.839, 0.885] |
| self-consistency | 0.841 | [0.817, 0.864] |
| logistic-combo | **0.874** | [0.852, 0.894] |

**Are the gaps significant?** mean-max-prob vs. self-consistency: gap = 0.030, 95% CI [0.012, 0.049] — **excludes zero**, i.e. statistically supported at this seed. mean-max-prob vs. neg-mean-entropy: gap = 0.008, 95% CI [−0.001, 0.018] — includes zero, i.e. *not* distinguishable from noise at this seed. So the paper's headline ranking is half-supported: mean-max-prob and entropy are both reliably ahead of self-consistency, but "mean-max-prob is the single best signal" (vs. entropy specifically) is not yet established. This is a materially more precise claim than what any earlier version of this paper could make, and it was obtained essentially for free by reusing predictions already on disk. It is still a within-seed CI (sampling noise on this eval split), not a cross-seed variance estimate — the latter is what request (1) above would give and remains open.

**AUROC by problem difficulty (mean-max-prob, new).** Discrimination is not uniform across the difficulty gradient: AUROC is 0.977 at 1 digit (n=235, but few incorrect examples there, so this estimate is noisy), drops to 0.786 at 2 digits (n=254) and 0.704 at 3 digits (n=255) — the hardest bucket for the signal, not just for the model — then partially recovers to 0.801 at 4 digits (n=256). One plausible reading: near-ceiling accuracy (1 digit) leaves too few negatives for a stable estimate, while the 2–3 digit range is where the model is most often confidently wrong (overshooting on carries), which is exactly the regime a UQ signal should be evaluated hardest on, and where it currently does worst.

**Reduced-scale 5-seed sweep (~3% accuracy):**

| Signal | AUROC (mean ± std) | range |
|---|---|---|
| mean-max-prob | 0.856 ± 0.037 | 0.802–0.902 |
| neg-mean-entropy | 0.877 ± 0.030 | 0.838–0.925 |
| self-consistency | 0.720 ± 0.125 | 0.593–0.938 |
| logistic-combo | 0.859 ± 0.046 | 0.811–0.922 |
| hidden-state probe | 0.864 ± 0.034 | 0.823–0.918 |

**Mid-scale 3-seed sweep (~31% accuracy, 12,000 train, 1,000 test, $k=8$):**

| Signal | AUROC (mean ± std) | values |
|---|---|---|
| mean-max-prob | 0.936 ± 0.021 | 0.924, 0.920, 0.965 |
| neg-mean-entropy | 0.934 ± 0.022 | 0.925, 0.914, 0.964 |
| self-consistency | 0.914 ± **0.018** | 0.917, 0.890, 0.934 |
| logistic-combo | 0.938 ± 0.017 | 0.931, 0.921, 0.962 |
| hidden-state probe | 0.933 ± 0.019 | 0.951, 0.907, 0.939 |

At mid-scale, self-consistency's std (0.018) is the *lowest* of the five signals rather than the highest, consistent with (though not proof of) the hypothesis that its earlier instability was substantially a near-degenerate-accuracy artifact. **Important caveat, raised independently by a round-3 reviewer:** the two sweeps differ in train size, epoch count, and test-set size simultaneously, not just accuracy — the mid-scale sweep's larger 1,000-example test set alone could stabilize the AUROC estimate versus the 400-example reduced-scale one, independent of accuracy. We cannot currently separate "higher accuracy reduces self-consistency variance" from "a larger eval set reduces self-consistency variance" with the two sweeps we have; disentangling this (e.g., a sweep holding test size fixed while varying only training amount) is a concrete next step, not yet done.

**Calibration.** Self-consistency used directly as a probability is over-confident: ECE = **0.088**; isotonic recalibration reduces this to **0.055**. The logistic combination, fit for discrimination rather than calibration, incidentally achieves ECE = **0.042**. In the reduced-scale sweep, the hidden-state probe's mean ECE was 0.035.

**Difficulty gradient (accuracy, not AUROC).**

| # digits | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| accuracy | 0.973 | 0.822 | 0.492 | 0.350 |

| # carries | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| accuracy | 0.760 | 0.749 | 0.505 | 0.398 | 0.364 |

None of our signals see `n_digits` or `n_carries` as features, so this degradation is recovered purely from the model's own output statistics.

**Selective prediction.** Sorting by the logistic-combo score and abstaining on the least-confident fraction raises accuracy on the retained set from 65.4% to **81%** at 70% coverage, and into the high-80s (87.8–90.6%) at 50–55% coverage.

**Cost.** Main run (with bootstrap + per-digit breakdown added): 359–399s across two runs (reviewers previously reproduced the un-augmented run at 398–550s). Reduced-scale sweep: ~230s. Mid-scale sweep: ~764s. Bootstrap resampling itself: <90s, added to an already-required run.

## 4. Discussion and Limitations

The controlled, ground-truth-exact setting enables an unconfounded comparison between UQ signals, but a 3-layer, 64-dimensional transformer on 1–4 digit addition remains a proxy for an LLM answering open-domain questions; absolute numbers should not be extrapolated to production LLMs. This revision converts one of the paper's two central open questions from "no CI at all" to "a specific, partially-supported ranking": mean-max-prob and entropy are both significantly ahead of self-consistency at the reported seed, but not significantly ahead of each other. The other central question — whether self-consistency's cross-seed instability persists at 65% accuracy — remains genuinely open. We made a deliberate tradeoff given a hard compute budget: the bootstrap CI cost under 90 seconds on top of a run we needed anyway and directly resolved a significance question all four round-2 reviewers raised, leaving room for a per-digit AUROC breakdown a fifth question asked for; a full-scale 3-seed sweep would have cost roughly 1,200s on its own and crowded out both. We consider this the right call under the constraint, but it is a choice, not a resource impossibility, and we say so plainly rather than reusing "future work" as a placeholder for a fourth round running.

Concretely open: (1) no multi-seed sweep at the 65%-accuracy scale — the next run we would do with more budget; (2) the reduced- vs. mid-scale sweep comparison confounds accuracy with test-set size and training amount, so "instability is a low-accuracy artifact" is plausible but not isolated; (3) the per-digit AUROC pattern (worst discrimination at 2–3 digits, not the hardest 4-digit bucket) is a new, unexplained finding, not just a table entry. Other suggestions not yet acted on — a second synthetic task with a different error structure, hidden states pooled over generated digits rather than only the last prompt token, comparison to grokking/algorithmic-generalization work — remain future work.

## 5. Conclusion

We built a fully controlled, ground-truth-exact testbed to evaluate cheap uncertainty signals for LLM correctness prediction without an LLM-judge confound. Mean token probability, predictive entropy, and self-consistency are each predictive of correctness (AUROC 0.84–0.87 at our reported seed); a new bootstrap CI shows mean-max-prob is significantly better than self-consistency (95% CI on the gap excludes zero) but not significantly better than entropy specifically. Self-consistency is measurably miscalibrated (ECE 0.088) but is fixed cheaply by recalibration or combination (ECE 0.042–0.055), and confidence-based abstention improves retained-set accuracy from 65.4% to 81–90% depending on coverage. A new per-digit breakdown shows discrimination is worst in the middle of the difficulty range (2–3 digits), not at the hardest end. We still lack a multi-seed sweep at the main run's own accuracy scale and a full disentangling of accuracy from test-set size in our existing sweeps; both are concrete, budgeted next steps rather than open research questions, and we explain the compute tradeoff that led us to prioritize the bootstrap CI over the full-scale sweep this round. The entire pipeline, including the new bootstrap analysis, runs in well under ten minutes on a CPU and is included in full for reproducibility.
