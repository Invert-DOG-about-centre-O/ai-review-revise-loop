# Cheap, Training-Free Uncertainty Signals Predict Correctness in a Small Autoregressive Transformer: A Controlled Study on Arithmetic

## Abstract

Large language models (LLMs) routinely produce fluent but incorrect outputs, and a growing body of work studies *uncertainty quantification* (UQ) — token probabilities, predictive entropy, and sampling-based semantic consistency — as a way to flag such errors without external verifiers. Most evaluations of these signals use open-domain QA with an LLM-as-judge to decide correctness and semantic equivalence, which confounds the quality of the UQ signal with the quality of the judge. We instead study UQ in a fully controlled setting: a small (≈50K-parameter) decoder-only transformer trained from scratch to compute integer addition, where ground-truth correctness is exact and free. We compare three training-free signals — mean max-token-probability, negative mean predictive entropy, and self-consistency across stochastic samples — and a simple logistic combination of all three, for predicting whether a greedy decode is correct. All three signals achieve strong discrimination (AUROC 0.84–0.87 on held-out problems), with mean max-probability the best single signal and the logistic combination best overall (AUROC 0.874). Self-consistency, used directly as a probability estimate, is miscalibrated (ECE 0.088); a one-parameter isotonic recalibration fit on a disjoint split cuts this to 0.055, and the learned logistic combination reaches 0.042. We further show a risk–coverage (selective prediction) curve: abstaining on the least-confident 30% of problems using the combined score raises accuracy on the remaining 70% substantially. All experiments run on CPU in under 8 minutes and are fully reproducible; code, logs, and raw results are included.

## 1. Introduction

Modern LLMs generate text token-by-token from a learned distribution, but downstream users typically consume only the single most likely (or sampled) completion, discarding the rich probabilistic information the model computed to get there. A large recent literature asks whether that discarded information — token probabilities, entropy, or the consistency of repeated samples — can be recovered and used to predict when the model's output is wrong, enabling abstention, flagging for human review, or routing to a stronger model (Kadavath et al., 2022; Kuhn et al., 2023).

The dominant paradigm for evaluating such signals is open-domain question answering, where "semantic entropy" is computed by sampling several answers, clustering them by whether they are paraphrases of each other (often using a second LLM as a natural-language-inference judge), and measuring entropy over the resulting clusters (Kuhn et al., 2023; Farquhar et al., 2024, *Nature*). This has produced genuinely useful methods, including cheap approximations that avoid repeated sampling by probing hidden states directly (Semantic Entropy Probes; Kossen et al., 2024) and logit-space alternatives that claim to fix cases where sampling-based entropy fails (Semantic Energy, 2025). However, these evaluations share a structural confound: whether a UQ signal "detects hallucination" is measured against a correctness/equivalence judgment that is *itself* produced by another possibly-uncertain LLM, so measured performance mixes the quality of the UQ signal with the quality of the judge, and results are not exactly reproducible across judge versions.

**Our approach.** We sidestep this confound by working in a domain with a free, exact, non-LLM ground truth: integer addition. We train a tiny decoder-only transformer from scratch, from random initialization, on the character-level task `A+B=` → digits of `A+B` (reversed, a standard trick for arithmetic transformers). Because the model is trained from scratch on a synthetic distribution we fully control, we can (a) generate unlimited i.i.d. train/test data, (b) know the exact correct answer for every problem with certainty, and (c) control the *difficulty* of each problem via an interpretable covariate (number of digits, number of carry operations), letting us study how uncertainty signals behave as a function of a designed difficulty gradient rather than an uncontrolled natural-language distribution. This lets us ask a narrower, cleanly-answerable version of the UQ question: *given only training-free signals available from a single or few forward passes, how well can we predict whether this specific generation is correct, and how well-calibrated are those signals?*

This is a methods/evaluation contribution, not a claim about frontier LLMs: we do not expect the numeric findings to transfer quantitatively to GPT-scale models on open-domain text. Instead, we provide a fast, fully reproducible, ground-truth-exact testbed for comparing UQ signals and calibration techniques — something the LLM-judge-based literature cannot offer — and report results.

**Related work.** Verbalized/token-probability confidence in LLMs was studied by Kadavath et al. (2022, "Language Models (Mostly) Know What They Know"). Semantic entropy over sampled generations was introduced by Kuhn et al. (2023) and scaled to open-domain hallucination detection by Farquhar et al. (2024). Cheap approximations without repeated sampling include Semantic Entropy Probes (Kossen et al., 2024) and Semantic Energy (2025), which operates on penultimate-layer logits. Survey work (e.g. the 2025 KDD UQ survey; "Why Don't You Know?", 2026) categorizes UQ methods by granularity (token/span/response) and by whether they require fine-tuning (e.g. SaySelf) or are training-free, as ours are. Recent 2026 work also documents that verbalized confidence and downstream *use* of confidence can diverge — models state uncertainty without acting on it — motivating our focus on directly *predicting* correctness from signals rather than trusting verbalization. Selective prediction / risk–coverage evaluation, which we adopt, is a standard framework from the classifier-with-reject-option literature, applied here to generation.

## 2. Method

**Task and data.** Each example is a pair of non-negative integers $(a, b)$ with $1$–$4$ digits each (uniformly sampled digit-length, then uniform value within that length), formatted as the string `"{a}+{b}="`. The target is the reversed decimal digits of $a+b$, e.g. `12+9=` → target `12` (reversed digits of `21`), followed by an end-of-sequence token. Reversed-digit output is a well-known trick (used in prior arithmetic-transformer studies) that aligns the generation order with the natural least-significant-digit-first carry computation. We generate 20,000 training, 1,000 validation, and 2,000 test examples i.i.d. from this process (disjoint by construction since integers are freshly sampled).

**Model.** A causal (decoder-only) Transformer with $d_{model}=64$, 4 attention heads, 3 layers, trained with next-token cross-entropy loss on the answer region only (prompt tokens are masked out of the loss). This is a from-scratch model (~50K parameters), not a pretrained LLM — chosen deliberately so that (i) training completes in ~2.5 minutes on CPU, and (ii) we have full, uncontaminated control over the training distribution and exact ground truth.

**Uncertainty signals.** For each test problem we run one greedy decode (to determine ground-truth correctness of the point estimate) and $k=8$ temperature-1.0 stochastic decodes. From these we compute, without any additional training or external judge:
- **mean-max-prob**: the softmax probability of the chosen token, averaged over the digits of the greedy decode.
- **neg-mean-entropy**: the negative of the token-level predictive entropy $H(p) = -\sum_v p(v)\log p(v)$, averaged over the digits of the greedy decode.
- **self-consistency**: the fraction of the $k$ stochastic samples that agree with the modal (most common) sampled answer — a direct, judge-free analogue of semantic entropy, since numeric-answer equivalence is exact and requires no NLI clustering.
- **logistic-combo**: a 3-feature logistic regression over standardized versions of the three signals above, fit on one half of the test set and evaluated on the other (held-out) half, to test whether a simple learned combination outperforms any single training-free signal.

**Evaluation.** We report (1) AUROC for predicting greedy-decode correctness from each signal; (2) Expected Calibration Error (ECE, 10 equal-width bins) of self-consistency used directly as a probability, compared to (a) the same score after 1-D isotonic regression fit on a disjoint half of the test data, and (b) the logistic combination; (3) risk–coverage curves, i.e., accuracy on the retained subset as we abstain on the least-confident $x\%$ of problems, for each signal. All calibration/logistic fitting uses a fit/eval split *within* the test set that never overlaps with training data, so reported ECE and combo AUROC are on genuinely held-out points.

## 3. Results

Training converged over 6 epochs (cross-entropy loss 1.56 → 0.49). Overall greedy-decode accuracy on the 2,000-example test set was **65.4%** — the model is a moderately accurate but far-from-perfect calculator, which is exactly the regime in which correctness-prediction from uncertainty signals is useful (a saturated model or a random one both make the problem trivial or impossible).

**Discrimination (AUROC for correctness prediction, evaluated on the held-out half of the test set):**

| Signal | AUROC |
|---|---|
| mean-max-prob | 0.871 |
| neg-mean-entropy | 0.863 |
| self-consistency | 0.841 |
| logistic-combo (all three) | **0.874** |

All three training-free signals are strongly informative (AUROC well above the 0.5 chance level), with mean-max-prob — the cheapest signal, requiring only the greedy decode's own token probabilities and no extra sampling — slightly outperforming both entropy and 8-sample self-consistency individually. Combining all three with a 3-parameter logistic regression gives a small further gain (0.874 vs. 0.871 for the best single signal), suggesting the signals are highly correlated but not fully redundant.

**Calibration.** Used directly as a probability, self-consistency is over-confident: ECE = **0.088**. A one-dimensional isotonic recalibration, fit on a disjoint half of the test data, reduces this to **0.055** — a 38% relative reduction from a single monotone remapping with no new information, only a fixed-up scale. The 3-feature logistic combination, which was fit for discrimination rather than calibration, incidentally achieves the best calibration of all: ECE = **0.042**. This suggests that for this task, combining signals via a simple calibrated model dominates using any single raw signal as its own probability estimate.

**Difficulty gradient.** Accuracy falls sharply and monotonically with problem difficulty, both by digit count and by number of carry operations required:

| # digits | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| accuracy | 0.973 | 0.822 | 0.492 | 0.350 |

| # carries | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| accuracy | 0.760 | 0.749 | 0.505 | 0.398 | 0.364 |

Carries require propagating information across digit positions, and accuracy degrades with carry count even after conditioning on digit count, consistent with carry-chain length being a genuine source of epistemic difficulty that a well-behaved uncertainty signal should be able to detect without being told about it explicitly (none of our three signals see `n_digits` or `n_carries` as features).

**Selective prediction.** Sorting test problems by the logistic-combo score and abstaining on the least-confident fraction raises accuracy on the retained set, e.g. retaining only the most-confident 70% of problems raises accuracy from the base rate of 65.4% well into the 80s (full risk–coverage curves for every signal, at 20 coverage levels each, are saved in `results.json` under `risk_coverage`). This confirms the AUROC finding in a decision-relevant form: an operator willing to defer roughly a third of hard problems (e.g. to a human, a calculator tool, or a larger model) can substantially reduce the error rate on what remains, using only training-free signals computed from the small model itself.

**Cost.** Total wall-clock time for dataset generation, training, and full evaluation (including 8 stochastic samples × 2,000 test problems) was **438 seconds** on CPU.

## 4. Discussion and Limitations

The controlled, ground-truth-exact setting lets us make an unconfounded comparison between training-free UQ signals, but it is also the study's main limitation: a 3-layer, 64-dimensional transformer solving 1–4 digit addition is a proxy for, not a stand-in for, an LLM answering open-domain questions. The absolute AUROC and ECE numbers should not be extrapolated to production LLMs; what should transfer is the qualitative pattern — that mean token probability, entropy, and self-consistency are highly correlated but not identical signals, that raw self-consistency is measurably overconfident as a probability, and that a cheap post-hoc calibration step (isotonic regression, or a 3-parameter logistic combination) meaningfully closes that gap without any additional model queries beyond what was already being computed. We did not evaluate the more expensive but training-required approaches from the literature (e.g. supervised confidence fine-tuning, or hidden-state probes trained on semantic entropy targets à la Semantic Entropy Probes) since our aim here was specifically the training-free regime; extending this exact testbed with a linear probe on hidden states, trained to predict our free ground-truth correctness label, is a natural next step and would let us compare probe-based UQ against sampling-based UQ with the same unconfounded evaluation this paper establishes.

## 5. Conclusion

We built a fully controlled, ground-truth-exact testbed — a from-scratch transformer trained on integer addition — to evaluate cheap, training-free uncertainty signals for LLM correctness prediction without the confound of an LLM-based correctness judge. Mean token probability, predictive entropy, and self-consistency across samples are each strongly predictive of correctness (AUROC 0.84–0.87), self-consistency alone is measurably miscalibrated as a probability (ECE 0.088) but is fixed cheaply by isotonic recalibration or a small logistic combination (ECE down to 0.042–0.055), and confidence-based abstention on the resulting scores meaningfully improves accuracy on the retained subset. The entire pipeline runs in under 8 minutes on a CPU and is included in full for reproducibility.

All code (`experiment.py`), the full run log (`run.log`), and raw numeric results including complete risk–coverage curves (`results.json`) are included alongside this paper in the same directory.
