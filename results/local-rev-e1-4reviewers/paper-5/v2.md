# Cheap Single-Pass Signals Can Match — and Cheap Probes Can Recover — Expensive Sampling-Based Uncertainty in a Toy Language Model

## Abstract

Detecting when a language model's output is wrong is central to LLM
reliability. Two families of methods dominate: cheap single-pass signals
from one forward pass (max-softmax probability, predictive entropy), and
expensive sampling-based signals that draw multiple stochastic completions
and measure their agreement or diversity (self-consistency, semantic
entropy). Recent work trains lightweight "probes" that predict the
expensive signal from cheap features. We study this trade-off in a fully
controlled, from-scratch toy setting: a small causal transformer trained on
synthetic two-digit addition, decoded under both greedy and stochastic
sampling. In our primary run (n=700 test examples), (1) cheap
max-probability is a *better* error detector than 20-sample self-consistency
or sampling entropy (AUROC 0.734 vs. 0.655/0.595); (2) a linear probe on
cheap features (max-probability, predictive entropy, mean hidden state)
recovers the sampling-entropy signal (held-out R²=0.26) well enough that the
*probe's prediction* matches the true 20-sample signal's downstream AUROC
almost exactly (0.621 vs. 0.620); (3) self-consistency agreement is
well-discriminating but badly miscalibrated (ECE=0.331 vs. 0.064 for
max-probability). Because a single training/eval run cannot establish
whether these gaps are signal or noise, we additionally re-ran the full
pipeline for 5 independent seeds (fresh model, data, and sampling each
time). Max-probability beats self-consistency in **5 of 5 seeds** (mean gap
0.105 AUROC points, range 0.027-0.274), and — notably — the step budget we
originally chose to obtain "~19% errors" is itself highly seed-sensitive
(test accuracy across the 5 seeds ranged from 7.7% to 88%, not clustered
near 19%). The maxprob-over-self-consistency ranking held even in the
seed with 92% errors, suggesting the effect is not an artifact of one
particular error regime. We release the full training/eval code, the
multi-seed script, and raw logs.

## 1. Introduction

Large language models are increasingly deployed in settings where knowing
*when to trust an answer* matters as much as producing the answer. Two
broad families of uncertainty quantification (UQ) methods have emerged.
**Single-pass signals** — token probability, predictive entropy, verbalized
confidence — are cheap but known to be poorly calibrated; models are
frequently overconfident, and verbalized confidence in particular diverges
from both internal probabilities and actual accuracy (Xiong et al. survey
work on verbalized calibration, 2025-2026). **Sampling-based signals** —
self-consistency, semantic entropy, semantic energy — draw multiple
completions and measure agreement or the entropy of the induced meaning
distribution. These tend to be more reliable but multiply inference cost by
5-20x.

This cost has motivated *cheap proxies for expensive signals*. Semantic
Entropy Probes (Kossen et al., 2024, arXiv:2406.15927) train a linear
classifier on a single forward pass's hidden states to predict semantic
entropy, reporting comparable detection at a fraction of the cost.
Efficient Bayesian estimation of semantic entropy (arXiv:2504.03579, 2025)
similarly reduces the number of samples needed via better estimation. A
2025-2026 survey (arXiv:2412.05563) catalogs UQ methods by access level and
flags the cost/reliability trade-off as open, alongside the finding that
many UQ methods are vulnerable to systematic overconfidence. Semantic
Energy (arXiv:2508.14496, 2025) argues that post-softmax semantic entropy
misses information present in raw internal confidence — evidence that even
"expensive" sampling-based methods have blind spots relative to cheaper
internal signals.

**Gap.** Most empirical evidence for the cheap-vs-expensive trade-off comes
from large pretrained models on open-ended QA, where the "expensive" signal
itself requires an auxiliary NLI model or LLM judge to cluster samples into
semantic equivalence classes. This makes it hard to isolate whether a probe
recovers genuine uncertainty structure or exploits benchmark artifacts, and
makes such studies expensive to reproduce. We ask: **does the same
qualitative pattern appear in a minimal, fully controlled, exactly-checkable
toy task**, where ground-truth correctness and answer clustering are
trivial and the pipeline runs on a CPU in under two minutes? A positive
answer suggests the phenomenon is a basic property of how these signals
relate to each other, not a benchmark artifact.

## 2. Method

**Task.** Two-digit addition as fixed-length strings: input `"aa+bb="` (6
chars, a,b uniform in [0,99]), target `"sss"` (3-digit zero-padded sum).
Fixed lengths avoid padding/masking complexity and make exact-match
correctness and answer clustering trivial.

**Model.** A causal, decoder-only Transformer trained from scratch (2
layers, d_model=64, 4 heads, feed-forward 128; ~130K parameters),
character-level vocabulary of 12 tokens. Teacher forcing, cross-entropy loss
on the 3 answer positions only, AdamW, lr=3e-3, batch 128. All data are
drawn i.i.d. from the full 10,000-example input space at each step/eval
(sampled on the fly, no fixed train file), so there is no train/test overlap
by construction.

**Uncertainty signals**, per test example from the greedy-decoded 3-digit
answer: *cheap, single forward pass* — mean max-softmax probability
(`maxprob`) and mean predictive entropy (`pred_entropy`) across the 3
positions; *expensive, K=20 stochastic samples* (temperature 1.0) —
self-consistency agreement with the greedy answer (`sample_agree`) and
Shannon entropy of the empirical distribution over distinct sampled answer
strings (`sample_entropy`), the toy analogue of semantic entropy where
clustering is exact string match (appropriate here: two digit strings are
never semantically equivalent).

**Cheap probe.** Following the Semantic Entropy Probes idea, we fit a
linear model (Ridge, alpha=1.0, standardized features) to predict
`sample_entropy` from cheap features alone: `maxprob`, `pred_entropy`, and
the mean hidden state (64-dim) at the 3 greedy-decoded positions. A 50/50
held-out split of the test examples (fit/evaluate) ensures both R² and
downstream AUROC are out-of-sample.

**Primary run.** 260 training steps, N_TEST=700, single seed (0). This
step budget was chosen because an earlier 1200-step run reached 100% test
accuracy, leaving no errors to detect; 260 steps gave a ~19% error rate in
that one run.

**Multi-seed robustness check.** A single seed cannot distinguish a real
effect from noise, and a single step-budget choice cannot show whether
"~19% errors" is a stable operating point or a lucky draw — both concerns
raised by reviewers of an earlier draft of this paper. We therefore wrote a
second script (`multiseed.py`) that repeats the *entire* pipeline — fresh
model init, fresh training data, fresh test set, fresh K=20 sampling — for
5 independent seeds (0-4) at the same 260-step budget, using N_TEST=400 for
speed (5 full runs on CPU). We report, per seed, all four AUROCs, the probe
R² and holdout AUROCs, and the maxprob-minus-self-consistency AUROC gap,
then summarize mean±std across seeds and the sign of the gap in each seed.

**Evaluation.** AUROC (sign-flipped so higher = more confident) for
correct-vs-incorrect greedy answers; Expected Calibration Error (10 bins)
for `maxprob` and `sample_agree` as raw confidence values in [0,1]; held-out
R² of the probe and the held-out AUROC of the probe's *prediction* used as a
confidence score, compared against the true expensive signal and cheap
baselines on the same held-out half.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Primary run (seed 0, n=700)

Greedy-decode test accuracy: **81.1%** (133 incorrect, 567 correct).

| Signal | Type | AUROC |
|---|---|---|
| maxprob | cheap, single-pass | **0.734** |
| neg. predictive entropy | cheap, single-pass | 0.618 |
| self-consistency agreement (K=20) | expensive, sampling | 0.655 |
| neg. sampling entropy (K=20) | expensive, sampling | 0.595 |

**Calibration (ECE, 10 bins):** `maxprob` is well calibrated (0.064);
`sample_agree` used directly as a confidence value is badly miscalibrated
(0.331) — samples agree with each other far more often than the greedy
answer is actually correct.

**Probe recovering the expensive signal (held-out n=350):** R²=0.261
predicting `sample_entropy` from cheap features. Using the probe's
*predicted* entropy as a confidence score gives AUROC=0.621, versus
AUROC=0.620 for the *true* 20-sample signal on the same held-out examples —
a near-exact match obtained with zero extra sampling at eval time. The
cheap `maxprob` baseline still outperforms both (AUROC=0.750 on this same
half): the probe is unnecessary here because an even cheaper signal is
already superior, but the *recovery* result (probe ≈ true expensive signal)
still supports the Semantic-Entropy-Probes-style claim.

### 3.2 Multi-seed robustness check (5 seeds, n=400 each)

| Seed | Accuracy | maxprob AUROC | self-consist. AUROC | gap | probe R² | true-signal holdout AUROC | probe holdout AUROC |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0.820 | 0.755 | 0.678 | 0.077 | 0.260 | 0.577 | 0.588 |
| 1 | 0.750 | 0.730 | 0.646 | 0.085 | 0.419 | 0.537 | 0.687 |
| 2 | 0.077 | 0.663 | 0.389 | 0.274 | 0.089 | 0.568 | 0.485 |
| 3 | 0.760 | 0.679 | 0.653 | 0.027 | 0.201 | 0.566 | 0.587 |
| 4 | 0.880 | 0.714 | 0.652 | 0.063 | 0.301 | 0.605 | 0.659 |
| **mean±std** | 0.658±0.294 | 0.708±0.033 | 0.603±0.108 | **0.105±0.087** | 0.254±0.109 | 0.571±0.022 | 0.601±0.070 |

Two things stand out. First, **maxprob beats self-consistency in 5 of 5
seeds** — the direction of the primary-run finding is not a fluke of seed
0, even though the *magnitude* of the gap varies substantially (0.027 to
0.274). Second, and unexpectedly, **the 260-step operating point is not a
stable "~19% error rate" checkpoint**: accuracy across the 5 seeds ranges
from 7.7% (seed 2, 92% errors) to 88% (seed 4, 12% errors), with the
originally-reported 19% error rate (81.1% accuracy, seed 0 at n=700) sitting
in the middle of this range rather than being representative. This is a
useful, if humbling, correction to the primary-run framing: 260 steps is a
point of high training variance for this architecture/task, not a
calibrated target error rate. Despite this, the maxprob advantage holds
even at the extreme (seed 2, 92% errors) — if anything the gap is *largest*
there (0.274) — which is evidence against the concern that the ranking is
an artifact specific to one narrow error regime. Probe R² is noisier
(0.089-0.419) and, on this smaller n=400/split=200 holdout, the probe
sometimes beats the true expensive signal's own holdout AUROC (seeds 1, 3,
4) — likely because R²=0.26-0.42 lets the probe smooth out sampling noise
in the K=20 estimate itself, an interesting side effect worth flagging
rather than a design goal.

**Ablation note.** An initial 1200-step run reached 100% accuracy (AUROC
undefined); a first probe attempt with unscaled features and plain linear
regression gave negative R². We report both, per `run_log.txt`, as
motivation for the final design (260 steps; Ridge on standardized
features) rather than results.

## 4. Discussion and Limitations

This is a deliberately minimal, synthetic testbed — a 130K-parameter
character model on two-digit addition, not a large language model, and its
"errors" are near-miss arithmetic mistakes rather than open-ended factual
hallucinations. Semantic clustering here is exact string match, which
sidesteps the genuinely hard part of semantic entropy estimation
(NLI-based clustering of paraphrases) that motivates methods like Kossen et
al.'s. Arithmetic near-misses have no notion of semantic equivalence
distinct from string identity; this is likely part of *why*
sampling-based signals underperform here, and it means the analogy to
open-ended semantic entropy is weaker than the framing might suggest — we
flag this explicitly rather than let the headline numbers imply otherwise.

The multi-seed check (§3.2) addresses the most consistent concern raised
about this line of work: without repeated runs, an 8-14 point AUROC gap
from a single seed is hard to distinguish from noise, and a single
"~19% error" checkpoint could be a cherry-picked operating point. Five
seeds is still a small sample for estimating the gap's precise magnitude
(std=0.087 on a mean of 0.105), and we did not compute bootstrap confidence
intervals within seeds or vary K (sample budget) or the probe's feature set
in this revision — both remain natural, cheap extensions given the
sub-3-minute-per-seed runtime. But the *sign* of the maxprob-over-self-
consistency gap was consistent across every seed we ran, including one with
an extreme, spontaneously-arising 92% error rate, which is stronger evidence
of directional robustness than the single-seed primary run alone could
offer, and directly answers the "is this an artifact of one error regime"
question with data rather than assertion.

We view three results as robust enough to flag as hypotheses for
pretrained LLMs, now with multi-seed support for the first: (1) cheap
single-pass probability should not be assumed inferior to sampling-based
UQ by default, and should be a baseline whenever expensive sampling-based
UQ is proposed — supported here across 5/5 seeds, not one; (2)
self-consistency agreement can be badly miscalibrated even with reasonable
discriminative power; (3) linear probes on single-pass features can recover
much of an expensive sampling signal's discriminative power, though on this
toy task an even cheaper signal (maxprob) already dominates both.

## 5. Conclusion

In a fully controlled, from-scratch toy LM trained on synthetic addition,
cheap single-pass max-probability was a better error detector than
20-sample self-consistency or sampling entropy — not just in a single run,
but in all 5 of 5 independent seeded reruns spanning wildly different error
rates (7.7% to 92%). Self-consistency agreement was substantially
miscalibrated despite reasonable AUROC, and a linear probe on single-pass
features recovered most of the expensive sampling signal's discriminative
power. A secondary finding from the robustness check — that the "~19%
error" operating point is itself seed-sensitive rather than a stable
checkpoint — is reported as an honest correction to the primary run's
framing, not smoothed over. These patterns qualitatively echo recent
findings on pretrained LLMs in a setting that is trivial to reproduce and
extend. All code (`experiment.py`, `multiseed.py`), logs, and raw results
are included alongside this paper.
