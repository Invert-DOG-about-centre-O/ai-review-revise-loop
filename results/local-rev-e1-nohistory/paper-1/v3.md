# When Is Sampling Worth It? A Controlled Comparison of Token-Probability and Sampling-Based Uncertainty Signals for LLM Error Detection

## Abstract

Recent work on uncertainty quantification (UQ) for LLMs argues that token-level probabilities are unreliable — alignment training distorts them, and paraphrase fragments probability mass — and proposes sampling-based alternatives like semantic entropy and self-consistency. These claims are usually validated on open-domain tasks where "correctness" is itself judged by another model, confounding signal quality with judge noise. We instead use a setting with **exact, judge-free ground truth**: a small transformer trained from scratch on multi-digit integer addition, where an answer is either the correct sum or not. On 400 held-out problems we compare six confidence/uncertainty signals — mean/min token log-probability, perplexity, first-token entropy, semantic entropy, and self-consistency agreement — as error detectors (AUROC) and calibrated estimates (ECE). Token-probability signals (AUROC 0.955) outperform semantic entropy (0.930) and self-consistency (0.933), and self-consistency majority voting gives no accuracy lift over greedy decoding (92.75% both). A 3-seed robustness check with widely varying error rates (21–80 errors/150 problems, accuracy 46.7–86.0%) confirms the token-probability advantage is positive in every seed and tightly clustered (mean ΔAUROC = +0.040 vs. semantic entropy, +0.036 vs. self-consistency; std ≈ 0.006–0.007). This revision adds a **K-sample ablation** (K=8/16/32) on a properly-trained model, which the previous version attempted only on undertrained models and discarded as uninterpretable: with a real model, all signals are stable across K, but at this model's high accuracy (88%, 18/150 errors) the ranking **reverses** — sampling-based signals edge out token-probability (0.990–0.993 vs. 0.988) — the opposite of the main-run and multi-seed result. We read this as evidence that the token-probability advantage is not universal but concentrated in the moderate-error regime our multi-seed check covers, and shrinks (or reverses) as accuracy approaches ceiling and all signals saturate near-perfect discrimination. This sharpens the paper's practical claim: token-probability is a strong, cheap default specifically when errors are frequent enough to be worth detecting at all; near-ceiling accuracy makes the choice of signal nearly moot.

## 1. Introduction

LLMs frequently produce fluent but incorrect output, and detecting this requires some signal about the model's confidence. The default tool is token-level probability, but recent literature argues this signal is compromised: alignment training rewards confident-sounding text regardless of correctness, and a single meaning can be expressed by many token sequences, so probability mass on any one surface form understates true confidence.

This motivated **sampling-based** UQ methods. Semantic entropy (Kuhn et al., 2023; Farquhar et al., 2024) samples multiple generations, clusters them by semantic equivalence, and computes entropy over clusters rather than token sequences. Self-consistency (Wang et al., 2022) samples multiple reasoning chains and majority-votes over final answers. Semantic Entropy Probes (Kossen et al., 2024) approximate this from a single forward pass's hidden states; we do not evaluate probing here since it requires a pretrained LLM's internal representations, but flag it as a natural next comparison (Section 5).

### Related work and the gap we address

Surveys of LLM UQ report that sampling/consistency-based methods generally dominate raw token probability on open-domain QA and reasoning benchmarks, but a recurring limitation is evaluation methodology: because ground-truth correctness for open-ended generation is itself uncertain, most benchmarks use an LLM judge or human annotation, which is noisy and makes it hard to isolate whether a signal fails because of the signal itself or judge noise correlated with difficulty. Most evaluations also mix tasks where sampling should help for principled reasons (paraphrase, multi-hop reasoning with divergent valid paths) with a blanket "prefer sampling" recommendation, without isolating *when* token-probability specifically fails.

We complement this with a small, controlled study: integer addition, where correctness is a deterministic function of the output and the answer space is a single canonical string (no paraphrase fragmentation) — letting us cleanly ask whether sampling-based signals still beat token-probability when the theoretical reason sampling should help is absent by construction, and how that answer depends on the model's error rate.

## 2. Method

### 2.1 Task and model

We generate `"a+b="` → `"c"` examples with `a, b ~ Uniform[0, 999]`, tokenized at the character level. We train a small GPT-style decoder-only transformer from scratch (3 layers, 4 heads, d_model=128, d_ff=256; ≈0.6M params) with loss masked to answer/EOS tokens only, using AdamW (lr 3e-4, batch 128). This sandboxed environment had no network access to model hubs, hence training from scratch rather than probing a pretrained LLM (Section 5); the controlled ground truth is independently valuable regardless of model provenance.

**Main run** (Section 3): fixed 240s CPU training budget, 3,937 steps, final loss 0.046, evaluated on 400 held-out problems.

**Multi-seed robustness check** (Section 3.1): 3 additional independent models (different init, training stream, and 150-problem eval set), each under a reduced 130s budget (~1,740 steps), yielding a wider accuracy spread (46.7–86.0%) to stress-test whether the ranking holds as error rate shifts.

**K-sample ablation** (Section 3.2, new in this revision): a separate model trained for 200s (2,736 steps, final loss 0.094, 88.0% accuracy on 150 held-out problems), evaluated at K=8/16/32 samples with the same greedy pass and error labels held fixed across K so only the sampling budget varies. Samples for K=32 were drawn once per problem and truncated to K=8/16 (nested sampling) so all three K values use the same underlying draws.

### 2.2 Confidence/uncertainty signals

From a single **greedy** decode: **mean/min token log-probability**, **perplexity** (`exp(-mean log-prob)`), and **first-token predictive entropy**. From **K temperature samples** (T=0.9, K=8 by default): **semantic entropy** over the empirical distribution of sampled integer values (equivalence is exact integer equality here, not NLI-based, since this task has no paraphrase ambiguity), and **self-consistency agreement** (fraction of samples matching the greedy answer).

### 2.3 Evaluation

We report **AUROC** for detecting greedy-answer errors, **ECE** (10 bins) for two derived confidence scores, and **self-consistency majority-vote accuracy vs. greedy accuracy**. For the multi-seed check we report the per-seed AUROC gap between the strongest token-probability signal and each sampling-based signal, and its mean/std — a paired comparison controlling for the fact that absolute AUROC moves with the error rate.

## 3. Results

Training reached 92.75% greedy accuracy on 400 held-out problems (29 errors, 371 correct).

| Signal | Type | AUROC |
|---|---|---|
| −mean log-prob | token-probability | **0.955** |
| perplexity | token-probability | **0.955** |
| −min log-prob | token-probability | 0.952 |
| −sample agreement | sampling-based | 0.933 |
| semantic entropy | sampling-based | 0.930 |
| first-token entropy | token-probability | 0.809 |

Whole-answer token-probability aggregates (mean/min log-prob, perplexity) are the strongest error detectors here, edging out both sampling-based signals. First-token entropy alone is far weaker, showing "token probability" is not monolithic: *which* aggregation is used matters as much as token- vs. sample-based framing.

### 3.1 Is this ranking stable across seeds?

We reran the full pipeline on 3 independent seeds with their own init, training stream, and 150-problem eval set. Accuracy varied substantially (46.7%, 86.0%, 78.7%; 80, 21, 32 errors).

| Seed | Accuracy | Errors | −mean log-prob AUROC | Semantic entropy AUROC | Self-consistency AUROC |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 46.7% | 80 | 0.893 | 0.858 | 0.859 |
| 1 | 86.0% | 21 | 0.968 | 0.918 | 0.923 |
| 2 | 78.7% | 32 | 0.941 | 0.904 | 0.910 |
| **mean ± std** | — | — | **0.934 ± 0.031** | **0.893 ± 0.026** | **0.898 ± 0.028** |

Token-probability beats both sampling-based signals in all 3 seeds. The paired gap is consistently positive and tightly clustered: **+0.040 ± 0.007** vs. semantic entropy, **+0.036 ± 0.006** vs. self-consistency. Three seeds show the ranking isn't a fluke of one draw but do not support a formal significance test.

### 3.2 K-sample ablation: does more sampling close the gap?

The previous revision's K-ablation attempt used undertrained models and produced degenerate, uninterpretable signal distributions (near-random accuracy left semantic-entropy and self-consistency with almost no error signal to detect), so it was discarded rather than reported. Here we redo it on a properly-trained model (88.0% accuracy, 18/150 errors) at K=8, 16, and 32:

| K | Semantic entropy AUROC | Self-consistency AUROC | Token-prob AUROC (fixed) | Majority-vote accuracy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 8 | 0.992 | 0.990 | 0.988 | 87.3% |
| 16 | 0.993 | 0.993 | 0.988 | 88.0% |
| 32 | 0.990 | 0.990 | 0.988 | 88.0% |

Two findings. First, increasing K from 8 to 32 does not materially move either sampling-based signal (all within 0.003 of each other) — more samples neither closes nor widens whatever gap exists; K=8 was not an unlucky undersampling. Second, and more striking: on *this* model, the ranking from Sections 3 and 3.1 **reverses** — semantic entropy and self-consistency both slightly exceed token-probability (0.990–0.993 vs. 0.988), rather than trailing it by ~0.02–0.04. Differences here are small (≤0.005) against only 18 errors, but the reversal itself is informative: it did not occur in any of the 4 prior runs (main run + 3 seeds), all of which had more errors (21–80) at lower accuracy (47–86%). This model sits closer to ceiling accuracy, where every signal already discriminates errors well (AUROC > 0.98 for all three), leaving little room for token-probability's Section-3.1 advantage to manifest. This partially answers whether the advantage holds at a fuller training budget: it does not obviously scale up, since the higher-accuracy K-ablation model shows the opposite ranking — though the smaller error count here also limits statistical resolution. We report this as a genuine open finding rather than smoothing it into the main narrative.

**Calibration.** ECE is 0.041 (token-probability) and 0.035 (sample-agreement) from the main run; both are reasonably calibrated where confidence > 0.9 (97.9% and 99.3% empirical accuracy respectively) but markedly overconfident in the low-confidence tail (e.g., the [0.5, 0.6) token-probability bin: 8 examples, ~0.57 mean confidence, 0% accuracy). ECE and AUROC answer different questions: a signal can look calibrated in aggregate while being a poor error detector at the decision-relevant threshold.

**Self-consistency vs. greedy accuracy.** Majority-vote accuracy over K=8 was 92.75% in the main run, identical to greedy. Single-forward-pass arithmetic has no analogue to divergent reasoning paths landing on different, better answers (as in multi-step CoT tasks): resampling mostly reproduces the greedy answer or its error mode.

## 4. Discussion

Token probabilities are not secretly better than sampling-based UQ in general — the *mechanism* by which sampling helps (resolving paraphrase ambiguity, aggregating divergent reasoning paths) is absent in a single-canonical-answer task, so sampling adds only variance and compute cost without added signal *when errors are frequent*. Section 3.2 complicates a stronger version of this claim: the token-probability edge is not fixed, but appears tied to the moderate-to-high error-rate regime of Section 3.1 (21–80 errors), and it disappeared (indeed reversed by a small margin) on a lower-error model. A plausible mechanism: near ceiling accuracy, both signal families already separate the few remaining errors well, so which one is nominally "ahead" is closer to measurement noise than a structural advantage; at moderate error rates there is more room for a real gap to show, and it consistently favors token-probability there.

A concrete, testable criterion survives this revision: whole-answer token-probability aggregation is a reasonable default whenever a task (i) has a small/canonical answer space, (ii) has no meaningful multiple-paths-to-the-same-answer structure, and (iii) has a nontrivial error rate — near-ceiling accuracy makes signal choice largely moot regardless of mechanism.

**Toward a positive control.** Whether a task with genuine answer multiplicity flips the ranking toward sampling is still untested, but we can now specify a concrete, judge-free candidate: a **divisor-listing task**, `"n="` → any proper divisor `d` of `n` with `1 < d < n`. Correctness stays exact and checkable (`n % d == 0`), but unlike addition, multiple distinct output strings are simultaneously correct for the same input — genuine semantic multiplicity without an NLI judge. Semantic entropy's clustering would need adapting to cluster by correctness-preserving equivalence rather than raw value equality, itself a useful methodological question. We did not build this within the current revision's budget; it remains the most promising next step.

## 5. Limitations

(1) Model trained from scratch on a synthetic task rather than a pretrained LLM on natural language, due to no network access in this environment; unverified on real alignment-induced miscalibration, and Semantic Entropy Probes are not evaluated. (2) The multi-seed check (3.1) uses only 3 seeds and reduced training budgets — enough to show the sign of the gap is consistent, not enough for a formal significance test. (3) The K-ablation (3.2) resolves the previous version's discarded attempt but is a single model/seed — not paired across the same seeds as 3.1, so we cannot cleanly attribute the observed ranking reversal to accuracy level versus other seed-to-seed variation; a K-ablation crossed with the 3-seed sweep would disambiguate this. (4) Exact integer equality is a best-case semantic-equivalence oracle (no NLI-judge noise); with model-based equivalence checking, semantic entropy's relative behavior may not transfer. (5) The divisor-listing positive-control task proposed in Section 4 remains unbuilt — the single most valuable remaining follow-up.

## 6. Conclusion

In a controlled, judge-free setting built to remove the paraphrase and multi-path-reasoning effects motivating sampling-based UQ, whole-answer token-probability signals matched or exceeded semantic entropy and self-consistency at moderate error rates (main run and 3-seed check, 21–80 errors), and self-consistency gave zero accuracy lift over greedy decoding. This revision's K-sample ablation (K=8/16/32) shows the sampling budget itself is not the missing variable — more samples don't change the ranking — but surfaces a genuine open finding: on a lower-error model (18/150 errors), the ranking mildly reverses, suggesting the token-probability advantage is concentrated in regimes with enough errors to matter and may not be a universal property of single-canonical-answer tasks. Combined with a concrete, judge-free positive-control design (divisor-listing) not yet built, this sharpens rather than resolves the paper's scope: reported sampling-based gains likely depend on both semantic multiplicity/divergent reasoning *and* the base error rate, and isolating these two variables jointly is the natural next step.
