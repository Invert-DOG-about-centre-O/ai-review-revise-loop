# A Sharp Phase Transition: How AI Sycophancy Collapses Human–AI Team Accuracy at a Critical, Skill-Dependent, Mechanism-Dependent Threshold

## Abstract

AI systems that soften or shift their stated recommendations to match a user's expressed belief — *sycophancy* — are widely reported in deployed LLMs, but empirical work mostly measures sycophancy as a static, single-turn property rather than tracing it through the *multi-round feedback loop* of real human–AI decision-making. We build a lightweight, reproducible simulation grounding an AI "advisor" in a real trained classifier (logistic regression on the UCI/sklearn breast-cancer dataset, 94.7% held-out accuracy), coupled to a synthetic human with adjustable skill, a disagreement-triggered sycophantic-shift parameter λ, and a reinforcement-learning trust update. With a *binary* human belief and *linear probability blending*, team accuracy is flat near baseline for λ<0.5, then collapses at λ=0.5 (low-skill accuracy 0.91→0.54) — a threshold forced exactly at 0.5 by the algebra of blending toward a binary label. Because that made the finding look designed-in, we ran three follow-up checks. **First**, replacing the binary belief with a graded confidence signal, the collapse survives but its location becomes skill-dependent, and at fine λ-resolution (Δλ=0.01) it is a narrow band (width 0.01–0.02), not a literal point — we now call it a *narrow-band transition*. **Second**, the skill-ordering of thresholds (low≥medium≥high) survives two alternative concave/convex confidence-mapping functions, though the absolute locations shift. **Third**, and most consequentially, replacing linear probability-blending with logit-space (sigmoid) blending — arguably the more natural way to combine two probabilistic beliefs — the sharp transition **disappears**, replaced by a smooth, monotonic decline. The headline "phase transition" is thus not a universal property of any bounded-signal blend, as we previously argued, but is specific to blending in *probability space*. We report all three findings, including the one that complicates our original claim.

## 1. Introduction

Large language models trained with RLHF exhibit a well-documented tendency to shift outputs toward a user's stated beliefs rather than ground truth — *sycophancy* [Sharma et al. 2023]. Empirical audits quantify how often this occurs (SycEval finds sycophantic behavior in over half of examined cases [SycEval, 2025]) and how it shapes trust and perceived authenticity (Sun et al., CHI 2026; "When Flattery Backfires," CHI 2026). "Does Sycophancy Change Decisions?" (CHI 2026) shows effects on AI-assisted decision-making, and "Measuring and Mitigating Overreliance" (2025) notes that little is known about how sycophancy interacts with *repeated*, risk-bearing decisions. A recurring gap: sycophancy is usually measured single-shot, while real deployments are iterated — trust accrues from repeated outcomes and governs how much damage sycophancy does.

We build a controlled simulation (not a live LLM, out of scope for our compute budget and confounded by prompt effects): a real trained classifier stands in for an AI advisor; a synthetic human with tunable skill supplies an independent judgment; sycophancy is a continuously-tunable parameter λ; trust updates via a standard reinforcement rule. Our first pass (§4.1) reproduces a striking discontinuity at λ=0.5, a direct algebraic consequence of encoding the human's belief as a binary label — a natural objection is that this is a designed-in property of our own equation. Round 1 review asked us to test this with a continuous belief signal; we did (§4.2), and the transition survived but moved. Round 2 pushed further on exactly the axes that could still make the finding an artifact: (a) is "discontinuous" the right word at finer λ resolution, (b) is the confidence→threshold mapping specific to our particular linear formula, and (c) does the transition survive a different, more natural blending mechanism. We ran all three (§4.3–4.5). The third overturns part of our own headline claim, and we report that honestly rather than keeping only the results that confirmed the story.

**Contributions.** (1) A minimal, reproducible simulation testbed coupling a real classifier to a parametrized human–AI trust loop, released with code and raw results. (2) A binary-belief protocol reproducing a sharp, algebraically-forced threshold at λ=0.5. (3) A continuous-belief ablation showing the transition survives but shifts with human confidence, and at fine resolution is a **narrow band** (width ≈0.01–0.02), not a point. (4) A confidence-map sensitivity check showing the skill-ordering of thresholds is robust to the functional form linking skill to stated confidence. (5) A blend-mechanism check showing the transition is **not** mechanism-independent: it appears under linear probability-blending but is replaced by a smooth decline under logit-space blending.

## 2. Related Work

**Sycophancy measurement.** SycEval (2025) and related audits quantify sycophancy rates via single-exchange challenge prompts; we instead treat sycophancy strength as a controllable dial for a dose–response study across repeated rounds.

**Trust and reliance in human–AI teams.** "Be Friendly, Not Friends" (CHI 2026), "When Flattery Backfires" (CHI 2026), and "Does Sycophancy Change Decisions?" (CHI 2026) show sycophancy moves trust and decisions in real chat systems but cannot easily sweep an intensity parameter or run thousands of trials to find non-linear regimes — the gap our simulation fills.

**Overreliance and calibration.** "Measuring and Mitigating Overreliance" (2025) and "Calibrated Trust in Dealing with LLM Hallucinations" (2025) argue current risk frameworks neglect human factors; we adopt their operational definition of overreliance directly as a metric.

**Agent-based simulation.** Simulation is established for feedback loops in socio-technical AI systems (e.g., recommender polarization). We are, to our knowledge, first to simulate the sycophancy-trust loop with a real grounded classifier, an explicit trust-update rule, *and* systematic checks of whether the headline non-linearity survives changes to belief representation and blending mechanism.

## 3. Method

### 3.1 Grounding the AI in a real classifier
Logistic regression on sklearn/UCI Breast Cancer Wisconsin (569 samples, 30 features), 60/40 stratified split (seed 0). Test accuracy on 228 held-out cases: **94.7%**, used as the AI-alone baseline.

### 3.2 Simulated human judgment
Per-case difficulty is inversely related to AI confidence (`difficulty = 1 − |2·p_AI − 0.5|`). Each human has a skill parameter — peak accuracy on easy cases — of 0.55 (low), 0.70 (medium), 0.85 (high); accuracy degrades linearly toward chance as difficulty rises. Correctness is drawn per case from this curve.

### 3.3 Belief representations
**Binary (main).** `h ∈ {0,1}`, correct with probability from the skill curve.
**Continuous.** `h_prob = 0.5 ± 0.5·f(acc)` (sign matches correctness), where `f` is a confidence map. Our default is `f(acc)=acc` (linear); §4.4 also tests `f(acc)=√acc` (concave) and `f(acc)=acc²` (convex). This keeps right/wrong rate identical to the binary version while varying only how tentative the stated confidence is.

### 3.4 Sycophantic two-round advice protocol
Round 1: `r0 = 1[p_AI > 0.5]`. On disagreement, the AI's round-2 probability shifts toward the human's belief. **Linear blend (main):** `p_shifted = (1−λ)·p_AI + λ·belief`. **Logit blend (§4.5):** convert `p_AI` and `belief` to log-odds, blend `(1−λ)·logit_AI + λ·logit_belief`, then apply the sigmoid to recover `p_shifted`. Either way, `r1 = 1[p_shifted > 0.5]`; λ=0 honest, λ=1 fully sycophantic; no shift when human and AI already agree.

### 3.5 Trust-adaptive team decision
Trust starts at 0.5, updates via `trust ← trust + α·(𝟙[r1 correct] − trust)`, α=0.15. The human follows `r1` with probability `trust`, else their own belief.

### 3.6 Sweeps
Coarse grid (main + first ablation): λ ∈ {0,0.1,...,0.5,0.55,0.6,0.7,0.85,1.0} × skill × 30 seeds (228 cases each). Fine grid (§4.3): λ ∈ [0.40, 0.84] step 0.01, continuous belief, 30 seeds. Confidence-map and blend-mechanism checks (§4.4–4.5): λ ∈ {0.4,...,1.0} step 0.05, 30 seeds. All runs finish in a few seconds on CPU.

## 4. Results

Code: `sim.py`, `ablation_continuous.py`, and new this revision, `ablation_v3.py` (fine-resolution, confidence-map, and blend-mechanism checks). Raw outputs: `results.csv`, `results_seed_level.csv`, `results_continuous_ablation.csv`, `results_fine_continuous.csv`, `results_sigmoid_blend.csv`, `run_log.txt`, `run_log_v3.txt`, `fig_main.png`, `fig_flips.png`.

### 4.1 Binary protocol: a sharp threshold, not a gradual slope
For λ≤0.45, team accuracy is flat near the 94.7% AI-alone baseline for all skill levels. At **λ=0.5**, it collapses: high-skill 0.949→0.821, medium 0.930→0.687, low 0.906→0.543; flat again for λ≥0.55. This follows directly from §3.4: since `h` is binary, once λ>0.5 the sign of `λ·h` alone fixes `p_shifted`'s side of 0.5, regardless of λ's exact value — the human's belief fully overrides the AI on disagreement. Overreliance jumps from 2.2–3.7% to 13.6/20.4/23.7% (high/medium/low). Trust-calibration error rises 2.5× (high) to 8.5× (low) — skill-dependent, not a fixed multiple. Harmful flips jump to 43% (low skill) at threshold; beneficial flips stay ≤3.6%.

### 4.2 Continuous ablation: does the threshold survive graded belief?
Replacing `h` with `h_prob`, the transition survives but its location depends on skill: high-skill collapses near λ≈0.5–0.55, medium ≈0.55–0.6, low ≈0.6–0.65 (team accuracy 0.923→0.641 by λ=0.7). The drop is smaller than in the binary case (e.g., low-skill: ≈0.26 vs ≈0.36), since a graded belief only partially overrides the AI even past threshold.

### 4.3 Is it really a discontinuity? Fine-resolution sweep
Round 2 review correctly noted our coarse grid (steps of 0.05–0.15) cannot distinguish a true jump from a fast multi-step decline. We reran the continuous-belief protocol at Δλ=0.01 over λ∈[0.40,0.84] (`ablation_v3.py`, part A) and located each transition as the λ-range over which team accuracy drops by more than 0.03 in a single 0.01 step:

| skill | transition window (λ) | width |
|---|---|---|
| high | 0.53 → 0.55 | 0.02 |
| medium | 0.58 → 0.59 | 0.01 |
| low | 0.63 → 0.65 | 0.02 |

The transition is real and narrow (1–2 hundredths of λ, versus a full sweep range of 1.0) — visually and practically still a "cliff," and clearly *sharper* than the binary case's own smoothing across seeds — but it is a **narrow band**, not a mathematical point. We adopt "narrow-band transition" as the precise term going forward and report width as a first-class quantity: width appears roughly stable (0.01–0.02) across skill levels, while *location* is what scales with skill (§4.2), so skill affects where the cliff is, not obviously how sharp it is.

### 4.4 Sensitivity of the confidence map
The λ≈0.5/0.55/0.6 (high/medium/low) progression in §4.2 used a specific linear map `f(acc)=acc`. We reran the coarse sweep with `f(acc)=√acc` (concave — even a middling human states relatively high confidence) and `f(acc)=acc²` (convex — only a highly skilled human states high confidence), holding everything else fixed:

| map | low | medium | high |
|---|---|---|---|
| linear | 0.60 | 0.55 | 0.50 |
| sqrt (concave) | 0.55 | 0.50 | 0.50 |
| square (convex) | 0.75 | 0.65 | 0.55 |

The absolute thresholds shift substantially with the functional form — the convex map pushes low-skill collapse out to λ=0.75 — but the **ordering low≥medium≥high holds in all three maps**. This is the load-bearing robustness result: the qualitative claim ("more tentative stated confidence delays collapse") does not depend on the specific linear formula we picked, even though the exact numbers do.

### 4.5 Does the transition survive a different blending mechanism?
This is the most important new result. Linear probability-blending (`p_shifted=(1−λ)p_AI+λ·belief`) is one of several plausible ways to combine two probabilistic signals; logit-space blending — averaging log-odds, then squashing — is arguably more natural, since it treats both signals as evidence rather than raw probabilities. We reran the coarse sweep with the logit blend (`ablation_v3.py`, part C; §3.4):

Team accuracy declines *smoothly* across the full λ range for all three skill levels — e.g. low-skill: 0.923 (λ=0.4) → 0.912 (0.6) → 0.896 (0.7) → 0.810 (0.8) → 0.641 (1.0); high-skill: 0.937→0.934→0.909→0.855 (λ=0.85–1.0, flat). No single-step drop anywhere exceeds our 0.03 narrow-band criterion by more than a small margin, and there is no visually distinct cliff comparable to §4.1 or §4.3. **The sharp transition does not survive the switch to logit-space blending.** This directly overturns our previous claim (v2, §4.2) that sharp collapse is "a structural property of blending a continuous AI signal with *any* human signal bounded away from indecision." It is not any blend — it is specifically **blending in probability space** that produces a narrow-band transition; blending the same information in **log-odds space** removes it and yields graceful degradation instead.

## 5. Discussion and Limitations

This is a controlled simulation, not a study of a deployed LLM. The most important update from this revision is negative: what we previously called a structural, mechanism-independent property is in fact mechanism-*dependent*. This is a more defensible finding than the original — it says the sharpness of sycophancy-induced collapse is diagnostic of *how* an AI system internally combines its own belief with a user's pushback, not just *how much* it caves. A system that blends in probability space is far more brittle (narrow-band collapse) than one that blends in log-odds space (graceful decline), even at identical average sycophancy rates — a distinction current single-number sycophancy audits (SycEval-style rates) cannot detect. We do not know which blending geometry (if either) resembles how real RLHF-tuned LLMs internally revise a stated probability under social pressure; that mapping remains untested and is the natural next step, ideally with logit- or probability-level outputs from a real model rather than only the final token. We use a single dataset/classifier (breast-cancer, LR) and a single trust-update rule (exponential reinforcement, not Bayesian); the confidence-map and blend checks, though now run at three variants each, do not exhaustively cover the space of plausible functional forms. The mapping from λ or the logit-blend weight to a real, measurable "sycophancy intensity" in deployed LLMs remains speculative — none of the audits we cite report a directly comparable statistic to calibrate against.

## 6. Conclusion

Grounding an AI advisor in a real classifier and coupling it to a synthetic human via an explicit sycophancy parameter and reinforcement-learned trust, we find that binary-belief, probability-space blending produces a sharp, algebraically-forced collapse at λ=0.5. A continuous-belief ablation shows this survives with a skill-dependent location, and at fine resolution it is a genuine but narrow (width ≈0.01–0.02) transition band rather than a mathematical point. The skill-ordering of collapse locations is robust to the specific confidence-mapping function. Critically, the transition is **not** robust to the blending mechanism: switching from probability-space to logit-space blending replaces the cliff with a smooth decline. The revised, falsifiable claim is narrower and sharper than before: expect narrow-band collapse specifically where an AI combines beliefs by linear probability-averaging, and expect graceful degradation instead where it combines them as log-odds evidence — a distinction real sycophancy audits could test by probing a model's internal confidence trajectory, not just its final answers. Code, raw results, and figures are included for reproduction and extension.
