# When Does a Language Model's Softmax Tell the Truth? A Controlled Study of Aleatoric Calibration, Epistemic Contamination, and the Sample Cost of Entropy Estimation

## Abstract

Uncertainty quantification (UQ) for large language models (LLMs) is usually evaluated indirectly, via downstream hallucination-detection AUROC, because the *true* generating distribution behind a natural-language answer is unknown. This makes it hard to separate two effects that the recent UQ literature explicitly calls out as conflated: (1) whether a model's predictive distribution is *calibrated to genuine randomness in the task* (aleatoric uncertainty), and (2) whether that distribution is merely distorted by *how much the model has seen* (epistemic exposure). We sidestep this identifiability problem by training a small transformer language model from scratch on a fully synthetic key→value task whose generating process we design and therefore know exactly: some keys map deterministically to one value, others map stochastically to a known categorical distribution, and every key is exposed to the model a controlled number of times during training (0, 3, 20, or 150 occurrences). This lets us directly measure calibration error (KL divergence to the *true* distribution) as a function of training frequency, rather than relying on a proxy. Because a single training run has only 5-10 keys per (type × tier) cell, we re-ran the entire pipeline — key/tier assignment, training, and all three experiments — under 8 independent seeds to check robustness. Across seeds we find (a) calibration error falls by roughly three orders of magnitude with training frequency for deterministic keys (mean ratio 2422×, range 1420-3611×) and about two orders for stochastic keys (71×, range 42-105×); (b) unseen deterministic keys reliably show high, spurious predictive entropy (mean 0.98 ± 0.13 nats) in the same range as genuine aleatoric entropy (0.898 nats), confirming raw entropy conflates "genuinely random" with "rarely seen" as a robust, not seed-specific, effect; (c) analytic entropy is exact by construction, while Monte-Carlo sampling needs ~100-400 samples to approach it; and (d) entropy-based error detection on deterministic keys averages AUROC 0.96 ± 0.04 (range 0.91-1.00), still driven mostly by the unseen tier. We also tested, and refute, an alternative explanation we had floated for why unseen-stochastic-key entropy looks close to the true value: it is not because the model's global marginal over value tokens collapses toward the task's aleatoric entropy — the global marginal entropy averages 2.00 ± 0.03 nats, more than double the 0.898-nat target, so the near-match is a coincidence of this particular design, not a predictable artifact. All code, logs, per-seed results, and raw per-key results are included.

## 1. Introduction

Probabilistic methods are central to how LLMs are built (maximum-likelihood pretraining, RLHF as approximate posterior shaping) and to how their outputs are trusted (calibration, uncertainty quantification, selective prediction). A recent wave of work has focused on hallucination detection via *semantic entropy* — sampling multiple generations, clustering them by meaning, and using the entropy of the resulting cluster distribution as an uncertainty signal (Kuhn et al., 2023; Farquhar et al., 2024). Follow-up work has tried to make this cheaper by probing hidden states directly instead of sampling (Semantic Entropy Probes; Kossen et al., 2024) or by using logit-level "semantic energy" instead of post-softmax entropy (2025). In parallel, a separate line of survey work has proposed taxonomies that split LLM uncertainty into input, reasoning, parameter, and prediction components (Liu et al., 2025; Shorinwa et al., 2025), and recent papers explicitly ask whether LLMs can express *calibrated probabilities* for genuinely stochastic scenarios as opposed to merely expressing *confidence* about deterministic facts ("Certain but not Probable?", 2025) — precisely the aleatoric/epistemic distinction we study here.

A recurring difficulty in this literature is that almost all evaluation happens on real text, where the ground-truth generating distribution of "the correct answer" is unknown and is itself estimated from the same model's samples or from a small set of human references. This makes claims like "the model is well calibrated" or "entropy correlates with hallucination" hard to disentangle from confounds like topic frequency, annotator disagreement, or paraphrase-clustering error.

**Gap addressed.** We could not find work that isolates aleatoric calibration from epistemic exposure in a fully controlled generative process while training an actual (small) transformer LM end-to-end, rather than analyzing a frozen pretrained model. Doing so lets us ask a sharper version of the "certain vs. probable" question: *given a task element that is genuinely random with a known distribution, does the model's predictive distribution converge to that true distribution as training exposure increases, and does the entropy of an unseen or rarely-seen element look different from — or identical to — the entropy of a genuinely random element? And are these effects robust across random reinitialization, or artifacts of one lucky run?*

**Contributions.**
1. A synthetic key→value generative process with known per-key aleatoric distributions and controlled per-key training frequency (0/3/20/150 exposures), letting us compute exact KL divergence between a trained model's predictive distribution and ground truth.
2. An empirical demonstration, replicated across 8 independent seeds, that low exposure inflates predictive entropy for *deterministic* keys into the same range as genuine aleatoric entropy — direct evidence for the conflation problem motivating "aleatoric vs. epistemic" taxonomies, shown to be stable rather than a single-run artifact.
3. A direct, controlled measurement of the sample-efficiency cost of Monte-Carlo entropy estimation relative to exact analytic entropy from a single forward pass.
4. A negative result: we test and refute our own initial "coincidental collapse toward the global prior" explanation for unseen-stochastic-key entropy, using the model's actual global marginal distribution.
5. Full runnable code, per-seed and raw per-key results; the single-seed run trains and evaluates in under 30s on CPU, the full 8-seed sweep in under 2 minutes.

## 2. Related Work

**Semantic entropy and sampling-based UQ.** Kuhn et al. (2023) and Farquhar et al. (2024, *Nature*) cluster multiple sampled generations by bidirectional entailment and compute the entropy of the cluster distribution as a hallucination signal; this requires many expensive samples per query. Kossen et al. (2024) train "Semantic Entropy Probes" on hidden states of a *single* generation to approximate this without sampling. A 2025 arXiv paper introduces "Semantic Energy," using penultimate-layer logits instead of post-softmax entropy. Our Experiment B quantifies, where the *exact* target entropy is analytically known, how many samples a black-box Monte-Carlo estimator needs to approach it.

**Calibration and uncertainty taxonomies.** Surveys (Liu et al., 2025; Shorinwa et al., 2025) document that pretrained LMs are reasonably calibrated on next-token log-probabilities for factual content but degrade after instruction-tuning/RLHF, and propose multi-axis taxonomies precisely because a single scalar like entropy conflates distinct sources. "Certain but not Probable?" (2025) studies whether LLM token probabilities reflect genuine outcome probabilities versus epistemic confidence — the closest prior work to ours — but evaluates a frozen pretrained model on natural-language-described scenarios, whereas we train from scratch so both the aleatoric distribution *and* the epistemic exposure schedule are known and controllable, and we can check robustness across reinitializations.

**Frequency/calibration relationships.** Model confidence on rare training instances is known to be unreliable, but this is rarely connected quantitatively, within one controlled and seed-robust experiment, to aleatoric calibration.

## 3. Method

### 3.1 Synthetic generative process
We define 60 "keys" and 8 "values." Each key is assigned a **type** (*deterministic*: probability mass 1.0 on one value; or *stochastic*: a categorical distribution with probabilities {0.6, 0.3, 0.1} randomly permuted over 3 candidate values; types alternate, 30 keys each) and a **frequency tier** (`unseen`=0, `rare`=3, `medium`=20, `frequent`=150 training occurrences; roughly 15 keys per tier, balanced across types). Training sequences have the fixed format `BOS key SEP value EOS` (length 5); each occurrence samples a value from the key's true distribution independently, yielding 2,595 training sequences.

### 3.2 Model
A tiny causal transformer: token + learned positional embeddings (d_model=64), 2 `TransformerEncoderLayer`s with causal masking (4 heads, feed-forward dim 128), linear output head over 72 tokens, 76,552 parameters. AdamW (lr=3e-3), 25 epochs, batch size 128, next-token cross-entropy, CPU only.

### 3.3 Measurements
For each key we extract the model's **exact** softmax over the 8 value tokens at the position after `SEP` (single forward pass), and compute against the *known* true distribution: KL divergence, total variation distance, and entropy.

**Experiment A (calibration vs. exposure)**: aggregate KL/entropy/argmax-accuracy by (type × frequency tier).
**Experiment B (sampling cost)**: for `medium`/`frequent`-tier keys, compare analytic entropy to empirical entropy from T ∈ {5, 20, 100, 400} categorical samples (30 trials per key per T), reporting MAE/RMSE.
**Experiment C (error detection)**: on deterministic keys, AUROC of predictive entropy and 1 − max-probability for detecting argmax errors.

**Robustness check (new in this revision).** A single training run gives only 5-10 keys per (type × tier) cell and no seed-variance estimate for headline numbers such as the "~1000×" KL reduction or the 0.99 AUROC. We therefore re-ran the *entire* pipeline — random key/tier/distribution assignment, corpus construction, model training, and Experiments A-C — under 8 independent seeds (0-7), and report mean ± population standard deviation across seeds. We also directly tested an explanation we had originally offered only as speculation: that unseen-stochastic-key entropy looks close to true aleatoric entropy because the model's predictions collapse toward its *global* marginal distribution over value tokens. We computed this global marginal (softmax averaged over all 60 keys) and its entropy in every seed.

No external baselines were downloaded (no network access in this environment; see Limitations), so all comparisons are internal.

## 4. Results

Single-seed training converges in ~20s; the full 8-seed robustness sweep (all of Experiments A-C repeated 8×, including retraining) completed in 100.2s total (`multiseed_results.json`), confirming the original 30-second single-run cost was not an outlier requiring exotic settings.

### 4.1 Calibration improves by orders of magnitude with exposure, robustly across seeds

| type | tier | seed-0 KL | 8-seed mean KL | unseen/frequent KL ratio (mean ± std) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| deterministic | unseen | 3.259 | 4.133 ± 0.841 | 2422× ± 708× |
| deterministic | frequent | 0.001 | 0.0018 ± 0.0006 | — |
| stochastic | unseen | 2.594 | 3.452 ± 0.520 | 71× ± 21× |
| stochastic | frequent | 0.042 | 0.0543 ± 0.0229 | — |

The calibration-improves-with-exposure effect is not a single-run artifact: the unseen→frequent KL ratio for deterministic keys stays firmly in the thousands across all 8 seeds (range 1420×-3611×, i.e. consistently "roughly three orders of magnitude," never less than three-digit or more than four-digit), and for stochastic keys stays in the tens (range 42×-105×, consistently "more than one order of magnitude"). The original single-seed numbers (KL 3.26→0.001 for deterministic, 2.59→0.042 for stochastic) sit well within these ranges.

The entropy conflation is similarly stable: across 8 seeds, unseen deterministic keys have mean model entropy 0.983 ± 0.134 nats and unseen stochastic keys have mean model entropy 0.928 ± 0.124 nats, both consistently close to the true aleatoric entropy of 0.898 nats (which is fixed by construction and has zero variance across seeds). An uncertainty consumer looking only at entropy magnitude cannot reliably tell "genuinely random" from "never seen" in *any* of the 8 independent runs, not just the one originally reported.

### 4.2 Monte-Carlo entropy estimation is expensive relative to the analytic alternative

| T (samples) | MAE(entropy) | RMSE(entropy) |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | 0.1431 | 0.2500 |
| 20 | 0.0826 | 0.1339 |
| 100 | 0.0404 | 0.0571 |
| 400 | 0.0220 | 0.0296 |

(Single-seed numbers, as in the original submission; error decays roughly as O(1/√T).) Even at T=400 samples, sampling-based entropy still carries ~0.022 nats of error that the analytic entropy has by construction (0 error, 1 forward pass). This quantifies the motivation behind single-pass approximations like Semantic Entropy Probes: matching one forward pass's precision here needs hundreds of samples.

### 4.3 Entropy detects errors well but with real run-to-run variance, still dominated by the unseen boundary

Across 8 seeds, AUROC(entropy) for detecting argmax errors on deterministic keys averages **0.959 ± 0.036** (range 0.905-1.000), confirming the original 0.992 was on the high end of typical rather than representative of the mean, but the qualitative claim ("entropy-based detection is strong") holds in every seed. In all 8 seeds, deterministic keys in the `rare`/`medium`/`frequent` tiers were recovered at or near perfect argmax accuracy, and errors concentrated in the `unseen` tier — so AUROC is still driven predominantly by the seen/unseen boundary rather than a graded relationship across exposure levels, though the seed-to-seed AUROC spread (0.905-1.000) itself hints at some finer-grained variability we did not have statistical power to characterize further within the time budget. Testing this with a harder task (more values per key, noisier stochastic assignment) remains a natural next step, as flagged in the original submission.

### 4.4 The "global prior collapse" explanation for unseen-stochastic entropy is refuted

The original submission speculated that unseen stochastic keys' model entropy (0.888, close to H_true=0.898) reflected "coincidental collapse toward the model's global prior over value tokens" rather than genuine calibration — but left this untested. We now compute the actual global marginal directly: averaged over all 60 keys and all 8 seeds, the model's mean predictive distribution has entropy **2.003 ± 0.026 nats**, more than double the 0.898-nat aleatoric target and closer to the near-uniform entropy over 8 values (ln 8 = 2.079). This refutes the specific "collapse toward global prior" mechanism: if unseen-key predictions were simply defaulting to the global marginal, their entropy would cluster near 2.0 nats, not near 0.9. The near-match between unseen-stochastic-key entropy and true aleatoric entropy is therefore better described as a coincidence of this task's specific design (3-of-8 candidate values with a 0.6/0.3/0.1 split) than as a predictable byproduct of any global-prior mechanism — a useful negative result for anyone tempted to use "distance from the global marginal" as an epistemic-uncertainty proxy in this kind of setup.

## 5. Discussion and Limitations

The central empirical finding — replicated across 8 independent seeds — is that a model's raw predictive entropy is not a reliable signal of *aleatoric* uncertainty on its own: an unseen deterministic key produces entropy in the same range as a genuinely stochastic key, even though their ground-truth uncertainty is 0 vs. 0.898 nats respectively, and this holds whether we look at one training run or eight. Distinguishing them requires either (i) an auxiliary epistemic signal (e.g., training frequency, retrieval confidence, ensemble disagreement) or (ii) comparison to the *shape* of the distribution via KL/TV against a reference, which remains far more discriminative than entropy alone. This directly supports multi-axis uncertainty taxonomies from recent surveys and gives a reproducible, seed-robust demonstration of *why* entropy-only UQ conflates the axes they try to separate.

**Limitations.** (1) No network access was available, so we could not benchmark a real pretrained LLM or semantic-entropy pipeline; our model is trained from scratch on a synthetic task, which isolates the phenomena cleanly but does not by itself establish effect sizes on natural language. (2) The task uses single-token predictions, so multi-token "semantic clustering" (a key ingredient of real semantic entropy) is not exercised — our Monte-Carlo experiment is a lower bound on the real sampling cost. (3) The 8-seed sweep strengthens confidence in the qualitative trends and gives explicit variance estimates, but per-seed per-cell n is still only 5-10 keys; more keys per cell (at proportionally higher compute) would tighten these ranges further. (4) We did not test post-hoc calibration (e.g. temperature scaling) as a repair for unseen-key entropy inflation, nor run the harder/noisier task needed for graded (rather than step-function) AUROC within seen tiers — both remain natural next steps.

## 6. Conclusion

Using a fully controlled synthetic generative process with known aleatoric distributions and known per-item training exposure, replicated across 8 independent seeds, we show that (a) a small transformer's predictive distribution converges toward the true generating distribution with more exposure, with a consistent 1000×-scale (deterministic) or 10×-100×-scale (stochastic) calibration-error reduction from 0 to 150 exposures in every seed; (b) entropy alone cannot reliably distinguish "genuinely random" from "rarely seen," since unseen deterministic items produce entropy comparable to genuinely stochastic items in all 8 runs (mean AUROC for error detection 0.96 ± 0.04); (c) Monte-Carlo sampling-based entropy estimation requires hundreds of samples to match the precision available for free from a single forward pass; and (d) a plausible-sounding mechanistic explanation we initially offered — collapse toward the global marginal — is directly falsified by measuring that marginal, illustrating the value of testing rather than merely asserting explanations even in small controlled studies. All code (`experiment.py`, `multiseed_experiment.py`), logs, and raw per-key/per-seed results are provided for inspection and re-running.
