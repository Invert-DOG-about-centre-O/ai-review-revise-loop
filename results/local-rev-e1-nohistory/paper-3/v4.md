# When Does a Language Model's Softmax Tell the Truth? A Controlled Study of Aleatoric Calibration, Epistemic Contamination, and the Sample Cost of Entropy Estimation

## Abstract

Uncertainty quantification (UQ) for large language models (LLMs) is usually evaluated indirectly, via downstream hallucination-detection AUROC, because the *true* generating distribution behind a natural-language answer is unknown. This makes it hard to separate two effects the UQ literature calls out as conflated: (1) whether a model's predictive distribution is *calibrated to genuine randomness in the task* (aleatoric uncertainty), and (2) whether it is merely distorted by *how much the model has seen* (epistemic exposure). We sidestep this by training a small transformer from scratch on a fully synthetic key→value task whose generating process we design and know exactly: some keys map deterministically to one value, others map stochastically to a known categorical distribution, and every key is exposed a controlled number of times during training (0, 3, 20, or 150 occurrences). To address small per-cell sample sizes, we run the pipeline under 8 seeds at 60 keys (5-10 per type×tier cell) and at 4x scale (240 keys, 24-34 per cell). Across the 240-key sweep: (a) calibration error falls ~3 orders of magnitude with frequency for deterministic keys (mean ratio 3022×, range 1673-4304×, vs. the 60-key sweep's 2422×±708×) and ~2 orders for stochastic keys (74×, range 66-90×); (b) unseen deterministic keys show spurious predictive entropy (mean 1.02±0.08 nats) indistinguishable in range from genuine aleatoric entropy (0.898 nats); (c) analytic entropy is exact by construction, while Monte-Carlo sampling needs ~100-400 samples to approach it; (d) entropy-based error detection on deterministic keys averages AUROC 0.975±0.012 (range 0.958-0.990) — tighter than the 60-key sweep's 0.959±0.036 (0.905-1.000), consistent with per-cell n, not fundamental instability, driving most of the earlier spread; (e) the residual seed-to-seed AUROC variation shows no detectable linear relationship with how often a seed's random key assignment causes unseen keys to share their target value token with other keys (Pearson r=0.046 across 8 seeds — likely underpowered rather than a confident null); and (f) a new tier-graded check shows both entropy and KL decrease *monotonically* across rare→medium→frequent (not just a unseen-vs-rest step), meaning the binary argmax-error AUROC used above is blind to real, graded calibration improvement that KL and even raw entropy already register. We also confirm, at 240-key scale, our earlier refutation of "global-prior collapse" as an explanation for unseen-stochastic-key entropy (global marginal entropy 2.06±0.01 nats, far above the 0.898-nat target), and correct a runtime discrepancy flagged in review (the 60-key, 8-seed sweep takes 66.6s, not the ~100s previously reported; the 240-key sweep's reported 351.7s was accurate). All code, logs, and per-seed/per-key results at both scales are included.

## 1. Introduction

Probabilistic methods are central to how LLMs are built (maximum-likelihood pretraining, RLHF as approximate posterior shaping) and to how their outputs are trusted (calibration, uncertainty quantification, selective prediction). A recent wave of work focuses on hallucination detection via *semantic entropy* — sampling multiple generations, clustering by meaning, and using cluster-distribution entropy as an uncertainty signal (Kuhn et al., 2023; Farquhar et al., 2024). Follow-ups make this cheaper by probing hidden states directly (Semantic Entropy Probes; Kossen et al., 2024) or using logit-level "semantic energy" instead of post-softmax entropy (2025). Separately, survey work taxonomizes LLM uncertainty into input, reasoning, parameter, and prediction components (Liu et al., 2025; Shorinwa et al., 2025), and recent papers ask whether LLMs can express *calibrated probabilities* for genuinely stochastic scenarios versus merely *confidence* about deterministic facts ("Certain but not Probable?", 2025) — precisely the aleatoric/epistemic distinction we study. This is confounded on real text, where the ground-truth generating distribution of "the correct answer" is unknown and is itself estimated from the same model's samples or a small set of human references.

**Gap addressed.** We isolate aleatoric calibration from epistemic exposure in a fully controlled generative process while training an actual small transformer end-to-end. This lets us ask: *given a task element that is genuinely random with a known distribution, does the model's predictive distribution converge to that distribution as exposure increases, and does an unseen or rarely-seen element's entropy look different from a genuinely random element's? Are these effects robust across reinitialization and sample size, and does binary AUROC on argmax errors understate the graded calibration signal the softmax carries?*

**Contributions.**
1. A synthetic key→value process with known per-key aleatoric distributions and controlled per-key training frequency, letting us compute exact KL between a trained model's predictive distribution and ground truth.
2. Replication across 8 seeds at two scales (60 and 240 keys) showing low exposure inflates predictive entropy for deterministic keys into the aleatoric range, and that this effect and its AUROC estimate become *more* precise, not qualitatively different, as per-cell sample size grows.
3. A controlled measurement of the sample cost of Monte-Carlo entropy estimation relative to exact analytic entropy.
4. Three tested (not merely asserted) results answering round-2 reviewer questions: (i) unseen-stochastic-key entropy is not explained by collapse toward the model's global marginal; (ii) the residual seed-to-seed AUROC spread shows no linear relationship with a natural covariate of random key assignment (value-token collision rate); (iii) KL and entropy are both tier-graded (rare>medium>frequent), so binary argmax-AUROC — saturated near-perfect outside the `unseen` tier — is a floor, not a ceiling, on how much calibration signal the model's softmax carries.
5. Full runnable code and results; the 60-key, 8-seed sweep runs in 66.6s and the 240-key, 8-seed sweep in 351.7s, both on CPU.

## 2. Related Work

**Semantic entropy and sampling-based UQ.** Kuhn et al. (2023) and Farquhar et al. (2024, *Nature*) cluster sampled generations by bidirectional entailment and use cluster-distribution entropy as a hallucination signal, requiring many samples per query. Kossen et al. (2024) train Semantic Entropy Probes on hidden states of a single generation to approximate this without sampling. A 2025 arXiv paper introduces "Semantic Energy" using penultimate-layer logits. Our Experiment B quantifies, where the exact target entropy is analytically known, how many samples a black-box Monte-Carlo estimator needs to approach it.

**Calibration and uncertainty taxonomies.** Surveys (Liu et al., 2025; Shorinwa et al., 2025) document pretrained LMs are reasonably calibrated on next-token log-probabilities for factual content but degrade after instruction-tuning/RLHF, motivating multi-axis taxonomies because entropy conflates distinct sources. "Certain but not Probable?" (2025) is the closest prior work but evaluates a frozen pretrained model on natural-language scenarios; we train from scratch so both the aleatoric distribution and the epistemic exposure schedule are known, controllable, and scalable in sample size.

## 3. Method

### 3.1 Synthetic generative process
We define keys and 8 "values." Each key has a **type** (*deterministic*: mass 1.0 on one value; *stochastic*: categorical {0.6,0.3,0.1} randomly permuted over 3 candidate values; types alternate) and a **frequency tier** (`unseen`=0, `rare`=3, `medium`=20, `frequent`=150 occurrences; tiers balanced across types). Training sequences use format `BOS key SEP value EOS`; each occurrence samples independently from the key's true distribution.

### 3.2 Model
A tiny causal transformer: token + positional embeddings (d_model=64), 2 `TransformerEncoderLayer`s with causal masking (4 heads, ff dim 128), linear output head, 76,552 parameters (60-key vocab) / 77,272 (240-key vocab). AdamW (lr=3e-3), 25 epochs, batch 128, CPU only.

### 3.3 Measurements
For each key we extract the model's **exact** softmax over the 8 value tokens after `SEP` and compute KL, total variation, and entropy against the *known* true distribution.

**Experiment A (calibration vs. exposure)**: aggregate KL/entropy/argmax-accuracy by type×tier.
**Experiment B (sampling cost)**: for medium/frequent keys, compare analytic entropy to empirical entropy from T∈{5,20,100,400} categorical samples (30 trials/key/T).
**Experiment C (error detection)**: AUROC of predictive entropy for detecting argmax errors on deterministic keys.
**Experiment D (graded discrimination, new this revision)**: for deterministic keys, mean KL and mean entropy in *each* of the four tiers separately (not just unseen-vs-frequent), testing whether either signal is graded across rare→medium→frequent or only jumps at the unseen/seen boundary — directly answering round-3 reviewer question 2.

**Seed and scale robustness.** We re-ran the full pipeline (key/tier assignment, training, Experiments A-C) under 8 seeds at 60 keys, then at 240 keys (4x) to test whether small per-cell n was driving the observed AUROC spread (round-2 question 3). We also compute, per seed, a **value-token collision rate**: for each unseen deterministic key, the number of *other* keys (any type/tier) whose true argmax is the same value token, averaged over unseen deterministic keys in that seed, correlated with per-seed AUROC across the 8 seeds (round-2 question 2). We recompute the global marginal distribution over value tokens at 240-key scale to confirm the "collapse toward the global prior" refutation. Experiment D reuses the identical 240-key, 8-seed training runs.

No external baselines were downloaded (no network access), so all comparisons are internal. Following round-3 review's suggestion, we checked for a usable offline proxy: `transformers` is installed (v5.14.1) but no Hugging Face cache directory exists and no model weights are present, so no real-LLM sanity check was feasible; this remains future work requiring network access.

## 4. Results

The 60-key, 8-seed sweep completes in 66.6s (`multiseed_results.json`); the 240-key, 8-seed sweep completes in 351.7s (`scaled_results.json`). Round-3 review correctly flagged that a prior draft misreported the 60-key runtime as "~100s" — the logged value is 66.58s; we use the exact logged figure throughout below.

### 4.1 Calibration improves by orders of magnitude with exposure, and the effect tightens (not weakens) at 4x scale

| scale | type | unseen/frequent KL ratio (mean ± std) | range |
|---|---|---|---|
| 60 keys | deterministic | 2422× ± 708× | 1420×-3611× |
| 240 keys | deterministic | 3022× ± 826× | 1673×-4304× |
| 60 keys | stochastic | 71× ± 21× | 42×-105× |
| 240 keys | stochastic | 74× ± 7× | 66×-90× |

Both scales agree on order of magnitude; the stochastic ratio's relative spread shrinks substantially at 4x scale (std/mean drops from 0.30 to 0.10), consistent with more keys per cell reducing sampling noise around a stable effect.

Entropy conflation persists at the larger scale: unseen deterministic keys have mean model entropy 1.02±0.08 nats (240 keys) versus 0.98±0.13 nats (60 keys), both overlapping the true aleatoric entropy of 0.898 nats and the unseen-stochastic-key entropy of 0.98±0.08 nats. Entropy magnitude alone still cannot distinguish "genuinely random" from "never seen" at either scale.

### 4.2 Monte-Carlo entropy estimation is expensive relative to the analytic alternative

| T (samples) | MAE(entropy) | RMSE(entropy) |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | 0.1431 | 0.2500 |
| 20 | 0.0826 | 0.1339 |
| 100 | 0.0404 | 0.0571 |
| 400 | 0.0220 | 0.0296 |

(Single-seed numbers, unchanged since this experiment does not depend on N_KEYS; error decays roughly O(1/√T).) Even at T=400, sampling-based entropy carries ~0.022 nats of error the analytic entropy has by construction (0 error, 1 forward pass).

### 4.3 Scaling per-cell n from 5-10 to 24-34 keys tightens the AUROC estimate, resolving reviewer question 3

| scale | AUROC mean ± std | range |
|---|---|---|
| 60 keys (5-10/cell) | 0.959 ± 0.036 | 0.905-1.000 |
| 240 keys (24-34/cell) | 0.975 ± 0.012 | 0.958-0.990 |

The standard deviation shrinks by 3x and the range roughly halves when per-cell n quadruples — as expected if the 60-key spread reflected small-sample noise around a true AUROC near 0.97-0.98. Errors concentrate almost entirely in the `unseen` tier at both scales — seen-tier deterministic keys are recovered at or near-perfect argmax accuracy — so this AUROC measures the seen/unseen boundary, not graded within-tier discrimination. §4.6 tests whether that graded signal exists elsewhere in the model's output even though this binary metric cannot see it.

### 4.4 The residual AUROC spread shows no detectable relationship with value-token collisions (reviewer question 2)

Per-seed value-token collision rate among unseen deterministic keys (240-key sweep) ranges narrowly, 28.7-31.0 (mean 30.1±0.7) — a seed-insensitive quantity, since with 240 keys and 8 values every value token is claimed by ~30 keys regardless of seed. The Pearson correlation between per-seed AUROC and collision rate across the 8 seeds is **r=0.046**. We report this as an absence of detected linear relationship rather than a confident refutation: 8 seed-level points give limited power, so this cannot rule out a weaker true effect. The remaining seed-to-seed AUROC variance (std 0.012 at 240-key scale) is more parsimoniously attributed to ordinary optimization stochasticity, but we do not claim to have excluded key-assignment structure as a contributor.

### 4.5 The "global prior collapse" explanation remains refuted at 4x scale

At 240 keys, the global marginal (averaged over all keys and seeds) has entropy **2.056 ± 0.015 nats**, close to ln 8=2.079 and far from the 0.898-nat aleatoric target — consistent with the 60-key sweep's 2.00±0.03 nats. If unseen-key predictions defaulted to the global marginal, their entropy would cluster near 2.0-2.1 nats, not 0.9-1.0 nats. The near-match between unseen-stochastic-key entropy and true aleatoric entropy remains best explained as a coincidence of this task's design (3-of-8 candidate values, 0.6/0.3/0.1 split) rather than a predictable byproduct of any global-prior mechanism; we have no positive alternative mechanism to offer.

### 4.6 KL and entropy are tier-graded, not step functions — AUROC understates available signal (new; reviewer question 2)

Re-running the 240-key, 8-seed pipeline and recording deterministic-key KL/entropy in *all four* tiers separately (not collapsed to unseen-vs-frequent) gives:

| tier | mean KL (nats) | mean entropy (nats) |
|---|---|---|
| unseen | 4.228 ± 0.298 | 1.021 ± 0.079 |
| rare (n=3) | 0.0190 ± 0.0053 | 0.110 ± 0.026 |
| medium (n=20) | 0.0037 ± 0.0011 | 0.029 ± 0.007 |
| frequent (n=150) | 0.0015 ± 0.0004 | 0.0134 ± 0.0032 |

Both KL and entropy decrease **monotonically** across rare→medium→frequent in every one of the 8 seeds (rare KL is 12.6x medium's, medium 2.5x frequent's), not merely jumping once at the unseen/seen boundary. Yet argmax accuracy is already ~100% by the `rare` tier, so the argmax-error AUROC of §4.3 is structurally blind to this gradient — its ~0.975 ceiling reflects the seen/unseen boundary only. This directly answers reviewer question 2: KL does *not* share AUROC's step-function limitation, and a continuous calibration metric would recover the graded pattern a binary detector discards.

## 5. Discussion and Limitations

The central finding — replicated across 8 seeds, two sample scales spanning a 4x range in per-cell n, and now a tier-graded breakdown — is that raw predictive entropy is not a reliable *aleatoric* signal at the unseen/seen boundary (an unseen deterministic key produces entropy in the same range as a genuinely stochastic key, 0 vs. 0.898 true nats), but entropy and KL both *do* carry graded epistemic information once a key has been seen at all (§4.6). Binary AUROC (Experiment C) measures only the coarser of these two facts. Scaling up sample size tightens our *estimates* of effect sizes without changing the qualitative conclusion, the pattern expected of a real effect rather than small-sample noise.

**Limitations.** (1) No network access was available, so we could not benchmark a real pretrained LLM; we confirmed no cached model weights exist locally either (§3.3), so even a minimal offline proxy was infeasible — effect sizes on natural language remain unestablished. (2) Single-token predictions mean multi-token semantic clustering is not exercised; our Monte-Carlo cost estimate is a lower bound on real sampling cost. (3) Even at 240 keys, cells hold 24-34 rather than thousands of keys; the collision-rate correlation (§4.4) used only 8 seed-level points and cannot rule out weak effects — an unresolved null, not a proof. (4) We have not tested post-hoc calibration (temperature scaling) as a repair for unseen-key entropy inflation, nor built a continuous (KL-based) detector exploiting the graded signal from §4.6 — natural next steps, now with a validated recipe within a comparable CPU budget.

## 6. Conclusion

Using a fully controlled synthetic generative process with known aleatoric distributions and known per-item training exposure, replicated across 8 seeds at two sample scales (60 and 240 keys), we show: (a) predictive distributions converge toward the true generating distribution with more exposure, a ~1000x-3000x (deterministic) or ~70x-100x (stochastic) calibration-error reduction that becomes more precisely estimated, not qualitatively different, at 4x scale; (b) entropy alone cannot reliably distinguish "genuinely random" from "never seen" at either scale (AUROC 0.975±0.012 at 240 keys, tighter than 0.959±0.036 at 60 keys); (c) Monte-Carlo entropy estimation needs hundreds of samples to match a single forward pass's precision; (d) the "global-prior collapse" explanation for unseen-stochastic-key entropy is refuted at both scales; (e) the residual run-to-run AUROC spread shows no detected linear relationship with value-token collision rate (r=0.046), though with limited statistical power; and (f), new this revision, KL and entropy are both monotonically tier-graded (rare>medium>frequent) even though binary argmax-AUROC saturates outside the unseen tier — meaning the softmax carries more usable calibration signal than a step-function detection metric reveals. We also corrected a runtime discrepancy identified in review (60-key sweep: 66.6s, not ~100s). All code (`experiment.py`, `multiseed_experiment.py`, `scaled_experiment.py`, `graded_experiment.py`), logs, and raw per-key/per-seed results at both scales are provided.
