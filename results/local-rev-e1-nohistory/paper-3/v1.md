# When Does a Language Model's Softmax Tell the Truth? A Controlled Study of Aleatoric Calibration, Epistemic Contamination, and the Sample Cost of Entropy Estimation

## Abstract

Uncertainty quantification (UQ) for large language models (LLMs) is usually evaluated indirectly, via downstream hallucination-detection AUROC, because the *true* generating distribution behind a natural-language answer is unknown. This makes it hard to separate two effects that the recent UQ literature explicitly calls out as conflated: (1) whether a model's predictive distribution is *calibrated to genuine randomness in the task* (aleatoric uncertainty), and (2) whether that distribution is merely distorted by *how much the model has seen* (epistemic exposure). We sidestep this identifiability problem by training a small transformer language model from scratch on a fully synthetic key&rarr;value task whose generating process we design and therefore know exactly: some keys map deterministically to one value, others map stochastically to a known categorical distribution, and every key is exposed to the model a controlled number of times during training (0, 3, 20, or 150 occurrences). This lets us directly measure calibration error (KL divergence and total variation distance to the *true* distribution) as a function of training frequency, rather than relying on a proxy. We find that (a) calibration error decreases monotonically and by roughly three orders of magnitude with training frequency for both deterministic and stochastic keys; (b) for unseen deterministic keys the model reports a high, spurious predictive entropy (mean 0.94 nats) that is observationally indistinguishable from genuine aleatoric entropy, confirming that raw entropy conflates "genuinely random" with "rarely seen"; (c) single-forward-pass (analytic) entropy is exact by construction, while Monte-Carlo sampling of the kind used by semantic-entropy methods needs on the order of 100-400 samples to bring its estimation error down to what the analytic quantity gives for free; and (d) entropy-based error detection on deterministic keys reaches AUROC 0.99, but this is driven almost entirely by the unseen tier, not by graded epistemic signal. All code, logs, and raw per-key results are included for inspection.

## 1. Introduction

Probabilistic methods are central to how LLMs are built (maximum-likelihood pretraining, RLHF as approximate posterior shaping) and to how their outputs are trusted (calibration, uncertainty quantification, selective prediction). A recent wave of work has focused on hallucination detection via *semantic entropy* — sampling multiple generations, clustering them by meaning, and using the entropy of the resulting cluster distribution as an uncertainty signal (Kuhn et al., 2023; Farquhar et al., 2024). Follow-up work has tried to make this cheaper by probing hidden states directly instead of sampling (Semantic Entropy Probes; Kossen et al., 2024) or by using logit-level "semantic energy" instead of post-softmax entropy (2025). In parallel, a separate line of survey work has proposed taxonomies that split LLM uncertainty into input, reasoning, parameter, and prediction components (Liu et al., 2025; Shorinwa et al., 2025), and recent papers explicitly ask whether LLMs can express *calibrated probabilities* for genuinely stochastic scenarios as opposed to merely expressing *confidence* about deterministic facts ("Certain but not Probable?", 2025) — precisely the aleatoric/epistemic distinction we study here.

A recurring difficulty in this literature is that almost all evaluation happens on real text, where the ground-truth generating distribution of "the correct answer" is unknown and is itself estimated from the same model's samples or from a small set of human references. This makes claims like "the model is well calibrated" or "entropy correlates with hallucination" hard to disentangle from confounds like topic frequency, annotator disagreement, or paraphrase-clustering error.

**Gap addressed.** We could not find work that isolates aleatoric calibration from epistemic exposure in a fully controlled generative process while training an actual (small) transformer LM end-to-end, rather than analyzing a frozen pretrained model. Doing so lets us ask a sharper version of the "certain vs. probable" question: *given a task element that is genuinely random with a known distribution, does the model's predictive distribution converge to that true distribution as training exposure increases, and does the entropy of an unseen or rarely-seen element look different from — or identical to — the entropy of a genuinely random element?*

**Contributions.**
1. A synthetic key→value generative process with known per-key aleatoric distributions and controlled per-key training frequency (0/3/20/150 exposures), letting us compute exact KL divergence and TV distance between a trained model's predictive distribution and ground truth.
2. An empirical demonstration that low exposure inflates predictive entropy for *deterministic* keys in a way that is quantitatively similar in magnitude to genuine aleatoric entropy — direct evidence for the conflation problem motivating "aleatoric vs. epistemic" taxonomies in the survey literature.
3. A direct, controlled measurement of the sample-efficiency cost of Monte-Carlo entropy estimation (the core primitive of semantic-entropy-style UQ) relative to the exact analytic entropy available from a single forward pass, quantifying the motivation behind cheaper single-pass alternatives (e.g., Semantic Entropy Probes).
4. Full runnable code and raw results, since the entire experiment trains and evaluates in under 30 seconds on CPU.

## 2. Related Work

**Semantic entropy and sampling-based UQ.** Kuhn et al. (2023) and Farquhar et al. (2024, *Nature*) cluster multiple sampled generations by bidirectional entailment and compute the entropy of the cluster distribution as a hallucination signal; this requires many expensive samples per query. Kossen et al. (2024) train "Semantic Entropy Probes" on hidden states of a *single* generation to approximate this quantity without sampling. A 2025 arXiv paper introduces "Semantic Energy," arguing that post-softmax entropy under-uses information available in the penultimate-layer logits. Our Experiment B quantifies, in a setting where the *exact* target entropy is analytically known, how many samples a black-box Monte-Carlo estimator needs to approach that exact value — giving a concrete number to the intuition motivating these cheaper alternatives.

**Calibration and uncertainty taxonomies.** Surveys (Liu et al., 2025; Shorinwa et al., 2025; the KDD 2025 tutorial survey) document that pretrained LMs are reasonably calibrated on next-token log-probabilities for factual content, but that calibration degrades after instruction-tuning/RLHF, and propose multi-axis taxonomies (input/reasoning/parameter/prediction uncertainty) precisely because a single scalar like entropy conflates distinct sources. "Certain but not Probable?" (2025) studies whether LLM token probabilities reflect genuine outcome probabilities in explicitly probabilistic scenarios (e.g., dice rolls) versus merely reflecting epistemic confidence, which is the closest prior work to our setup — but it evaluates a frozen pretrained model on natural-language-described probabilistic scenarios, whereas we train a model from scratch on a fully synthetic task so that both the aleatoric distribution *and* the epistemic exposure schedule are exactly known and independently controllable.

**Frequency/calibration relationships.** It is well documented in the broader calibration literature (e.g., work on memorization and long-tail generalization) that model confidence on rare training instances is unreliable, but this is rarely connected quantitatively, within one controlled experiment, to the aleatoric-calibration question — we do so directly via KL divergence to a *known* ground-truth categorical distribution.

## 3. Method

### 3.1 Synthetic generative process
We define 60 "keys" and 8 "values." Each key is assigned:
- **Type**: *deterministic* (probability mass 1.0 on one value) or *stochastic* (a categorical distribution with probabilities {0.6, 0.3, 0.1} randomly permuted over 3 candidate values). Types alternate, giving 30 keys of each type.
- **Frequency tier**: `unseen` (0 training occurrences), `rare` (3), `medium` (20), or `frequent` (150), assigned so each tier contains roughly 15 keys, balanced across types.

Training sequences have the fixed format `BOS key SEP value EOS` (length 5); each key's training occurrences are generated by sampling a value from its true distribution independently each time. This yields 2,595 training sequences total.

### 3.2 Model
A tiny causal transformer: token + learned positional embeddings (d_model=64), 2 `TransformerEncoderLayer`s with causal masking (4 heads, feed-forward dim 128), and a linear output head over the 72-token vocabulary. Total 76,552 parameters. Trained with AdamW (lr=3e-3) for 25 epochs, batch size 128, standard next-token cross-entropy loss, entirely on CPU.

### 3.3 Measurements
For each key, we extract the model's **exact** softmax distribution over the 8 value tokens at the position immediately after `SEP` (a single forward pass — no sampling needed since prediction is one token). We compute, against the *known* true distribution: KL divergence KL(true‖model), total variation distance, and entropy of both distributions.

**Experiment A (calibration vs. exposure)**: aggregate KL/TV/entropy/argmax-accuracy by (type × frequency tier).

**Experiment B (sampling cost)**: for keys in the `medium`/`frequent` tiers, we compare the *analytic* entropy (exact, one forward pass) to an *empirical* entropy estimated from T ∈ {5, 20, 100, 400} categorical samples drawn from the model's own distribution (30 repeated trials per key per T), reporting mean absolute error and RMSE of the sampled estimate relative to the analytic value. This directly measures the cost that black-box/sampling-based semantic-entropy-style methods pay relative to a white-box single-pass estimate.

**Experiment C (error detection)**: restricted to deterministic keys (where "error" is unambiguous — any deviation from the single true value is wrong), we compute AUROC of two single-pass uncertainty scores (predictive entropy; 1 − max softmax probability) for distinguishing keys where the model's argmax differs from the true value.

No external baselines were downloaded (network access to model hubs was unavailable in this environment; see Limitations), so all comparisons are internal: analytic vs. Monte-Carlo entropy, and calibration across frequency tiers, both computed on the same trained model.

## 4. Results

Training converged in 20.2s (loss 1.91 → 0.90 over 25 epochs); full run including evaluation took 29.4s (log: `results.log`, raw numbers: `results.json`).

### 4.1 Calibration improves ~1000x with exposure, but unseen keys look "randomly confident"

| type | tier (n exposures) | n | KL(true‖model) | TV | H_true | H_model | argmax acc |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| deterministic | unseen (0) | 5 | 3.259 | 0.876 | 0.000 | 0.942 | 0.00 |
| deterministic | rare (3) | 7 | 0.028 | 0.027 | 0.000 | 0.129 | 1.00 |
| deterministic | medium (20) | 9 | 0.006 | 0.006 | 0.000 | 0.043 | 1.00 |
| deterministic | frequent (150) | 9 | 0.001 | 0.001 | 0.000 | 0.011 | 1.00 |
| stochastic | unseen (0) | 10 | 2.594 | 0.680 | 0.898 | 0.888 | 0.10 |
| stochastic | rare (3) | 8 | 0.705 | 0.294 | 0.898 | 0.696 | 0.75 |
| stochastic | medium (20) | 6 | 0.188 | 0.162 | 0.898 | 0.859 | 1.00 |
| stochastic | frequent (150) | 6 | 0.042 | 0.099 | 0.898 | 0.896 | 1.00 |

KL divergence to the true distribution falls by roughly 3 orders of magnitude from `unseen`/`rare` to `frequent` for deterministic keys (3.26 → 0.001) and by more than an order of magnitude for stochastic keys (2.59 → 0.042), with just 3 exposures already recovering most of the aleatoric structure for stochastic keys (KL 0.71 vs. 2.59 for unseen).

The critical conflation shows up in the entropy columns: unseen deterministic keys have **H_model = 0.942 nats**, essentially indistinguishable in magnitude from the *genuine* aleatoric entropy of stochastic keys (H_true = 0.898 nats). An uncertainty consumer looking only at entropy — with no access to our ground truth — cannot tell "this is genuinely a coin flip" from "the model has simply never seen this key." Note also that unseen stochastic keys have H_model = 0.888, deceptively close to H_true = 0.898 — but this is coincidental collapse toward the model's global prior over value tokens, not evidence of correct calibration: argmax accuracy for unseen stochastic keys is only 0.10 (worse than the 0.33 expected from guessing among 3 candidates), and KL divergence is high (2.59), showing the *shape* of the distribution is wrong even though its *entropy* happens to be similar.

### 4.2 Monte-Carlo entropy estimation is expensive relative to the analytic alternative

| T (samples) | MAE(entropy) | RMSE(entropy) |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | 0.1431 | 0.2500 |
| 20 | 0.0826 | 0.1339 |
| 100 | 0.0404 | 0.0571 |
| 400 | 0.0220 | 0.0296 |

Error decays roughly as O(1/√T) as expected for a Monte-Carlo estimator, but even at T=400 samples the sampling-based entropy estimate still carries ~0.022 nats of error that the analytic (single forward pass) entropy has by construction (0 error, 1 forward pass vs. up to 400). This is a controlled, quantitative instance of the motivation behind Semantic Entropy Probes and similar single-pass approximations to sampling-based semantic entropy: in our setting, matching the precision of one forward pass would require on the order of hundreds of samples.

### 4.3 Entropy detects errors well, but the signal is dominated by the unseen/seen boundary

On deterministic keys (n=30, 5 argmax errors — all from the `unseen` tier, which had 0/5 correct), both predictive entropy and 1−max-probability achieve AUROC 0.992 for detecting argmax errors. However, per-tier accuracy (`unseen`: 0.00, `rare`/`medium`/`frequent`: 1.00 each) shows this AUROC is driven entirely by a step function at the seen/unseen boundary rather than a graded relationship across exposure levels — with only 3 exposures, deterministic keys are already recovered perfectly in this task. We did not observe graded errors within the `rare`/`medium`/`frequent` tiers to test finer-grained AUROC trends; a harder or noisier task would be needed to separate these tiers behaviorally.

## 5. Discussion and Limitations

The central empirical finding is that a model's raw predictive entropy is not a reliable signal of *aleatoric* uncertainty on its own: an unseen deterministic key produces almost the same entropy as a genuinely stochastic key, even though their ground-truth uncertainty is 0 vs. 0.898 nats respectively. Distinguishing them requires either (i) an auxiliary epistemic signal (e.g., training frequency, retrieval confidence, ensemble disagreement) or (ii) comparison to the *shape*, not just the magnitude, of the distribution — KL/TV against a reference is far more discriminative than entropy alone in our data. This directly supports the multi-axis uncertainty taxonomies proposed in recent surveys, and gives a concrete, reproducible demonstration of *why* entropy-only UQ conflates the axes they try to separate.

**Limitations.** (1) The environment had no network access, so we could not benchmark an actual pretrained LLM (e.g., GPT-2) or real semantic-entropy pipelines; our model is trained from scratch on a synthetic task, which isolates the phenomena cleanly but does not by itself establish effect sizes on natural language. (2) The task uses single-token predictions, so "semantic clustering" of multi-token generations (a key ingredient of real semantic entropy) is not exercised — our Monte-Carlo experiment measures pure categorical sampling noise, a lower bound on the real cost, since natural text also requires paraphrase clustering. (3) Only one random seed was used for the main run due to the time budget; the qualitative frequency-vs-calibration trend is consistent with basic statistical-learning intuition (more data → better estimate of a categorical parameter) and is unlikely to be a fluke, but exact numbers would shift with reseeding. (4) We did not test whether temperature scaling or other post-hoc calibration can repair the unseen-key entropy inflation; this is a natural next step.

## 6. Conclusion

Using a fully controlled synthetic generative process with known aleatoric distributions and known per-item training exposure, we show that (a) a small transformer's predictive distribution converges toward the true generating distribution with more exposure, with roughly 1000x calibration-error reduction from 0 to 150 exposures; (b) entropy alone cannot distinguish "genuinely random" from "rarely seen," since unseen deterministic items produce entropy comparable to genuinely stochastic items; and (c) Monte-Carlo sampling-based entropy estimation — the core operation in semantic-entropy-style UQ — requires hundreds of samples to match the precision available for free from a single forward pass. These results, obtained in under 30 seconds of CPU compute, give a controlled testbed for isolating aleatoric from epistemic uncertainty and quantify a concrete motivation for single-pass UQ approximations over sampling-heavy ones. All code (`experiment.py`), logs (`results.log`), and raw per-key results (`results.json`) are provided for inspection and re-running.
