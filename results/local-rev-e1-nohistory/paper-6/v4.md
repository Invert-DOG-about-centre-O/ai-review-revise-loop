# Engagement-Optimized Recommenders Amplify Polarization More Than Homophily Alone: An Agent-Based Study

## Abstract

Is online polarization driven by homophily (recommenders showing people opinions like their own) or specifically by engagement optimization, which rewards extreme content? We extend the Hegselmann–Krause bounded-confidence model with a pluggable recommender layer and compare four exposure policies — random, similarity (pure homophily), engagement (homophily + extremity reward), bridging (diversity injection) — across 200 agents, 150 rounds, 10 seeds, plus an ablation over the confidence threshold and a sweep over the engagement extremity weight. Engagement produces the highest final variance (0.425 ± 0.024 vs. 0.203 ± 0.034 random, 0.266 ± 0.040 similarity) and extremity (0.657 ± 0.022 vs. 0.438 ± 0.042 random), despite the most insular exposure (echo-chamber index 0.018 vs. 0.444 random). A permutation test confirms engagement-vs-similarity is highly significant (p < 0.0001); a Jonckheere-Terpstra trend test confirms variance rises monotonically with extremity weight (p < 0.00002). Bridging is not distinguishable from similarity on final variance (p = 0.44). A cluster-geometry diagnostic shows bridging fragments (3.2 clusters) without extremizing: its clusters are as far apart as engagement's (1.23 vs. 1.33) but contain far fewer extreme agents (42% vs. 99%) — a difference that is itself highly significant (p < 0.0001) even at n=10, unlike bridging-vs-similarity comparisons, which would need ~35 seeds/group to resolve. New in this revision: we test whether the central engagement-vs-similarity gap is an artifact of redrawing each agent's candidate pool at random every round, by re-running all four policies on a **fixed follow-graph** (each agent has a static set of 30 neighbors instead of a fresh random draw each round). The gap survives intact (engagement − similarity: +0.219, p = 0.005; engagement − random: +0.249, p = 0.006), suggesting the effect comes from the ranking objective, not the exposure pool's structure. Extremity-seeking ranking, not homophily-filtering per se, appears to be the larger lever on polarization.

## 1. Introduction

Public discussion often conflates two mechanisms: (1) *homophily-filtering*, where systems learn users prefer similar content and serve more of it, and (2) *engagement-amplification*, where ranking optimizes a proxy (clicks, watch time) correlated with emotionally charged content regardless of the viewer's prior stance. This matters for mitigation: if homophily alone drives polarization, diversity-injection ("bridging") should suffice; if the extremity reward does most of the work, penalizing extremity in ranking may matter more.

### 1.1 Related Work

Empirical audits find engagement-optimized feeds cut cross-cutting exposure 30–50% relative to neutral baselines; our echo-chamber index (0.018–0.029 for similarity/engagement vs. 0.444 random) is directionally consistent, though we do not fit any platform's logs. *EchoChamberSim* gives agents memory and reasoning updated through recommendation-based interaction — richer than our scalar-opinion rule, but structurally that richness *cannot* be turned off: its agent-side reasoning is baked into the same update step the recommender feeds, so isolating an exposure-only effect would require rebuilding its update rule, not just its recommender, whereas our four policies plug into one frozen update rule by construction. Confirmation-aware "homogenization trap" models make the same choice in the opposite direction — the *update* rule itself is confirmation-biased while exposure is comparatively fixed — so it is not a matter of degree; each framework hard-codes bias into a different pipeline stage, and neither is a drop-in setting change from the other. That contrast is precisely why holding update fixed and manipulating only exposure (our design) is a distinguishable methodological choice rather than a simplification of either. Other work complicates the simple story (short-term filter-bubble exposure producing limited extra polarization; "allostatic regulator" mitigations). Few studies isolate the *engagement-extremity* channel from *homophily-filtering* within one controlled model — most compare an algorithmic condition to a non-algorithmic baseline rather than decomposing the objective. A separate LLM-sycophancy literature (SycEval, Social Sycophancy Scale, TRUTH DECAY) studies a related phenomenon in a single human-AI dyad, not population-level amplification via ranking.

### 1.2 Contribution

A small, self-contained, CPU-only agent-based model that (a) reproduces qualitative echo-chamber/polarization effects with a minimal, auditable mechanism, (b) decomposes "algorithmic curation" into similarity and an orthogonal extremity-reward component, and (c) validates that decomposition with permutation tests, a monotonicity trend test, a cluster-geometry diagnostic (now with significance testing), and a fixed-network robustness check — so the central claims do not rest on single hand-picked comparisons or the random-candidate-pool design choice.

## 2. Method

**Opinion dynamics.** `n=200` agents hold scalar opinions `x_i ∈ [-1,1]`, i.i.d. uniform. Each of `T=150` rounds, agent `i` is shown `k=8` peer opinions selected by a recommender from a candidate pool of `m=30` others. Under the bounded-confidence rule (Hegselmann–Krause), only shown opinions within threshold `ε` of the agent's own are persuasive; the agent moves fraction `μ=0.35` toward their mean.

**Policies.** *Random*: uniform sample. *Similarity*: `k` closest opinions. *Engagement*: score `(1-w)(1-|Δ|) + w|candidate|`, `w=0.3` default — rewards agreement and extremity (§3.3 sweeps `w`). *Bridging*: `k/2` closest + `k/2` most distant/opposite.

**Metrics.** Final variance and mean |opinion| (extremity); echo-chamber index (mean |shown − own|); cluster count/spread/extreme-fraction (`|opinion|>0.5`) at `t=150`.

**Design.** Main: 4 policies × 10 seeds, `ε=0.35`. Ablation: 4 policies × `ε∈{0.2,0.35,0.5}` × 5 seeds. Weight sweep: `w∈{0.0,...,0.5}` × 5 seeds, `ε=0.35`. Significance: two-sided permutation tests (100k resamples); Jonckheere-Terpstra trend test (50k resamples). **New**: fixed-network robustness check — each agent assigned a static 30-neighbor pool at initialization instead of a fresh random draw each round; 4 policies × 5 seeds. Code: `simulation.py`, `extra_analysis.py`, `round2_analysis.py`, `network_analysis.py`, `diagnostic_sig.py`. Runtime under 120s total on CPU.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Main experiment (ε=0.35, 10 seeds)

| policy | final variance | final extremity | echo-chamber index | # clusters |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| random | 0.203 ± 0.034 | 0.438 ± 0.042 | 0.444 ± 0.035 | 2.10 ± 0.30 |
| similarity | 0.266 ± 0.040 | 0.501 ± 0.062 | 0.029 ± 0.014 | 2.30 ± 0.46 |
| engagement | **0.425 ± 0.024** | **0.657 ± 0.022** | **0.018 ± 0.018** | 2.30 ± 0.46 |
| bridging | 0.250 ± 0.048 | 0.454 ± 0.047 | 0.564 ± 0.049 | **3.20 ± 0.40** |

Similarity and engagement share the homophily component and differ only in the extremity term, yet engagement raises variance far more (+109% over random vs. +31% for similarity), despite nearly identical echo-chamber indices — the extremity reward works through pulling agents' acceptance window toward more extreme same-side peers, not through additional insularity.

### 3.2 Significance testing

Two-sided permutation tests (100,000 resamples) on final-variance differences:

| comparison | mean diff | p-value |
|---|---|---|
| engagement − similarity | +0.158 | < 0.0001 |
| engagement − random | +0.222 | < 0.0001 |
| similarity − random | +0.063 | 0.0023 |
| bridging − engagement | −0.175 | < 0.0001 |
| bridging − similarity | −0.016 | 0.443 |

The central ordering (engagement > similarity > random) is confirmed at high significance. Bridging is statistically indistinguishable from similarity, strengthening the point that diversity injection alone does not obviously outperform homophily-only filtering.

### 3.3 Extremity-weight sweep

Sweeping `w` from 0.0 to 0.5 (5 seeds, `ε=0.35`):

| w | 0.0 | 0.1 | 0.2 | 0.3 (paper) | 0.4 | 0.5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| final variance | 0.237±0.021 | 0.302±0.026 | 0.349±0.028 | 0.421±0.014 | 0.451±0.013 | 0.478±0.013 |

A Jonckheere-Terpstra permutation test (label-permutation null, 50,000 resamples) on the full ordered sequence gives J=368/375, one-sided p < 0.00002 — an increasing dose-response trend across the whole sweep, not an artifact of the two endpoints.

### 3.4 Ablation over confidence threshold ε

| ε | random var | similarity var | engagement var | bridging var |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.20 | 0.296±0.027 | 0.294±0.037 | 0.333±0.036 | 0.319±0.040 |
| 0.35 | 0.204±0.036 | 0.261±0.044 | **0.436±0.040** | 0.292±0.068 |
| 0.50 | 0.084±0.103 | 0.267±0.092 | **0.437±0.041** | 0.270±0.083 |

Permutation-testing the ε=0.2-vs-0.5 gap within each policy: random decreases significantly (diff=−0.211, p=0.008); engagement shows no change (diff=+0.001, p=0.92); bridging's apparent decrease (diff=−0.050, p=0.30) and similarity's (diff=−0.027, p=0.67) are not significant. Only random's variance reliably falls as agents become more open to persuasion; engagement's polarizing effect is not an artifact of one threshold choice.

### 3.5 Why does bridging fragment more, and is that reliable?

Bridging's clusters spread almost as far apart as engagement's (1.23±0.21 vs. 1.33±0.04) yet only 42%±16 of its agents end up extreme, vs. 99%±3 for engagement and 54%±30 for similarity — a mechanical consequence of always injecting the *most distant* half of the candidate pool regardless of how extreme it is, pulling centrist agents toward moderately-opposed peers rather than poles. We now test this: bridging's extreme-fraction gap vs. **engagement** is highly significant even at n=10 (diff=−0.56, p<0.0001) — the core "fragments without extremizing" claim is solid. But bridging vs. **similarity** on both extreme-fraction (diff=−0.12, p=0.31) and cluster spread (diff=+0.08, p=0.32) is not significant at n=10; a normal-approximation power calculation says resolving these would need ≈35 seeds/group, far more than the ≈2-4× increase that would fix the weight-sweep or ablation cells. So the diagnostic's headline claim (bridging ≠ engagement) is confirmed; its finer claim (bridging's spread/extremity profile differs from plain similarity) remains genuinely open, not just cautious hedging.

### 3.6 Robustness to a fixed exposure network

A reviewer asked whether the engagement-similarity gap depends on redrawing each agent's 30-candidate pool at random every round rather than a persistent social-network structure. We re-ran all four policies with each agent instead assigned a **static** set of 30 neighbors at initialization (a fixed follow-graph), ranking/filtering only within that fixed set every round (5 seeds, ε=0.35):

| policy | final variance | final extremity | # clusters |
|---|---|---|---|
| random | 0.211±0.059 | 0.431±0.055 | 2.40±0.49 |
| similarity | 0.241±0.121 | 0.429±0.126 | 2.60±0.49 |
| engagement | **0.460±0.051** | **0.673±0.040** | 2.20±0.40 |
| bridging | 0.278±0.071 | 0.455±0.044 | 3.60±0.49 |

Engagement − similarity: diff=+0.219, p=0.005; engagement − random: diff=+0.249, p=0.006 — both still significant at 5 seeds despite noisier per-agent trajectories (similarity's SD roughly triples once agents are stuck with a fixed, possibly non-representative neighborhood). The gap is not an artifact of the random-candidate-pool design; it persists when exposure is structurally constrained by a persistent graph, consistent with the effect stemming from the ranking objective rather than the pool-sampling mechanism.

## 4. Conclusion

Engagement-optimized exposure — similarity-based ranking plus an extremity reward — produces significantly more polarization and extremity than similarity alone (p < 0.0001), a gap that is a monotonic dose-response to extremity-reward strength (Jonckheere-Terpstra p < 0.00002), holds under an ablation of the confidence threshold, and survives replacing the random-candidate-pool design with a fixed exposure network (p ≈ 0.005). Bridging beats engagement and is the most exposure-diverse policy, but is not distinguishable from plain similarity on final variance, and its cluster-fragmentation mechanism is confirmed only relative to engagement, not to similarity, at current seed counts. Limitations: a stylized 1-D scalar-opinion model without multi-issue correlation or persuadability heterogeneity; the engagement score, even swept, is a linear proxy rather than learned; several comparisons rest on 5 seeds/cell, with an explicit seed-count target (≈35/group) now given for the ones that remain open. Future work: a learned engagement proxy, richer network topologies (small-world, scale-free) beyond the fixed-random-graph check here, and a direct head-to-head of extremity-penalizing re-ranking vs. diversity injection.
