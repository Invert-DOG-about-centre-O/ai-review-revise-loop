# Engagement-Optimized Recommenders Amplify Polarization More Than Homophily Alone: An Agent-Based Study

## Abstract

Algorithmic content curation is widely blamed for online polarization, but disentangling *why* is hard: is it simply that recommenders show people opinions similar to their own (homophily), or is it specifically optimization for engagement — which rewards extreme, attention-grabbing content — that drives extra polarization? We build a lightweight, fully reproducible agent-based simulation isolating this question, extending the Hegselmann–Krause bounded-confidence opinion dynamics model with a pluggable "recommender" layer controlling which peer opinions each agent is exposed to each round, drawn from a larger random candidate pool. We compare four exposure policies — random, similarity (pure homophily), engagement (homophily plus a reward for opinion extremity), and bridging (diversity-injecting) — across 200 agents, 150 rounds, 10 seeds, with an ablation over the bounded-confidence threshold and a sweep over the engagement score's extremity weight. Engagement produces the highest final opinion variance (0.425 ± 0.024 vs. 0.203 ± 0.034 random, 0.266 ± 0.040 similarity) and extremity (0.657 ± 0.022 vs. 0.438 ± 0.042 random), while also producing the most insular exposure (echo-chamber index 0.018 vs. 0.444 random). A permutation test confirms the engagement-vs-similarity gap is highly significant (p < 0.0001), and a sweep over the extremity weight (0.0–0.5) shows final variance rises monotonically with weight — confirmed by a Jonckheere-Terpstra permutation trend test (p < 0.00002), not just a single pairwise comparison. Bridging is *not* statistically distinguishable from plain similarity on final variance (p = 0.44); testing the ablation table's "engagement flat / random and bridging decrease" narrative pairwise, random's decrease is significant (p = 0.008) but bridging's is not (p = 0.30) at 5 seeds. A cluster-geometry diagnostic explains why bridging still fragments the population (3.2 clusters vs. ~2.1–2.3 elsewhere) without pushing it to extremes: its clusters spread as far apart as engagement's (1.23 vs. 1.33) but contain far fewer extreme agents (42% with |opinion|>0.5 vs. 99% for engagement, 54% for similarity) — injecting a fixed opposite-side half regardless of its extremity pulls sub-groups into multiple moderate camps rather than two poles. Extremity-seeking ranking, not homophily-filtering per se, appears to be the larger lever on polarization — a more promising mitigation target than diversity injection alone.

## 1. Introduction

The socio-technical framing of recommender systems asks not just "does the algorithm work" but "what social dynamics does the algorithm's optimization target produce when embedded in a population of interacting people." A large body of observational and simulation work links algorithmic curation to opinion polarization and echo chambers, but two distinct mechanisms are often conflated in public discussion: (1) *homophily-driven filtering*, where systems learn users prefer content resembling their existing views and simply serve more of it, and (2) *engagement-driven amplification*, where ranking optimizes a proxy (clicks, watch time, replies) empirically correlated with emotionally charged content, independent of whether it matches the viewer's prior stance. Distinguishing these matters for mitigation: if homophily alone drives polarization, diversity-injection ("bridging") should suffice; if the extremity reward does most of the work, penalizing extremity in ranking may matter more.

### 1.1 Related Work

Empirical audits find engagement-optimized feeds increase exposure to ideologically aligned content and reduce cross-cutting exposure substantially (30–50%) relative to chronological or neutral baselines, and recommendation feedback loops can produce echo chambers with negative effects on information diversity and social cohesion. We do not reproduce these magnitudes — our model is a stylized abstraction, not fit to any platform's logs — but our echo-chamber index (0.018–0.029 for similarity/engagement vs. 0.444 random) is directionally consistent with the pattern those audits report. Frameworks such as *EchoChamberSim* give agents memory and reasoning updated through recommendation-based interactions, richer than our scalar-opinion rule, but that richness is exactly why it cannot cleanly isolate an exposure-only effect: agent-side memory and reasoning are extra free parameters that could themselves produce polarization, confounding attribution to the recommender. Confirmation-aware models similarly study "homogenization traps" by making the *update* rule itself confirmation-biased rather than holding it fixed and manipulating only *exposure* — the opposite design choice from ours. We hold the Hegselmann–Krause update mechanism constant across all four policies so any resulting difference is attributable purely to what agents are shown, not how they reason about it — narrower than either richer framework, but what makes the similarity-vs-engagement decomposition interpretable. Some work complicates the simple story (short-term filter-bubble exposure producing limited additional polarization; "allostatic regulator" mitigations for feedback loops). Few studies isolate the *engagement-extremity* channel from *homophily-filtering* within one controlled model — most compare an algorithmic condition to a non-algorithmic baseline rather than decomposing the objective, the gap this paper targets. A separate LLM-sycophancy literature (SycEval, Social Sycophancy Scale, TRUTH DECAY) studies a related phenomenon in a single human-AI dyad, not population-level amplification via a ranking layer.

### 1.2 Contribution

We contribute a small, self-contained, CPU-only agent-based model that (a) reproduces the qualitative echo-chamber and polarization effects reported in the literature with a minimal, auditable mechanism, (b) decomposes "algorithmic curation" into a similarity component and an orthogonal extremity-reward component, showing their marginal effects and their sensitivity to agents' openness to persuasion, and (c) validates that decomposition with permutation tests, a weight sweep with an explicit monotonicity test, and a diagnostic of *why* the diversity-injecting policy fragments rather than moderates the population, so the central claims do not rest on single hand-picked comparisons.

## 2. Method

### 2.1 Opinion dynamics model

We use a population of `n = 200` agents, each holding a scalar opinion `x_i ∈ [-1, 1]` initialized i.i.d. uniform. At each of `T = 150` rounds, every agent `i` is shown `k = 8` peer opinions selected by a recommender policy (below) from a randomly drawn candidate pool of `m = 30` other agents (a broader universe of content the platform *could* show). Agent `i` updates via a bounded-confidence rule (Hegselmann–Krause): among the `k` shown opinions, only those within confidence threshold `ε` of the agent's current opinion are persuasive; the agent moves a fraction `μ = 0.35` toward the mean of those. Opinions outside `ε` are seen but ignored — the standard bounded-confidence assumption that people discount views too far from their own. Updates are synchronous per round.

### 2.2 Recommender policies

- **Random** (baseline): uniformly random subset of the candidate pool — no algorithmic curation.
- **Similarity**: the `k` candidates closest in opinion to the agent's own — pure homophily, no extremity component.
- **Engagement**: scores each candidate as `(1-w)·(1 − |Δopinion|) + w·|candidate opinion|`, `w = 0.3` by default, shows top-`k` — rewards agreement *and* extremity, modeling engagement-proxy ranking correlated with sensational content. §3.3 sweeps `w`.
- **Bridging**: `k/2` closest candidates plus `k/2` from the most distant/opposite part of the spectrum — diversity injection, as proposed in the mitigation literature.

### 2.3 Metrics

- **Final opinion variance**: population-level polarization at `t = 150`.
- **Mean absolute opinion (extremity)**: how far, on average, opinions sit from the neutral center.
- **Echo-chamber index**: mean `|shown opinion − own opinion|` over agents/rounds — how insular each policy's exposure actually is, independent of downstream opinion change.
- **Cluster count / cluster-center spread / extreme fraction**: distinct opinion clusters at `t=150` (adjacent opinions within a 0.08 gap merge), the distance between outermost cluster centers, and the fraction of agents with `|opinion| > 0.5` — together separating "many moderate camps" from "collapsed onto two extreme poles."

### 2.4 Experimental design

*Main*: all four policies × 10 seeds, default `ε = 0.35`. *Ablation*: all four policies × `ε ∈ {0.2, 0.35, 0.5}` × 5 seeds. *Significance*: two-sided permutation tests (100,000 resamples) on final-variance differences for the main experiment's five key policy pairs, and, newly, for the ablation table's four eps=0.2-vs-0.5 within-policy comparisons. *Weight sweep*: engagement re-run at `w ∈ {0.0,...,0.5}` × 5 seeds; monotonicity tested with a Jonckheere-Terpstra statistic under a label-permutation null (50,000 resamples). *Bridging diagnostic*: cluster-center spread and extreme-opinion fraction from stored final opinions (no re-simulation needed). Code: `simulation.py`, `extra_analysis.py`, `round2_analysis.py`; raw results in `results.json`, `ablation_results.json`, `significance_results.json`, `weight_sweep_results.json`, `round2_analysis_results.json`; plot `trajectories.png`. Runtime under 100s on CPU.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Main experiment (ε = 0.35, 10 seeds)

| policy | final variance | final extremity | echo-chamber index | # clusters |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| random | 0.203 ± 0.034 | 0.438 ± 0.042 | 0.444 ± 0.035 | 2.10 ± 0.30 |
| similarity | 0.266 ± 0.040 | 0.501 ± 0.062 | 0.029 ± 0.014 | 2.30 ± 0.46 |
| engagement | **0.425 ± 0.024** | **0.657 ± 0.022** | **0.018 ± 0.018** | 2.30 ± 0.46 |
| bridging | 0.250 ± 0.048 | 0.454 ± 0.047 | 0.564 ± 0.049 | **3.20 ± 0.40** |

All three algorithmic policies raise final variance above the random baseline, but *engagement* raises it far more (+109% over random) than *similarity* alone (+31%). Since similarity and engagement share the homophily component and differ only in the added extremity term, this gap is attributable specifically to extremity-seeking ranking. The echo-chamber index is nearly identical for similarity and engagement (0.029 vs. 0.018) — both highly insular — yet engagement produces substantially more polarization and extremity, indicating the extremity reward works through a different channel than pure exposure-similarity: it pulls agents' acceptance window toward the more extreme members of their own similar-opinion neighborhood, dragging it outward over time. Bridging achieves the highest echo-chamber index (least insular, as intended) and the most fragmented population (3.2 clusters vs. ~2.1–2.3 elsewhere) — §3.5 diagnoses why, without pushing the population toward extremes.

### 3.2 Significance testing

A reviewer noted that with only 10 (main) or 5 (ablation) seeds per condition, mean ± SD alone cannot establish which comparisons are robust. We ran two-sided permutation tests (100,000 resamples) on final-variance differences for the five most load-bearing comparisons:

| comparison | mean diff | p-value |
|---|---|---|
| engagement − similarity | +0.158 | < 0.0001 |
| engagement − random | +0.222 | < 0.0001 |
| similarity − random | +0.063 | 0.0023 |
| bridging − engagement | −0.175 | < 0.0001 |
| bridging − similarity | −0.016 | 0.443 |

The paper's central claim — engagement produces significantly more polarization than similarity, which produces significantly more than random — is confirmed at high significance. Bridging clearly beats engagement and is the most exposure-diverse policy, but with 10 seeds we cannot support a directional claim about bridging vs. similarity on final variance — they are statistically indistinguishable, which if anything strengthens the paper's core point that diversity injection alone does not obviously outperform homophily-only filtering, let alone match removing the extremity reward.

### 3.3 Extremity-weight sweep

To test whether the engagement-similarity gap is an artifact of the hand-picked 0.7/0.3 split, we swept the extremity weight `w` in the engagement score from 0.0 (pure agreement, ≈similarity) to 0.5, at `ε = 0.35`, 5 seeds each:

| w | 0.0 | 0.1 | 0.2 | 0.3 (paper) | 0.4 | 0.5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| final variance | 0.237 ± 0.021 | 0.302 ± 0.026 | 0.349 ± 0.028 | 0.421 ± 0.014 | 0.451 ± 0.013 | 0.478 ± 0.013 |

Final variance rises monotonically with `w`, and a pairwise permutation test confirms `w = 0.3` (paper's default) is significantly higher than `w = 0.0` (diff = +0.184, p = 0.0068). A reviewer asked whether the "dose-response, not threshold effect" claim should rest on more than one pairwise comparison — visual monotonicity across six means is not itself a trend test. We ran a Jonckheere-Terpstra test (label-permutation null, 50,000 resamples) on the full ordered sequence of six weight groups: the observed statistic (368 of a maximum possible 375 concordant group-pairs) is far outside the null distribution, one-sided p < 0.00002 — confirming an increasing trend across the whole sweep, not just at the two endpoints inspected pairwise.

### 3.4 Ablation over confidence threshold ε

| ε | random var | similarity var | engagement var | bridging var |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.20 | 0.296 ± 0.027 | 0.294 ± 0.037 | 0.333 ± 0.036 | 0.319 ± 0.040 |
| 0.35 | 0.204 ± 0.036 | 0.261 ± 0.044 | **0.436 ± 0.040** | 0.292 ± 0.068 |
| 0.50 | 0.084 ± 0.103 | 0.267 ± 0.092 | **0.437 ± 0.041** | 0.270 ± 0.083 |

At the narrowest threshold (ε = 0.2), all four policies converge to similarly high variance. As ε widens from 0.2 to 0.5, engagement's variance stays essentially unchanged (0.436→0.437) and remains highest at every threshold. A reviewer flagged that, unlike the main table, this one was not permutation-tested, so the "flat"/"decreasing" language might not survive 5-seed noise. Testing the ε=0.2-vs-0.5 gap within each policy: **random** decreases significantly (diff = −0.211, p = 0.008); **engagement** shows no significant change (diff = +0.001, p = 0.92, consistent with "flat"); but **bridging**'s apparent decrease (0.319→0.270) is *not* significant (diff = −0.050, p = 0.30), nor is similarity's (diff = −0.027, p = 0.67). We revise the claim accordingly: only random's variance reliably falls as agents become more open to persuasion. Engagement's polarizing effect is not an artifact of one threshold choice and, unlike random, does not shrink where widening ε would otherwise let a population converge. `trajectories.png` shows engagement separating from the other three within ~20–30 rounds and continuing to climb, while random and bridging plateau earlier and lower.

### 3.5 Why does bridging fragment more?

A reviewer asked whether bridging's higher cluster count (3.2 vs. ~2.1–2.3) reflects a genuine mechanism or noise. Using final opinions already recorded in the main experiment, we computed each policy's cluster-center spread (distance between outermost cluster means) and the fraction of agents ending with `|opinion| > 0.5`:

| policy | cluster-center spread | fraction extreme (\|opinion\|>0.5) |
|---|---|---|
| similarity | 1.15 ± 0.13 | 0.54 ± 0.30 |
| engagement | 1.33 ± 0.04 | 0.99 ± 0.03 |
| bridging | 1.23 ± 0.21 | 0.42 ± 0.16 |

Bridging's clusters are spread almost as far apart as engagement's (1.23 vs. 1.33), yet only 42% of its agents end up extreme, versus 99% for engagement and 54% for similarity. This is a mechanical consequence of the fixed-`k/2`-opposite-side rule: bridging always injects the *most distant* half of the candidate pool regardless of how extreme that half actually is, so a centrist agent gets pulled by moderately-opposed peers rather than poles, while an agent already near a pole gets reinforced by both its similar half and an "opposite" half drawn from the same broad region. The result is several separated, only moderately extreme camps rather than convergence to one center (random, similarity) or collapse to two poles (engagement). Bridging's echo-chamber index (0.564), the highest of all four policies, means its clusters are also the ones most exposed to genuinely opposing views each round — consistent with fragmentation, not reinforcement. Whether this persists at larger seed counts is untested; §3.2 already shows bridging vs. similarity is not reliably distinguishable at n=10, so we treat it as suggestive rather than confirmed.

## 4. Conclusion

Using a minimal, fully reproducible bounded-confidence simulation, we find that an engagement-optimized exposure policy — one that adds an extremity reward on top of ordinary similarity-based ranking — produces significantly more polarization and extremity than similarity-based filtering alone (permutation p < 0.0001), despite both producing comparably insular echo chambers. A weight sweep, backed by a Jonckheere-Terpstra trend test (p < 0.00002) rather than a single pairwise comparison, shows this gap is a monotonic dose-response to extremity-reward strength, not an artifact of one hand-picked weighting. This separates two mechanisms often bundled in the "algorithms cause polarization" narrative. We remain cautious about bridging: it beats engagement and is the most exposure-diverse policy, but is not distinguishable from plain similarity on final variance at our seed count, its apparent ε-ablation decrease is likewise not significant, and it produces more opinion clusters (3.2 vs. ~2.1–2.3) — a fragmentation effect we trace mechanistically to its fixed opposite-side injection rule rather than genuine moderation. This still suggests diversity injection alone may under-perform interventions that directly discourage extremity in ranking, presented as directionally supported rather than established. Limitations: a stylized 1-D scalar-opinion model without a real network topology, multi-issue correlation, or persuadability heterogeneity; the engagement score, even swept, remains a linear proxy rather than learned; and several comparisons still rest on 5 seeds per cell. Future work: a networked (non-random-pool) topology, a learned engagement proxy, and a direct head-to-head of extremity-penalizing re-ranking vs. diversity injection.
