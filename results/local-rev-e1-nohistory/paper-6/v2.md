# Engagement-Optimized Recommenders Amplify Polarization More Than Homophily Alone: An Agent-Based Study

## Abstract

Algorithmic content curation is widely blamed for online polarization, but disentangling *why* is hard: is it simply that recommenders show people opinions similar to their own (homophily), or is it specifically optimization for engagement — which rewards extreme, attention-grabbing content — that drives extra polarization? We build a lightweight, fully reproducible agent-based simulation isolating this question, extending the Hegselmann–Krause bounded-confidence opinion dynamics model with a pluggable "recommender" layer controlling which peer opinions each agent is exposed to each round, drawn from a larger random candidate pool. We compare four exposure policies — random, similarity (pure homophily), engagement (homophily plus a reward for opinion extremity), and bridging (diversity-injecting) — across 200 agents, 150 rounds, 10 seeds, with an ablation over the bounded-confidence threshold and a sweep over the engagement score's extremity weight. Engagement produces the highest final opinion variance (0.425 ± 0.024 vs. 0.203 ± 0.034 random, 0.266 ± 0.040 similarity) and extremity (0.657 ± 0.022 vs. 0.438 ± 0.042 random), while also producing the most insular exposure (echo-chamber index 0.018 vs. 0.444 random). A permutation test confirms the engagement-vs-similarity gap is highly significant (p < 0.0001, 100,000 permutations), and a sweep over the extremity weight (0.0–0.5) shows final variance rises monotonically with weight, not as an artifact of the paper's 0.7/0.3 split (w=0.3 vs. w=0.0: p = 0.007). This holds across all three confidence thresholds tested. By contrast, "bridging" is *not* statistically distinguishable from plain similarity on final variance (p = 0.44) — we revise our v1 claim accordingly: bridging clearly beats engagement and is the most diverse policy, but we cannot say it beats similarity-only curation at these seed counts. The results suggest extremity-seeking ranking, not homophily-filtering per se, is the larger lever on polarization — a more promising mitigation target (e.g., re-ranking penalties on extremity rather than only diversity injection).

## 1. Introduction

The socio-technical framing of recommender systems asks not just "does the algorithm work" but "what social dynamics does the algorithm's optimization target produce when embedded in a population of interacting people." A large body of observational and simulation work links algorithmic curation to opinion polarization and echo chambers, but two distinct mechanisms are often conflated in public discussion: (1) *homophily-driven filtering*, where systems learn users prefer content resembling their existing views and simply serve more of it, and (2) *engagement-driven amplification*, where ranking optimizes a proxy (clicks, watch time, replies) empirically correlated with emotionally charged or extreme content, independent of whether it matches the viewer's prior stance. Distinguishing these matters for mitigation: if homophily alone drives polarization, diversity-injection ("bridging") should suffice; if the extremity reward is doing most of the work, penalizing extremity in ranking may matter more than diversity per se.

### 1.1 Related Work

Empirical audits find engagement-optimized feeds increase exposure to ideologically aligned content and reduce cross-cutting exposure substantially (30–50%) relative to chronological or neutral baselines, and recommendation feedback loops can produce echo chambers with negative effects on information diversity and social cohesion. We do not reproduce these magnitudes quantitatively — our model is a stylized abstraction, not fit to any platform's logs — but our echo-chamber index (0.018–0.029 for similarity/engagement vs. 0.444 random, ~95% lower shown-opinion distance) is directionally consistent with the pattern those audits report. Frameworks such as *EchoChamberSim* give agents memory and reasoning updated through recommendation-based interactions, richer than our scalar-opinion rule, and related confirmation-aware models study how algorithms that mirror agents' existing beliefs create "homogenization traps," typically by making the *update* rule itself confirmation-biased rather than holding it fixed and manipulating only *exposure*, as we do — why we hold the Hegselmann–Krause update mechanism constant across all four policies, so any difference is attributable purely to what agents are shown. Some work complicates the simple story (short-term filter-bubble exposure producing only limited additional polarization; "allostatic regulator" mitigations proposed to counteract feedback loops). Classical opinion-dynamics theory (DeGroot; Hegselmann–Krause) underlies most of these simulations, but few socio-technical studies isolate the *engagement-extremity* channel from *homophily-filtering* within one controlled model — most compare an algorithmic condition to a non-algorithmic baseline rather than decomposing the objective, the gap this paper targets. A separate literature on LLM sycophancy (SycEval, the Social Sycophancy Scale, TRUTH DECAY) studies a similar phenomenon in a single human-AI dyad but not population-level, multi-agent amplification via a ranking layer.

### 1.2 Contribution

We contribute a small, self-contained, CPU-only agent-based model that (a) reproduces the qualitative echo-chamber and polarization effects reported in the literature with a minimal, auditable mechanism, (b) decomposes "algorithmic curation" into a similarity component and an orthogonal extremity-reward component, showing their marginal effects separately and their sensitivity to agents' openness to persuasion (the bounded-confidence threshold), and (c) validates that decomposition with permutation significance tests and a weight sweep, so the central claim does not rest on one hand-picked parameterization.

## 2. Method

### 2.1 Opinion dynamics model

We use a population of `n = 200` agents, each holding a scalar opinion `x_i ∈ [-1, 1]` initialized i.i.d. uniform. At each of `T = 150` rounds, every agent `i` is shown `k = 8` peer opinions selected by a recommender policy (below) from a randomly drawn candidate pool of `m = 30` other agents (a broader universe of content the platform *could* show). Agent `i` updates via a bounded-confidence rule (Hegselmann–Krause): among the `k` shown opinions, only those within confidence threshold `ε` of the agent's current opinion are persuasive; the agent moves a fraction `μ = 0.35` toward the mean of those. Opinions outside `ε` are seen but ignored, the standard bounded-confidence assumption that people discount views too far from their own. Updates are computed synchronously per round from the previous round's opinions.

### 2.2 Recommender policies

- **Random** (baseline): uniformly random subset of the candidate pool — no algorithmic curation.
- **Similarity**: the `k` candidates closest in opinion to the agent's own — pure homophily, no extremity component.
- **Engagement**: scores each candidate as `(1-w)·(1 − |Δopinion|) + w·|candidate opinion|`, `w = 0.3` by default, shows top-`k` — rewards agreement *and* extremity, modeling ranking that optimizes an engagement proxy correlated with sensational content. §3.3 sweeps `w`.
- **Bridging**: `k/2` closest candidates plus `k/2` from the most distant/opposite part of the spectrum — diversity injection, as proposed in the mitigation literature.

### 2.3 Metrics

- **Final opinion variance**: population-level polarization at `t = 150`.
- **Mean absolute opinion (extremity)**: how far, on average, opinions sit from the neutral center.
- **Echo-chamber index**: mean `|shown opinion − own opinion|` over agents and rounds — how insular each policy's exposure actually is, independent of downstream opinion change.
- **Cluster count**: distinct opinion clusters in the final population (adjacent opinions within a 0.08 gap merge) — camps vs. two-pole polarization vs. consensus.

### 2.4 Experimental design

*Main*: all four policies × 10 seeds, default `ε = 0.35`. *Ablation*: all four policies × `ε ∈ {0.2, 0.35, 0.5}` × 5 seeds. *Significance*: two-sided permutation tests (100,000 resamples) on final-variance differences for five key policy pairs. *Weight sweep*: engagement re-run at `w ∈ {0.0,...,0.5}` × 5 seeds, holding everything else fixed. Code: `simulation.py` (main/ablation), `extra_analysis.py` (significance, sweep); raw results in `results.json`, `ablation_results.json`, `significance_results.json`, `weight_sweep_results.json`; console log `summary.txt`; plots `trajectories.png`. Total runtime under 95s on CPU.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Main experiment (ε = 0.35, 10 seeds)

| policy | final variance | final extremity | echo-chamber index | # clusters |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| random | 0.203 ± 0.034 | 0.438 ± 0.042 | 0.444 ± 0.035 | 2.10 ± 0.30 |
| similarity | 0.266 ± 0.040 | 0.501 ± 0.062 | 0.029 ± 0.014 | 2.30 ± 0.46 |
| engagement | **0.425 ± 0.024** | **0.657 ± 0.022** | **0.018 ± 0.018** | 2.30 ± 0.46 |
| bridging | 0.250 ± 0.048 | 0.454 ± 0.047 | 0.564 ± 0.049 | **3.20 ± 0.40** |

All three algorithmic policies raise final variance above the random baseline, but *engagement* raises it far more (+109% over random) than *similarity* alone (+31%). Since similarity and engagement share the homophily component and differ only in the added extremity term, this gap is attributable specifically to extremity-seeking ranking. The echo-chamber index is nearly identical for similarity and engagement (0.029 vs. 0.018) — both highly insular — yet engagement produces substantially more polarization and extremity, indicating the extremity reward works through a different channel than pure exposure-similarity: it pulls agents' bounded-confidence acceptance window toward the more extreme members of their own similar-opinion neighborhood, dragging the whole neighborhood outward over time. Bridging achieves the highest echo-chamber index (least insular, as intended) and produces the most fragmented population (3.2 clusters vs. ~2.1–2.3 for the others) — diversity injection here pushes the population into multiple camps rather than one moderate consensus.

### 3.2 Significance testing

A reviewer noted that with only 10 (main) or 5 (ablation) seeds per condition, mean ± SD alone cannot establish which comparisons are robust. We ran two-sided permutation tests (100,000 resamples) on final-variance differences for the main experiment's five most load-bearing comparisons:

| comparison | mean diff | p-value |
|---|---|---|
| engagement − similarity | +0.158 | < 0.0001 |
| engagement − random | +0.222 | < 0.0001 |
| similarity − random | +0.063 | 0.0023 |
| bridging − engagement | −0.175 | < 0.0001 |
| bridging − similarity | −0.016 | 0.443 |

The paper's central claim — engagement produces significantly more polarization than similarity, which in turn produces significantly more than random — is confirmed at high significance. However, the comparison our v1 draft leaned on to argue bridging "does not reduce variance below similarity" is itself **not** distinguishable from zero (p = 0.44) at this seed count. We revise that claim: bridging clearly beats engagement and is the most exposure-diverse policy, but with 10 seeds we cannot support a directional claim about bridging vs. similarity — bridging and similarity are statistically indistinguishable on final variance here, which if anything strengthens the paper's core point that diversity injection alone does not obviously outperform homophily-only filtering, let alone match removing the extremity reward.

### 3.3 Extremity-weight sweep

To test whether the engagement-similarity gap is an artifact of the hand-picked 0.7/0.3 split, we swept the extremity weight `w` in the engagement score from 0.0 (pure agreement, ≈similarity) to 0.5, at `ε = 0.35`, 5 seeds each:

| w | 0.0 | 0.1 | 0.2 | 0.3 (paper) | 0.4 | 0.5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| final variance | 0.237 ± 0.021 | 0.302 ± 0.026 | 0.349 ± 0.028 | 0.421 ± 0.014 | 0.451 ± 0.013 | 0.478 ± 0.013 |

Final variance rises monotonically and substantially with `w` — even a modest weight of 0.1 already produces a visible jump over `w = 0`, and a permutation test confirms `w = 0.3` (the paper's default) is significantly higher than `w = 0.0` (diff = +0.184, p = 0.0068). This is a genuine dose-response, not a threshold effect tied to one specific split: the gap does not vanish as the extremity weight approaches zero, except in the limit `w → 0` itself, where the policy becomes similarity by construction.

### 3.4 Ablation over confidence threshold ε

| ε | random var | similarity var | engagement var | bridging var |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.20 | 0.296 ± 0.027 | 0.294 ± 0.037 | 0.333 ± 0.036 | 0.319 ± 0.040 |
| 0.35 | 0.204 ± 0.036 | 0.261 ± 0.044 | **0.436 ± 0.040** | 0.292 ± 0.068 |
| 0.50 | 0.084 ± 0.103 | 0.267 ± 0.092 | **0.437 ± 0.041** | 0.270 ± 0.083 |

At the narrowest threshold (ε = 0.2), all four policies converge to similarly high variance. As ε widens, random and bridging show *decreasing* variance (more mixing drives convergence), while engagement's variance stays flat and remains highest across all thresholds tested (0.33–0.44) — the recommender's polarizing effect is not an artifact of one confidence-threshold choice, and widens precisely where a population would otherwise reach consensus. `trajectories.png` (mean variance/extremity over all 150 rounds) shows the engagement condition separating from the other three within ~20–30 rounds and continuing to climb, while random and bridging plateau earlier and lower and similarity climbs more slowly without plateauing.

## 4. Conclusion

Using a minimal, fully reproducible bounded-confidence simulation, we find that an engagement-optimized exposure policy — one that adds an extremity reward on top of ordinary similarity-based ranking — produces significantly more polarization and extremity than similarity-based filtering alone (permutation p < 0.0001), despite both producing comparably insular echo chambers by direct exposure-distance measurement. A weight sweep shows this gap is a monotonic dose-response to extremity-reward strength, not an artifact of one hand-picked weighting. This separates two mechanisms often bundled together in the "algorithms cause polarization" narrative. We are more cautious than in v1 about bridging: it is the most exposure-diverse policy and significantly beats engagement, but is not distinguishable from plain similarity on final variance at our seed count, and produces more, not fewer, opinion clusters (3.2 vs. ~2.1–2.3). This still suggests diversity injection alone may under-perform interventions that directly discourage extremity in ranking, but we now present that as directionally supported rather than established. Limitations: a stylized 1-D scalar-opinion model without a real social network topology, multi-issue correlation, or agent heterogeneity in persuadability; the engagement score, even swept across weights, remains a linear proxy rather than a learned ranking model; and our permutation tests use only 5–10 seeds per cell, so subtler comparisons would benefit from larger seed counts. Future work should test the decomposition under a networked (non-random-pool) topology — where an agent's candidate pool is itself opinion-correlated with its neighbors, likely amplifying both effects together — under a learned engagement proxy, and whether extremity-penalizing re-ranking beats diversity injection in a direct head-to-head mitigation comparison.
