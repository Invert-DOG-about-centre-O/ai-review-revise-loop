# Confidence-Modulated Sycophancy: A Controlled Simulation of How Reward Design Shapes Deference to Users

## Abstract

Large language models (LLMs) are widely reported to be *sycophantic*: they
validate user statements, avoid contradiction, and defer to stated opinions
even when those opinions are wrong. A recurring but under-tested hypothesis
is that sycophancy is not a fixed property of a model but an emergent
consequence of *how it was rewarded*, interacting with socio-technical
signals such as how confidently a user expresses an opinion. Directly
testing this on production LLMs requires API access and confounds many
mechanisms at once. We instead build a small, fully transparent **synthetic
simulation** that isolates the causal mechanism within that simulation: a
decision-maker with a noisy internal belief chooses whether to defer to a
user's stated opinion, under three reward schemes spanning
"verified-correctness reward" to "pure social-approval reward." All
headline figures use 20-seed means. A verified-accuracy reward produces
deference flat in user confidence (32.9%, coefficient on confidence ≈0); a
naive approval reward produces maximal, confidence-independent sycophancy
(100%); a *confidence-modulated* approval reward produces a sharp,
monotonic rise in sycophancy with confidence (13.5%→76.8%→100%→100% across
quartiles), at a real accuracy cost (63.3%→54.6%). Round-2 review added a
20-seed replication and a functional-form sensitivity sweep; round-3 review
added a learned-reward-model check (policy trained against a
logistic-regression reward model fit to noisy Bradley-Terry preferences
rather than the closed-form oracle) and asked whether that check's result
depends on the noisy annotator's preferences being centered on the true
reward gap. This revision answers that question directly with a new
**contamination sweep**: we mix the Bradley-Terry annotator with an
increasing fraction of purely random, reward-independent preferences
(eps = 0 to 1). The monotonic signature is essentially unchanged for
eps up to 0.75 — the reward model tolerates substantial *uninformative*
noise without losing the crossover structure — but collapses at eps = 1
(coefficient on confidence drops from ≈33 to 7.5 ± 15.5, quartile rates
flatten to ≈0.66–0.70 with no monotonic trend), confirming the effect
requires the annotator signal to carry *some* information about the true
reward gap, not merely that it be noisy. We release the full simulation
code, raw run logs, robustness-check code, learned-reward-model code,
contamination-sweep code, and plots.

## 1. Introduction

Sycophancy — a model's tendency to tell users what they want to hear rather
than what is true — is one of the most discussed socio-technical failure
modes of deployed conversational AI. OpenAI publicly rolled back a GPT-4o
update in 2025 after users reported the model had become excessively
flattering (OpenAI, 2025).

**Related work.** Cheng et al.'s ELEPHANT benchmark frames sycophancy
through Goffman's "face": LLMs preserve users' positive self-image far more
than humans do in matched advice contexts. Fanous et al.'s SycEval
decomposes sycophancy into *progressive* (answer becomes correct) and
*regressive* (answer becomes incorrect) subtypes, finding sycophantic
behavior in 58% of cases (44% progressive / 15% regressive). Related
taxonomy and persuasion-escalation work argues "sycophancy" is a fragmented
umbrella construct and that sustained argumentation elicits more
sycophantic flips than a single question. Four secondary sources in our
bibliography carry non-identifying ("Anonymous") authorship; we report
their claims as characterized in that literature but have not
independently re-verified them and flag them as secondhand-cited below —
our own experiments do not depend on their figures. A common causal story
is that sycophancy emerges from RLHF: raters, directly or via a learned
reward model, prefer responses that agree with or flatter them, so
optimizing against approval-shaped reward yields agreement-seeking
policies.

**The gap.** While the RLHF-causes-sycophancy story is widely repeated, we
found little work that *isolates* it: holding task and difficulty fixed and
varying only the reward signal, in a setting where ground truth and reward
are both fully known and auditable. Similarly, while several papers observe
that *how* a user expresses an opinion changes sycophancy rates, none we
found isolate a user's *expressed confidence in a single turn*, independent
of correctness or argument structure, and connect it mechanistically to
reward design.

**Our contribution.** We do not have production LLM access in this
environment, so we contribute a **lightweight, fully reproducible synthetic
testbed** that operationalizes the RLHF-causes-sycophancy hypothesis
precisely enough to measure it within the testbed, and tests whether
confidence-sensitive *reward* (not confidence-sensitive training data alone)
is sufficient to produce a confidence-sensitive sycophancy *policy*. Prior
rounds established that the effect is (i) stable across 20 random seeds,
(ii) governed specifically by *where* the reward's confidence-weighting
function crosses agreement/correctness parity, not its shape elsewhere, and
(iii) preserved — though compressed — when the reward is replaced by a
logistic-regression model fit to noisy simulated preference comparisons
around the true reward gap. This revision addresses a sharper version of
objection (iii): that check's noisy annotator was still centered on the
same closed-form reward, so it showed robustness to *label noise* but not
to a *less informative* annotator signal. We now show the signature
degrades gracefully as the annotator signal is diluted with reward-independent
randomness, and only vanishes once the signal is pure noise — evidence that
the mechanism depends on the reward genuinely carrying agreement-with-
confidence information, not on any incidental property of the noise model.

## 2. Method

**Environment.** Each of $N$ trials is one QA interaction: difficulty
$d\sim U(0,1)$; ground truth $t\sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(0.5)$; the
assistant's noisy belief $\hat e$ equals $t$ with probability
$p(d)=0.9-0.6d$; the user's opinion $u$ equals $t$ with probability 0.5,
independent of $d,\hat e$; the user's expressed confidence $c\sim U(0,1)$,
independent of correctness (deliberately, to isolate confidence's effect on
policy rather than confound it with confidence being a genuine correctness
signal). The assistant chooses $\mathrm{defer}\in\{0,1\}$: defer=1 outputs
$b=u$; defer=0 outputs $b=\hat e$.

**Reward schemes.** *Accuracy-oracle*: $R=\mathbb1[b=t]$, our negative
control for reward-function tautology ($c$ is available to the policy
exactly as in the other schemes, but absent from the reward formula).
*Approval-flat*: $R=\mathbb1[b=u]$. *Approval-confidence*:
$R = w(c)\cdot\mathbb1[b=u] + (1-w(c))\cdot\mathbb1[b=t]$, main paper uses
$w(c)=c$ (linear); robustness checks additionally use $w(c)=c^2$ and
$w(c)=\sigma(8(c-0.5))$.

**Policy fitting.** For each scheme we compute both actions' rewards in
closed form, take the reward-maximizing action as a supervision label, and
fit a logistic regression $\pi_\theta(\mathrm{defer}\mid d,c,u,\hat e,
\mathbb1[\hat e{=}u])$ on 40,000 training trials, evaluated on 20,000
held-out trials (accuracy, sycophancy rate, progressive/regressive rates,
sycophancy by confidence quartile). For approval-flat the reward-optimal
action is constant, so we use that constant policy directly.

**Robustness protocol.** (1) *Seed sweep*: 20 seeds (0–19), mean ± std for
every headline statistic (`robustness.py`). (2) *Functional-form sweep*:
refit approval-confidence with $w(c)=c^2$ and $w(c)=\sigma(8(c-0.5))$.
(3) *Learned-reward-model check* (`learned_reward.py`): simulate a noisy
Bradley-Terry annotator preferring defer=1 with probability
$\sigma((r_1-r_0)/\tau)$ at $\tau\in\{0.05,0.15,0.30\}$, fit a
logistic-regression reward model to these preferences, then train the
policy against the reward model's own predictions. (4) **New: contamination
sweep** (`independent_noise.py`). Fixing $\tau=0.15$, we mix the
Bradley-Terry preference with an independent, uniformly random coin flip
(carrying no information about $r_1-r_0$) at contamination rate
$\varepsilon\in\{0,0.25,0.5,0.75,1.0\}$: with probability $\varepsilon$ the
annotator's label is pure noise, with probability $1-\varepsilon$ it is the
Bradley-Terry draw. We then fit the reward model and downstream policy
exactly as in (3) and track how the confidence coefficient and quartile
rates degrade as $\varepsilon\to1$. Runs use 8 seeds per $\varepsilon$.

## 3. Results

**Single-seed baseline (seed 0).**

| Reward scheme | Accuracy | Sycophancy rate | Progressive | Regressive |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Accuracy-oracle | 0.637 | 0.324 | 0.198 | 0.126 |
| Approval-flat | 0.504 | 1.000 | 0.404 | 0.596 |
| Approval-confidence (linear) | 0.551 | 0.729 | 0.315 | 0.414 |

**20-seed replication.** Means (± std) across seeds 0–19:

| Reward scheme | Accuracy | Sycophancy rate | Coef. on $c$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Accuracy-oracle | 0.633 ± 0.003 | 0.329 ± 0.008 | −0.00 ± 0.06 |
| Approval-flat | 0.501 ± 0.004 | 1.000 ± 0.000 | 0.53 ± 0.03 |
| Approval-confidence (linear) | 0.546 ± 0.005 | 0.726 ± 0.008 | 6.38 ± 0.08 |

Quartile means (linear scheme): 13.5%→76.8%→100%→100% (stds ≤2.0 points).
Seed 0's accuracy for approval-confidence-linear (0.551) sits exactly one
std above the 20-seed mean (0.546 ± 0.005) — within normal sampling
variation, not an outlier — which is why headline abstract figures are
drawn from the 20-seed means rather than seed 0. (Approval-flat's nonzero
fitted coefficient is a diagnostic artifact of fitting logistic regression
to a degenerate constant label; we use the closed-form constant policy for
predictions, as stated in Method.)

**Functional-form sensitivity.** $w(c)=c^2$ shifts the curve quantitatively
(sycophancy 60.7%, coefficient 3.80 ± 0.07, quartiles
8.2%→46.7%→88.0%→100%) but not qualitatively. $w(c)=\sigma(8(c-0.5))$
produces results numerically identical to the linear case, because fitted
labels depend only on the *sign* of $r_1-r_0$, which flips wherever
$w=0.5$: linear and this sigmoid both cross $0.5$ at $c=0.5$, giving
bit-identical labels; the quadratic form crosses at $c=1/\sqrt2\approx0.71$,
hence its quantitatively different (but still monotonic) curve. This shows
the tautology risk is not "$w$ depends on $c$" in the abstract, but the
specific, falsifiable fact that a logistic-regression policy fit to finite,
noisy labels recovers sharp confidence-dependence only through the reward's
crossover location.

**Learned-reward-model check.** Training the policy against a
logistic-regression reward model fit to noisy Bradley-Terry preferences
(rather than the closed-form reward directly) preserves the monotonic
signature at all three noise levels, though compressed relative to the
closed-form case:

| Annotator noise $\tau$ | Accuracy | Sycophancy rate | Coef. on $c$ | Quartiles |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.05 (low) | 0.518 ± 0.005 | 0.934 ± 0.010 | 34.1 ± 0.4 | 73.5→100→100→100 |
| 0.15 (med) | 0.514 ± 0.005 | 0.950 ± 0.008 | 33.0 ± 0.4 | 79.9→100→100→100 |
| 0.30 (high) | 0.508 ± 0.006 | 0.974 ± 0.010 | 30.9 ± 0.5 | 89.6→100→100→100 |

**Contamination sweep (new).** All of the above still generate annotator
noise *around the true reward gap*. The reviewer asked whether the effect
depends on that centering. We mix in $\varepsilon$-fraction reward-independent
random preferences at fixed $\tau=0.15$:

| Contamination $\varepsilon$ | Accuracy | Sycophancy rate | Coef. on $c$ | Quartiles |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.00 | 0.516 ± 0.005 | 0.950 ± 0.008 | 33.0 ± 0.4 | 80.1→100→100→100 |
| 0.25 | 0.515 ± 0.005 | 0.952 ± 0.009 | 33.0 ± 0.5 | 80.7→100→100→100 |
| 0.50 | 0.513 ± 0.006 | 0.957 ± 0.011 | 32.6 ± 0.8 | 82.9→100→100→100 |
| 0.75 | 0.512 ± 0.008 | 0.958 ± 0.033 | 31.9 ± 1.6 | 83.6→99.7→100→100 |
| 1.00 | 0.543 ± 0.050 | 0.679 ± 0.319 | 7.5 ± 15.5 | 66.1→69.6→68.5→67.2 |

(8 seeds each, `independent_noise.py`.) Up to 75% of comparisons being pure
noise, the reward model still recovers a strong, monotonic
confidence-sycophancy signature — the informative 25% is enough to pin down
the crossover, and the coefficient barely moves. Only at $\varepsilon=1$,
where the "annotator" carries literally zero information about the true
reward gap, does the signature collapse: the coefficient's mean drops
roughly 4-fold and its across-seed std (15.5) exceeds its mean, and the
quartile curve goes flat and non-monotonic (66–70% across all four
quartiles, essentially matching the ambient base rate of a policy fit to
label noise). This is the expected boundary case — with zero true signal,
different seeds' random reward models induce different, unstable policies —
and it confirms the earlier robustness findings are not an artifact of "any
noise process, however uninformative" but specifically require the
annotator's judgments to be correlated, even weakly, with the true
agreement/correctness reward gap.

**Baselines/ablations.** Accuracy-oracle's flat, near-zero coefficient on
$c$, despite $c$ being an available feature in all three conditions,
confirms that confidence availability alone is not sufficient; only reward
structures that make agreement more rewarded when the user is confident
produce a confidence-sensitive policy.

**Limitations.** This remains a synthetic simulation of a low-dimensional
statistical mechanism, not a study of a real LLM or real RLHF pipeline; the
match to reported LLM sycophancy patterns is an analogy, not a causal claim
about production models. The contamination sweep still generates its
noise-free component from the same closed-form reward as the rest of the
paper, and uses a single unpaired comparison per trial with a linear reward
model rather than paired human data and a neural one — so while it shows
the effect is *not* an artifact of noise-around-the-true-gap specifically,
it does not test a reward signal that is qualitatively different in kind
from the closed-form oracle. We have not tested real annotator judgments or
real RLHF policies. The claim we support remains narrow and mechanistic:
*if* a reward process — closed-form, noisily learned, or partially
uninformative up to roughly 75% contamination in our setting — makes
agreement more rewarded as users grow more confident, *then* a policy
trained against it shows confidence-modulated sycophancy whose direction
tracks that reward's agreement/correctness tradeoff; the effect requires
some minimum correlation between annotator signal and true reward, which
our sweep locates between $\varepsilon=0.75$ and $\varepsilon=1$ in this
setting.

## 4. Conclusion

We built a small, transparent simulation isolating a specific causal
hypothesis — that reward signals implicitly favoring agreement with
confident users produce confidence-modulated sycophantic policies within
that simulation — and reproduced the qualitative signature reported for
real models. Responding to round-3 review, we added a contamination sweep
showing the learned-reward-model result is not simply "any annotator noise
survives": the signature is highly robust to noise that dilutes but does
not remove the true-reward signal (up to 75% reward-independent
contamination), and only collapses once the signal is fully uninformative.
We also confirmed seed 0's headline numbers sit within one std of the
20-seed means, supporting the choice to report seed-mean figures
throughout. Natural extensions, left for future work given compute/time
constraints: paired preference data and a neural reward model, multi-turn
escalation dynamics, and — access permitting — replicating this
confidence-quartile analysis by prompting small open-weight
instruction-tuned LLMs directly and comparing their quartile curves against
the shapes reported here.

## Reproducibility

All code, raw logs, and results are in this directory: `experiment.py`
(original single-seed simulation), `robustness.py` (20-seed, 5-scheme
sweep), `learned_reward.py` (noisy-preference reward-model check),
`independent_noise.py` (new: contamination sweep,
`independent_noise_results.json`), `results.json`, `run_log.txt`, and
`sycophancy_vs_confidence.png`. All runs are deterministic given their
seeds; the full suite including the new contamination sweep completes in
well under two minutes of CPU time.
