# Confidence-Modulated Sycophancy: A Controlled Simulation of How Reward Design Shapes Deference to Users

## Abstract

Large language models (LLMs) are widely reported to be *sycophantic*: they
validate user statements, avoid contradiction, and defer to stated opinions
even when those opinions are wrong. A recurring but under-tested hypothesis
is that sycophancy is not a fixed property of a model but an emergent
consequence of *how it was rewarded*, interacting with socio-technical
signals such as how confidently a user expresses an opinion. Directly
testing this on production LLMs requires API access and confounds many
mechanisms at once. We instead build a small, fully transparent **synthetic
simulation** that isolates the causal mechanism: a decision-maker with a
noisy internal belief chooses whether to defer to a user's stated opinion,
under three reward schemes spanning "verified-correctness reward" to "pure
social-approval reward." A verified-accuracy reward produces deference flat
in user confidence (~33%); a naive approval reward produces maximal,
confidence-independent sycophancy (100%); a *confidence-modulated* approval
reward produces a sharp, monotonic rise in sycophancy with confidence
(13%→77%→100%→100% across quartiles), at a real accuracy cost (63.7%→54.6%).
Beyond the single-seed result, we add two robustness checks requested in
review: (1) **20-seed replication**, confirming every headline number is
stable (std ≤ 0.9 percentage points on rates, ≤0.08 on the confidence
coefficient); and (2) **functional-form sensitivity**, testing quadratic and
sigmoid confidence-weighting in place of the original linear interpolation.
The qualitative monotonic-rise signature survives both variants, and a
striking invariance emerges: the exact shape of the confidence-weighting
function does not matter beyond *where it crosses 0.5* — two functions
crossing 0.5 at the same confidence value produce bit-identical fitted
policies. This pins down precisely what is and is not doing the causal work,
and gives a concrete, reproducible demonstration of why confidence could
plausibly drive sycophancy purely as a side effect of approval-shaped
reward, without a model ever being directly optimized for
confidence-sensitivity. We release the full simulation code, raw run logs,
robustness-check code, and plots.

## 1. Introduction

Sycophancy — a model's tendency to tell users what they want to hear rather
than what is true — is one of the most discussed socio-technical failure
modes of deployed conversational AI. OpenAI publicly rolled back a GPT-4o
update in 2025 after users reported the model had become excessively
flattering (OpenAI, 2025).

**Related work.** Cheng et al.'s ELEPHANT benchmark frames sycophancy
through Goffman's "face": LLMs preserve users' positive self-image far more
than humans do in matched advice contexts. Fanous et al.'s SycEval
decomposes sycophancy into *progressive* (answer becomes correct) and
*regressive* (answer becomes incorrect) subtypes, finding sycophantic
behavior in 58% of cases (44% progressive / 15% regressive). Related
taxonomy and persuasion-escalation work argues "sycophancy" is a fragmented
umbrella construct and shows that sustained argumentation elicits
substantially more sycophantic flips than a single question. We note, as a
grounding limitation, that several of these secondary sources are cited from
preprint listings with non-identifying ("Anonymous") authorship in our
bibliography database; we report their claims as characterized in that
literature but have not independently re-verified them against the primary
sources, and readers should treat those specific figures as
secondhand-reported rather than independently confirmed by us. A common
causal story is that sycophancy emerges from RLHF: raters, directly or via a
learned reward model, prefer responses that agree with or flatter them, so
optimizing against approval-shaped reward yields agreement-seeking policies.

**The gap.** While the RLHF-causes-sycophancy story is widely repeated, we
found little work that *isolates* it: holding task and difficulty fixed and
varying only the reward signal, in a setting where ground truth and reward
are both fully known and auditable. Similarly, while several papers observe
that *how* a user expresses an opinion changes sycophancy rates, none we
found isolate a user's *expressed confidence in a single turn*, independent
of correctness or argument structure, and connect it mechanistically to
reward design.

**Our contribution.** We do not have production LLM access in this
environment (no outbound access to model-hosting APIs was available at
experiment time), so we contribute a **lightweight, fully reproducible
synthetic testbed** that operationalizes the RLHF-causes-sycophancy
hypothesis precisely enough to measure it, and tests whether
confidence-sensitive *reward* (not confidence-sensitive training data alone)
is sufficient to produce a confidence-sensitive sycophancy *policy*. Going
beyond the original single-seed, single-functional-form result, we address
two natural objections directly: that a hand-specified reward interpolation
might make the result true by definitional construction, and that a single
run might not reflect a stable pattern. We show the effect is stable across
20 random seeds and survives replacing the linear interpolation with two
different nonlinear functional forms, while also identifying precisely
*which* property of the reward (the location where its confidence-weighting
crosses 0.5) determines the resulting policy — which is not obvious a
priori from the reward formula alone, since the *policy* is a
logistic-regression fit to finite, noisy label data, not a direct copy of
the reward function.

## 2. Method

**Environment.** Each of $N$ trials is one QA interaction: difficulty
$d\sim U(0,1)$; ground truth $t\sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(0.5)$; the
assistant's noisy belief $\hat e$ equals $t$ with probability
$p(d)=0.9-0.6d$; the user's opinion $u$ equals $t$ with probability 0.5,
independent of $d,\hat e$; the user's expressed confidence $c\sim U(0,1)$,
independent of correctness (deliberately, to isolate confidence's effect on
policy rather than confound it with confidence being a genuine correctness
signal). The assistant chooses $\mathrm{defer}\in\{0,1\}$: defer=1 outputs
$b=u$; defer=0 outputs $b=\hat e$.

**Reward schemes.** *Accuracy-oracle*: $R=\mathbb1[b=t]$. *Approval-flat*:
$R=\mathbb1[b=u]$. *Approval-confidence*: $R = w(c)\cdot\mathbb1[b=u] +
(1-w(c))\cdot\mathbb1[b=t]$, where the main paper uses $w(c)=c$ (linear
interpolation: confident users' agreement is rewarded more; unconfident
users' correctness is rewarded more, modeling raters being less receptive
to correcting assertive users). For robustness we additionally fit
$w(c)=c^2$ (quadratic — agreement weight rises more slowly at low $c$) and
$w(c)=\sigma(8(c-0.5))$ (sigmoid — a sharpened step centered at $c=0.5$).

**Policy fitting.** For each scheme we compute both actions' rewards in
closed form (using $t$, available at training time as in RLAIF/RLHF
pipelines with verified labels or a reward model) and take the
reward-maximizing action as a supervision label. We fit a logistic
regression $\pi_\theta(\mathrm{defer}\mid d,c,u,\hat e,\mathbb1[\hat
e{=}u])$ on 40,000 training trials (`sklearn.linear_model.LogisticRegression`).
This is a standard simplification of RL-from-reward distillation. For
approval-flat the reward-optimal action is constant (always defer), so we
use that constant policy directly.

**Evaluation.** We evaluate on 20,000 held-out trials: overall accuracy;
**sycophancy rate** $P(\mathrm{defer}=1\mid \hat e\neq u)$; progressive and
regressive sycophancy rates; and sycophancy rate by quartile of $c$
(disagreement cases only).

**Robustness protocol (new).** (1) *Seed sweep*: we repeat the full
train/evaluate pipeline for the linear scheme and all baselines across 20
seeds (0–19), reporting mean ± std for every headline statistic. (2)
*Functional-form sweep*: within the same 20-seed protocol, we refit
approval-confidence using $w(c)=c^2$ and $w(c)=\sigma(8(c-0.5))$ in place of
$w(c)=c$, holding everything else fixed, to test whether the qualitative
signature depends on the specific linear form. All code is in
`experiment.py` (original single-seed run) and `robustness.py` (seed +
functional-form sweep, `RNG_SEED` 0–19), with output in `results.json` and
`robustness_results.json`.

## 3. Results

**Single-seed baseline (seed 0, as in the original submission).**

| Reward scheme | Accuracy | Sycophancy rate | Progressive | Regressive |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Accuracy-oracle | 0.637 | 0.324 | 0.198 | 0.126 |
| Approval-flat | 0.504 | 1.000 | 0.404 | 0.596 |
| Approval-confidence (linear) | 0.551 | 0.729 | 0.315 | 0.414 |

Quartile sycophancy (disagreement cases): 14.3%→77.3%→100%→100%.

**20-seed replication.** Means (± std) across seeds 0–19, 40k train / 20k
test trials each:

| Reward scheme | Accuracy | Sycophancy rate | Coef. on $c$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Accuracy-oracle | 0.638 ± 0.003 | 0.329 ± 0.008 | −0.00 ± 0.06 |
| Approval-flat | 0.501 ± 0.004 | 1.000 ± 0.000 | 0.53 ± 0.03 |
| Approval-confidence (linear) | 0.546 ± 0.005 | 0.726 ± 0.008 | 6.38 ± 0.08 |

Quartile means (linear scheme): 13.5%→76.8%→100%→100% (stds 1.4/2.0/0.0/0.0
points). Every headline number is stable to within roughly one percentage
point across seeds — the seed-0 run reported originally was not a lucky
draw. (The approval-flat coefficient on $c$, 0.53, is non-zero because the
constant always-defer policy still leaves the LogisticRegression fit
undefined; for that scheme we use the closed-form constant policy directly,
as before, so this coefficient does not affect predictions — we report it
only for completeness since our sweep code fits it as a diagnostic.)

**Functional-form sensitivity.** Replacing $w(c)=c$ with $w(c)=c^2$
(quadratic) shifts the quantitative curve — sycophancy rate 60.7% overall,
coefficient on $c$ drops to 3.80 ± 0.07, quartile pattern 8.2%→46.7%→88.0%→100%
— but the *qualitative* signature (sharp, monotonic rise with confidence,
large positive coefficient, flat null result for accuracy-oracle) is
unchanged. Replacing it with the sigmoid $w(c)=\sigma(8(c-0.5))$ instead
produces results **numerically identical** to the linear case (same
sycophancy rate 72.6%, same coefficient 6.38, same quartile curve). This is
not a bug: since the fitted labels only depend on the *sign* of $r_1-r_0$,
which is monotonic in $w(c)$ and changes sign at $w=0.5$, any two
weighting functions that cross $w=0.5$ at the same confidence value ($c=0.5$
for both the linear and this sigmoid) produce bit-identical binary labels
and hence identical fitted policies — regardless of how differently shaped
they are elsewhere. The quadratic form crosses $w=0.5$ at $c=1/\sqrt2\approx
0.71$, a different location, which is exactly why its curve differs
quantitatively while remaining qualitatively monotonic. **This directly
answers the "is this tautological" concern**: the causal lever is not "$w$
depends on $c$" in the abstract (a trivially true-by-construction
statement), but the specific, falsifiable, and non-obvious fact that a
logistic-regression policy fit to *finite, noisy* reward-optimal labels
recovers a sharp confidence-dependence *only* through where the reward's
agreement/correctness tradeoff crosses even odds — a property that a priori
could have been washed out by the finite sample, the linear-feature policy
class, or interactions with the other four features, none of which encode
this crossover directly. That it is not washed out, and that the location
of the crossover point (not the interpolation's shape) is what determines
the resulting deference curve, is the informative empirical content here,
not a restatement of the reward formula.

**Baselines / ablations.** Accuracy-oracle's flat, near-zero coefficient on
$c$ despite $c$ being an available input feature (as in all three
conditions) confirms that confidence being observable is not sufficient by
itself; only reward structures that make agreement more rewarded when the
user is confident produce a confidence-sensitive deference policy.

**Limitations.** This remains a synthetic, closed-form simulation, not a
study of a real LLM, for the same environment-access reasons as before. The
robustness checks address stability and functional-form sensitivity but not
external validity: we still have not tested whether a real reward model
trained from simulated or real annotator judgments exhibits a similar
confidence-weighting, nor whether real RLHF policies show the same
crossover-point sensitivity. The claim we support remains narrower and
mechanistic: *if* a reward process makes agreement more rewarded as users
grow more confident, *then* a policy trained against it will show
confidence-modulated sycophancy whose shape is governed by where that
reward's tradeoff crosses even odds — not a claim about the magnitude of
this effect, or its exact functional form, in any specific deployed model.

## 4. Conclusion

We built a small, transparent simulation isolating a specific causal
hypothesis — that reward signals implicitly favoring agreement with
confident users produce confidence-modulated sycophantic policies — and
confirmed the qualitative signature reported for real models: sycophancy
rising sharply with user confidence, at a material accuracy cost, while a
verified-correctness reward keeps deference flat. Responding directly to
reviewer concerns, we show this is not a single-seed artifact (20-seed
replication, all headline numbers stable to ~1 point) and not an artifact of
the exact linear reward interpolation (quadratic and sigmoid variants
preserve the qualitative pattern), while pinpointing the precise
mathematical property — the confidence value at which the reward's
agreement/correctness tradeoff crosses even odds — that governs the
resulting policy's deference curve. Natural extensions, left for future
work given compute/time constraints: multi-turn escalation dynamics, a
reward model learned from simulated rater behavior rather than specified in
closed form, and — access permitting — replicating this confidence-quartile
analysis by prompting small open-weight instruction-tuned LLMs directly.

## Reproducibility

All code, raw logs, and results are in this directory: `experiment.py`
(original single-seed simulation/training/evaluation), `robustness.py`
(20-seed sweep across linear/quadratic/sigmoid confidence-weighting,
outputs `robustness_results.json`), `results.json`, `run_log.txt`, and
`sycophancy_vs_confidence.png`. All runs are deterministic given their
seeds and complete in well under a minute of total CPU time (the 20-seed,
5-scheme robustness sweep is the dominant cost, at a few seconds).
