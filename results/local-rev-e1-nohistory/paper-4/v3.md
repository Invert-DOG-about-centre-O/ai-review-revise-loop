# Confidence-Modulated Sycophancy: A Controlled Simulation of How Reward Design Shapes Deference to Users

## Abstract

Large language models (LLMs) are widely reported to be *sycophantic*: they
validate user statements, avoid contradiction, and defer to stated opinions
even when those opinions are wrong. A recurring but under-tested hypothesis
is that sycophancy is not a fixed property of a model but an emergent
consequence of *how it was rewarded*, interacting with socio-technical
signals such as how confidently a user expresses an opinion. Directly
testing this on production LLMs requires API access and confounds many
mechanisms at once. We instead build a small, fully transparent **synthetic
simulation** that isolates the causal mechanism: a decision-maker with a
noisy internal belief chooses whether to defer to a user's stated opinion,
under three reward schemes spanning "verified-correctness reward" to "pure
social-approval reward." Using 20-seed means throughout for consistency, a
verified-accuracy reward produces deference flat in user confidence
(32.9%, coefficient on confidence ≈0); a naive approval reward produces
maximal, confidence-independent sycophancy (100%); a *confidence-modulated*
approval reward produces a sharp, monotonic rise in sycophancy with
confidence (13.5%→76.8%→100%→100% across quartiles), at a real accuracy
cost (63.3%→54.6%). This revision adds three checks requested in review:
(1) **20-seed replication**, confirming every headline number is stable
(std ≤0.9 points on rates); (2) **functional-form sensitivity**, showing the
qualitative signature survives quadratic and sigmoid confidence-weighting,
and that two weighting functions crossing agreement-vs-correctness parity
at the same confidence value produce bit-identical fitted policies; and (3),
new in this revision, a **learned-reward-model check**: instead of fitting
the policy to the closed-form oracle reward directly, we simulate noisy
Bradley-Terry-style preference comparisons around that reward, fit a
logistic-regression reward model to the noisy comparisons, and train the
policy against *that*. The monotonic confidence-sycophancy signature
survives this additional layer of indirection and noise at every annotator-noise
level tested, though the transition compresses toward saturating faster than
in the closed-form case. We release the full simulation code, raw run logs,
robustness-check code, learned-reward-model code, and plots.

## 1. Introduction

Sycophancy — a model's tendency to tell users what they want to hear rather
than what is true — is one of the most discussed socio-technical failure
modes of deployed conversational AI. OpenAI publicly rolled back a GPT-4o
update in 2025 after users reported the model had become excessively
flattering (OpenAI, 2025).

**Related work.** Cheng et al.'s ELEPHANT benchmark frames sycophancy
through Goffman's "face": LLMs preserve users' positive self-image far more
than humans do in matched advice contexts. Fanous et al.'s SycEval
decomposes sycophancy into *progressive* (answer becomes correct) and
*regressive* (answer becomes incorrect) subtypes, finding sycophantic
behavior in 58% of cases (44% progressive / 15% regressive). Related
taxonomy and persuasion-escalation work argues "sycophancy" is a fragmented
umbrella construct and shows that sustained argumentation elicits
substantially more sycophantic flips than a single question. Several of
these secondary sources are cited from preprint listings with
non-identifying ("Anonymous") authorship in our bibliography database; we
report their claims as characterized in that literature but have not
independently re-verified them, and readers should treat those figures as
secondhand-reported. A common causal story is that sycophancy emerges from
RLHF: raters, directly or via a learned reward model, prefer responses that
agree with or flatter them, so optimizing against approval-shaped reward
yields agreement-seeking policies.

**The gap.** While the RLHF-causes-sycophancy story is widely repeated, we
found little work that *isolates* it: holding task and difficulty fixed and
varying only the reward signal, in a setting where ground truth and reward
are both fully known and auditable. Similarly, while several papers observe
that *how* a user expresses an opinion changes sycophancy rates, none we
found isolate a user's *expressed confidence in a single turn*, independent
of correctness or argument structure, and connect it mechanistically to
reward design.

**Our contribution.** We do not have production LLM access in this
environment, so we contribute a **lightweight, fully reproducible synthetic
testbed** that operationalizes the RLHF-causes-sycophancy hypothesis
precisely enough to measure it, and tests whether confidence-sensitive
*reward* (not confidence-sensitive training data alone) is sufficient to
produce a confidence-sensitive sycophancy *policy*. This revision addresses
three objections raised in review: that a hand-specified reward
interpolation might make the result true by construction, that a single run
might not reflect a stable pattern, and that fitting the policy directly to
a closed-form oracle reward sidesteps the noisy, learned nature of real
reward models. We show the effect is (i) stable across 20 random seeds,
(ii) governed specifically by *where* the reward's confidence-weighting
function crosses agreement/correctness parity, not its shape elsewhere, and
(iii) preserved — though quantitatively compressed — when the reward itself
is replaced by a logistic-regression model fit to noisy simulated preference
comparisons rather than specified in closed form.

## 2. Method

**Environment.** Each of $N$ trials is one QA interaction: difficulty
$d\sim U(0,1)$; ground truth $t\sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(0.5)$; the
assistant's noisy belief $\hat e$ equals $t$ with probability
$p(d)=0.9-0.6d$; the user's opinion $u$ equals $t$ with probability 0.5,
independent of $d,\hat e$; the user's expressed confidence $c\sim U(0,1)$,
independent of correctness (deliberately, to isolate confidence's effect on
policy rather than confound it with confidence being a genuine correctness
signal). The assistant chooses $\mathrm{defer}\in\{0,1\}$: defer=1 outputs
$b=u$; defer=0 outputs $b=\hat e$.

**Reward schemes.** *Accuracy-oracle*: $R=\mathbb1[b=t]$. This scheme is
also our negative control for reward-function tautology: $c$ is available
to the assistant and to the downstream policy fit exactly as in the other
two schemes, but the reward formula makes zero reference to $c$; a
near-zero fitted coefficient on $c$ under this scheme (§3) confirms that
merely *having access to* confidence is not sufficient to produce
confidence-sensitive deference. *Approval-flat*: $R=\mathbb1[b=u]$.
*Approval-confidence*: $R = w(c)\cdot\mathbb1[b=u] + (1-w(c))\cdot\mathbb1[b=t]$,
main paper uses $w(c)=c$ (linear); robustness checks additionally use
$w(c)=c^2$ (quadratic) and $w(c)=\sigma(8(c-0.5))$ (sharpened sigmoid).

**Policy fitting.** For each scheme we compute both actions' rewards in
closed form and take the reward-maximizing action as a supervision label,
fitting a logistic regression $\pi_\theta(\mathrm{defer}\mid d,c,u,\hat e,
\mathbb1[\hat e{=}u])$ on 40,000 training trials
(`sklearn.linear_model.LogisticRegression`), evaluated on 20,000 held-out
trials with the same metrics as before (accuracy, sycophancy rate,
progressive/regressive rates, sycophancy by confidence quartile). For
approval-flat the reward-optimal action is constant, so we use that
constant policy directly.

**Robustness protocol.** (1) *Seed sweep*: 20 seeds (0–19), mean ± std for
every headline statistic (`robustness.py`). (2) *Functional-form sweep*:
within the same protocol, refit approval-confidence with $w(c)=c^2$ and
$w(c)=\sigma(8(c-0.5))$ in place of $w(c)=c$. (3) **New: learned-reward-model
check** (`learned_reward.py`). Rather than fitting the policy directly to
the closed-form oracle reward, we (a) compute the true reward gap
$r_1-r_0$ for the linear approval-confidence scheme on a fresh batch of
trials; (b) simulate a noisy Bradley-Terry-style annotator that prefers
defer=1 with probability $\sigma((r_1-r_0)/\tau)$, at three noise
temperatures $\tau\in\{0.05,0.15,0.30\}$ (higher $\tau$ = noisier
annotator); (c) fit a logistic-regression *reward model* to these noisy
binary preferences on the same five features as the policy; (d) generate
policy training labels from the reward model's own preferred action on a
fresh batch, and fit the downstream policy exactly as before. This tests
whether the crossover-driven signature survives an additional layer of
indirection and stochastic noise between the causal hypothesis (reward
favors agreement with confident users) and the fitted policy — closer to
how real RLHF reward models are trained from noisy human comparisons rather
than specified in closed form.

## 3. Results

**Single-seed baseline (seed 0).**

| Reward scheme | Accuracy | Sycophancy rate | Progressive | Regressive |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Accuracy-oracle | 0.637 | 0.324 | 0.198 | 0.126 |
| Approval-flat | 0.504 | 1.000 | 0.404 | 0.596 |
| Approval-confidence (linear) | 0.551 | 0.729 | 0.315 | 0.414 |

**20-seed replication.** Means (± std) across seeds 0–19:

| Reward scheme | Accuracy | Sycophancy rate | Coef. on $c$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Accuracy-oracle | 0.633 ± 0.003 | 0.329 ± 0.008 | −0.00 ± 0.06 |
| Approval-flat | 0.501 ± 0.004 | 1.000 ± 0.000 | 0.53 ± 0.03 |
| Approval-confidence (linear) | 0.546 ± 0.005 | 0.726 ± 0.008 | 6.38 ± 0.08 |

Quartile means (linear scheme): 13.5%→76.8%→100%→100% (stds ≤2.0 points).
**Correction from the previous revision:** the accuracy-oracle row was
previously mis-transcribed as 0.638 ± 0.003; rerunning `robustness.py`
verbatim gives 0.633 ± 0.003, which we report here. This does not affect
any qualitative claim — it is a null-coefficient row by construction — but
we flag it explicitly since round-2 review caught it against our own
reproducibility guarantee. Every other headline number reproduced exactly
against the checked-in code. (Approval-flat's nonzero coefficient is a
diagnostic artifact of our sweep code fitting a logistic regression to a
degenerate constant label; we use the closed-form constant policy for
predictions, as stated in Method.)

**Functional-form sensitivity.** $w(c)=c^2$ shifts the curve quantitatively
(sycophancy 60.7%, coefficient 3.80 ± 0.07, quartiles
8.2%→46.7%→88.0%→100%) but not qualitatively. $w(c)=\sigma(8(c-0.5))$
instead produces results numerically identical to the linear case (72.6%
sycophancy, coefficient 6.38, same quartiles). This is because fitted
labels depend only on the *sign* of $r_1-r_0$, which flips at $w=0.5$: any
two weighting functions crossing $0.5$ at the same $c$ (linear and this
sigmoid both cross at $c=0.5$) yield bit-identical binary labels and hence
identical fitted policies, regardless of shape elsewhere. The quadratic
form crosses $w=0.5$ at $c=1/\sqrt2\approx0.71$, a different location,
which is why its curve differs quantitatively while staying monotonic. This
is the answer to "is this tautological by construction": the lever is not
"$w$ depends on $c$" in the abstract, but the specific, falsifiable fact
that a logistic-regression policy fit to *finite, noisy* labels recovers
sharp confidence-dependence only through the reward's crossover location —
a property that could a priori have been washed out by sampling noise, the
linear-feature policy class, or interactions with the other four features.

**Learned-reward-model check (new).** Fitting the policy against a
logistic-regression reward model trained on noisy Bradley-Terry preferences
(rather than the closed-form reward directly) preserves the qualitative
signature at all three noise levels tested:

| Annotator noise $\tau$ | Accuracy | Sycophancy rate | Coef. on $c$ | Quartiles |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.05 (low) | 0.518 ± 0.005 | 0.934 ± 0.010 | 34.1 ± 0.4 | 73.5→100→100→100 |
| 0.15 (med) | 0.514 ± 0.005 | 0.950 ± 0.008 | 33.0 ± 0.4 | 79.9→100→100→100 |
| 0.30 (high) | 0.508 ± 0.006 | 0.974 ± 0.010 | 30.9 ± 0.5 | 89.6→100→100→100 |

(10 seeds each, `learned_reward.py`.) Sycophancy still rises with $c$ and
the confidence coefficient stays large and positive at every noise level,
so the qualitative claim survives an added layer of indirection. It is not
a free lunch, however: the transition compresses — quartiles 2–4 already
saturate at 100% rather than rising gradually as in the closed-form case,
and overall sycophancy is higher (93–97% vs. 72.6%) and accuracy lower
(51–52% vs. 54.6%). This is expected: the reward model is a *linear*
classifier over all five features fit to *noisy binary* labels, so its own
decision boundary in $c$ is blurrier and shifted relative to the sharp
$c=0.5$ crossover of the closed-form reward, and errors compound across the
reward-model-fit and policy-fit stages. The direction and monotonicity of
the effect survive; its precise quantitative shape does not transfer
cleanly through a learned, noisy reward stage — a caveat we consider
important context for extrapolating this mechanism to real RLHF pipelines,
where reward models are always fit to noisy comparisons.

**Baselines/ablations.** Accuracy-oracle's flat, near-zero coefficient on
$c$, despite $c$ being an available feature in all three conditions and
serving as the tautology negative control described in Method, confirms
that confidence availability alone is not sufficient; only reward
structures that make agreement more rewarded when the user is confident
produce a confidence-sensitive policy.

**Limitations.** This remains a synthetic simulation, not a study of a real
LLM. The learned-reward-model check narrows, but does not close, the gap
to real RLHF: our noisy-preference simulator is still built around the same
closed-form reward function as its ground truth, uses a single (unpaired)
comparison per trial rather than genuine paired preference data, and a
linear reward model rather than a neural one. We have not tested real
annotator judgments or real RLHF policies. The claim we support remains
narrower and mechanistic: *if* a reward process — closed-form or learned
from noisy comparisons — makes agreement more rewarded as users grow more
confident, *then* a policy trained against it will show confidence-modulated
sycophancy whose direction is governed by that reward's agreement/
correctness tradeoff, though a learned reward stage measurably compresses
and shifts the effect's quantitative shape.

## 4. Conclusion

We built a small, transparent simulation isolating a specific causal
hypothesis — that reward signals implicitly favoring agreement with
confident users produce confidence-modulated sycophantic policies — and
confirmed the qualitative signature reported for real models. Responding to
round-2 review, we (1) corrected a transcription error in the 20-seed
accuracy-oracle accuracy (0.638→0.633, no qualitative change), (2) clarified
that accuracy-oracle already serves as the intended negative control for
reward-function tautology, and (3) added a learned-reward-model check
showing the monotonic confidence-sycophancy signature survives fitting the
policy against a noisy, logistic-regression reward model instead of the
closed-form oracle — though with a compressed, less gradual transition.
Natural extensions, left for future work given compute/time constraints:
paired preference data and a neural reward model, multi-turn escalation
dynamics, and — access permitting — replicating this confidence-quartile
analysis by prompting small open-weight instruction-tuned LLMs directly.

## Reproducibility

All code, raw logs, and results are in this directory: `experiment.py`
(original single-seed simulation), `robustness.py` (20-seed, 5-scheme
sweep, `robustness_results.json`), `learned_reward.py` (new:
noisy-preference reward-model check, `learned_reward_results.json`),
`results.json`, `run_log.txt`, and `sycophancy_vs_confidence.png`. All runs
are deterministic given their seeds; the full suite including the new
learned-reward-model sweep completes in well under a minute of CPU time.
