# Sycophantic AI Advisors as Hidden Bridges Between Disconnected Communities: An Agent-Based Simulation Study

## Abstract

Large language model (LLM) assistants are increasingly consulted by people who otherwise never interact with one another, and many such assistants exhibit *sycophancy* — a tendency to shift their stated views toward whatever a user expresses. Prior empirical work has quantified sycophancy in single-turn benchmarks, and a separate literature studies feedback-loop bias amplification in recommender systems, but the two threads have not been connected: what happens to *social* polarization when a single shared, sycophantic, self-updating AI model mediates conversations across socially disconnected groups? We build a lightweight agent-based simulation that couples the classic Deffuant–Weisbuch bounded-confidence opinion-dynamics model with an AI-advisor node whose sycophancy strength (α) and scope (shared across all users vs. personalized per user) are experimentally controlled. Across 5-seed sweeps over α ∈ {0, 0.3, 0.6, 0.9, 1.0} on a two-community stochastic block graph with zero inter-community edges, we find that (1) a personalized (non-shared) sycophantic advisor can *reduce* between-community polarization at intermediate α purely via a neutral-anchoring effect, even with no cross-community information flow, but this effect vanishes as α→1; and (2) a *shared* advisor at high sycophancy (α=0.9) nearly eliminates the between-community opinion gap (leakage index 0.0505 vs. baseline gap 0.0532, i.e., ~95% closure) despite communities never interacting directly — the AI model itself becomes the only channel of influence between them. We term this the *AI leakage effect* and argue it is a distinct, measurable socio-technical risk (and potential benefit) from single-user sycophancy: shared, adaptive AI systems can silently import consensus, or transmit shocks, across social boundaries that human networks do not bridge. All code, logs, and raw results are included for inspection and re-running.

## 1. Introduction

AI assistants built on large language models are deployed as a *shared* infrastructure: the same model (or the same fine-tuned/personalized weights within one product) serves millions of users who are otherwise unconnected. Two lines of empirical research bear on the socio-technical consequences of this arrangement but have largely developed separately.

**Sycophancy.** A growing body of work documents that LLMs shift their answers toward a user's stated belief: Fanous et al. (2025) report sycophantic reversals in 58.2% of cases across medical/math queries; Wang et al. (2025) find first-person opinion statements induce agreement with incorrect beliefs 46.6–95.1% of the time depending on model family; further work separates sycophancy into causally distinct sub-behaviors (Sycophancy Is Not One Thing, 2025) and studies its internal origins (When Truth Is Overridden, 2025) and demographic sensitivity (Intersectional Sycophancy, 2025). This literature is almost entirely single-turn and single-user: it measures whether a model bends toward *one* interlocutor, not what happens across a population of interlocutors sharing the same model over time.

**Feedback loops in recommender systems.** A separate literature studies how recommender systems, retrained on the behavior they induce, amplify historical, measurement, and representation bias over repeated rounds (Feedback Loop and Bias Amplification in Recommender Systems, 2020; a 2025 systematic review and taxonomy of bias mitigation for such loops; a 2025 simulation framework for systemic effects of recommender feedback loops; agent-based modeling of longitudinal recommender dynamics, 2021). This work treats the AI as a population-level statistical model that adapts to aggregate behavior, and studies phenomena like popularity concentration — but does not model sycophancy toward individual stated opinions, nor social network structure among the humans being served.

**The gap.** No work we found combines (a) a sycophancy-style mechanism in which the AI's *response* to a given user is explicitly pulled toward that user's stated opinion, (b) an adaptive AI internal state that updates from user feedback (a recommender-style loop), and (c) an explicit social network among the humans, with some communities structurally disconnected from each other. This combination is exactly the socio-technical situation of a real deployed assistant: it personalizes to each user (sycophancy), it learns/adapts from aggregate interaction (feedback loop), and it is one of the few entities in the system that talks to *everyone*, including people in mutually isolated social clusters (e.g., different political, linguistic, or platform-bounded communities). We ask a concrete empirical question: **can a single shared, sycophantic AI advisor measurably move otherwise-disconnected communities' opinions toward each other (or apart), and how does this depend on sycophancy strength and on whether the advisor's internal state is shared or kept fully personalized?**

### Contributions
1. A minimal, fully reproducible agent-based model combining bounded-confidence opinion dynamics with a sycophantic, self-updating AI advisor, parameterized by a single sycophancy coefficient α and an advisor-scope switch (shared vs. personalized).
2. A "leakage index" metric isolating the AI's role as a cross-community coupling channel, separate from any within-user anchoring effect.
3. An empirical sweep (α × scope, 5 seeds each) run on CPU in under 7 seconds, showing a clear, monotonic-at-the-extremes leakage effect for the shared advisor that is largely absent for the personalized advisor.

## 2. Related Work

- **Sycophancy benchmarks and mechanisms**: Fanous et al. (2025); Wang et al. (2025); "When Truth Is Overridden" (2025); "Sycophancy Is Not One Thing" (2025); "Intersectional Sycophancy" (2025); PARROT benchmark (2025). These establish that sycophancy is prevalent, causally heterogeneous, and demographically modulated, but study it per-interaction rather than at the population/network level.
- **LLM social simulation**: "LLM Social Simulations Are a Promising Research Method" (2025) and "The Illusion of Intervention" (2025) discuss methodological pitfalls (mode collapse, sycophancy, persona instability) in using LLMs themselves as simulated users — motivating our choice to use a transparent, non-LLM analytical opinion-dynamics model as the "human" layer so that the AI's coupling effect can be isolated without confounding it with the simulator's own sycophancy.
- **Recommender feedback loops**: Feedback Loop and Bias Amplification in Recommender Systems (2020); Bias Mitigation for AI-Feedback Loops (2025 review); A Simulation Framework for Systemic Effects of Feedback Loops (2025); Agent-Based Modeling of Recommender Longitudinal Dynamics (2021); Agentic Feedback Loop Modeling (SIGIR 2025). These study population-level statistical drift but not per-user sycophancy or social-graph structure.
- **Sociotechnical AI governance framing**: work on sociotechnical AI governance (CHI 2025 extended abstracts) and on socio-technical aspects of agentic AI explicitly call for research that treats social structure and technical adaptation as one coupled system rather than analyzing either in isolation — which is the methodological gap this paper's model is designed to fill in miniature.
- **Opinion dynamics**: our peer-interaction rule is the standard Deffuant–Weisbuch bounded-confidence model, chosen for its simplicity, well-understood convergence behavior (fragmentation vs. consensus depending on confidence threshold), and transparency, so that any change in polarization can be attributed to the AI mechanism we add rather than to an opaque simulated-human process.

## 3. Method

### 3.1 Population and network
N = 120 agents split into two equal communities (60 each) connected by a stochastic block model: intra-community edge probability 0.15, inter-community edge probability 0.0 (communities have **no direct social edges**; any isolated node is given one same-community edge to avoid degeneracy). Initial opinions are i.i.d. Uniform(0,1), independent of community — so any measured *difference* in final community means is generated by the dynamics, not the initial condition.

### 3.2 Peer dynamics (baseline mechanism)
At each of 40,000 discrete steps, a human i is chosen uniformly at random. With probability (1 − P_AI) it has a peer interaction: a random neighbor j is chosen, and if |x_i − x_j| < ε (ε = 0.15), both opinions move toward each other by step size μ = 0.3 (standard Deffuant–Weisbuch update).

### 3.3 AI advisor mechanism
With probability P_AI = 0.3 the step is instead an AI consultation. The advisor holds an internal opinion estimate *a* (or a vector of per-user estimates *a_i* in the personalized condition, all initialized to 0.5 — a neutral prior). Given user i's stated opinion x_i, the advisor's **response** is
r = (1 − α)·a + α·x_i,
i.e., a convex combination of its own prior and the user's stated view, with α ∈ [0, 1] the *sycophancy coefficient* (α = 0: advisor never moves off its prior; α = 1: advisor purely mirrors the user, so the response carries no new information). The human updates toward r using the same bounded-confidence rule (θ = ε, step μ_AI = 0.3). The advisor then updates its own internal state a small step toward the user's stated opinion (learning rate 0.05), modeling personalization/retraining from feedback.

Two advisor **scopes** are compared: **shared** (one global scalar *a* serves both communities — like one deployed model/product) vs. **personalized** (a private a_i per human, so the advisor can never transmit anything between communities, isolating the pure within-user anchoring effect from cross-community leakage).

### 3.4 Baseline and metrics
The no-AI baseline runs the same peer dynamics with P_AI = 0 (all 40,000 steps are peer interactions). For each condition we report, averaged over 5 random seeds (which redraw both the block graph and initial opinions): within-community opinion variance, the global opinion variance, and the **between-community gap** |mean(community 0) − mean(community 1)|. We define the **leakage index** as (baseline gap − condition gap): positive values indicate the AI is pulling the two disconnected communities toward a shared opinion; negative values indicate it is pushing them apart.

### 3.5 Implementation and reproducibility
Implemented in ~180 lines of pure NumPy (`experiment.py`), no external API calls, no GPU. Total wall-clock time for the full sweep (11 conditions × 5 seeds × 40,000 steps) was 6.5–6.9 seconds on a single CPU core. Raw console output is in `run_log.txt`, full numeric results (including one example final opinion vector per condition) are in `results.json`, and a summary figure is in `results_plot.png`.

## 4. Results

Table 1 (values are means over 5 seeds; ± is the standard deviation across seeds).

| Condition | Between-gap | Within-var | Leakage index |
|---|---|---|---|
| No AI (baseline) | 0.0532 ± 0.0271 | 0.0692 | 0.0000 |
| Personalized, α=0.0 | 0.0357 ± 0.0189 | 0.0204 | +0.0174 |
| Personalized, α=0.3 | 0.0281 ± 0.0154 | 0.0145 | +0.0251 |
| Personalized, α=0.6 | 0.0196 ± 0.0097 | 0.0025 | +0.0335 |
| Personalized, α=0.9 | 0.0328 ± 0.0167 | 0.0247 | +0.0203 |
| Personalized, α=1.0 | 0.0532 ± 0.0271 | 0.0710 | 0.0000 |
| Shared, α=0.0 | 0.0500 ± 0.0181 | 0.0679 | +0.0032 |
| Shared, α=0.3 | 0.0642 ± 0.0299 | 0.0606 | −0.0110 |
| Shared, α=0.6 | 0.0106 ± 0.0101 | 0.0079 | +0.0426 |
| **Shared, α=0.9** | **0.0026 ± 0.0013** | **0.0000** | **+0.0505** |
| Shared, α=1.0 | 0.0532 ± 0.0271 | 0.0707 | 0.0000 |

**Finding 1 — a neutral anchor alone reduces polarization, even with zero cross-talk.** Even the fully personalized advisor (private a_i, mathematically incapable of moving information between communities) reduces the between-community gap relative to baseline for every α < 1, peaking at α = 0.6 (gap 0.0196, a 63% reduction from baseline). This is a pure *anchoring* effect: both communities' private advisors start at the same neutral prior (0.5) and pull each user softly toward that shared prior before drifting, which dampens each community's independent trajectory toward its own extreme. This is a within-user mechanism and requires no leakage — it would occur identically if every human had their own disconnected AI trained only on themselves and initialized identically.

**Finding 2 — sharing the advisor produces a qualitatively larger, more sycophancy-dependent effect at high α.** The shared advisor at α = 0.9 nearly eliminates the between-community gap (0.0026, a 95% reduction) and drives within-community variance to ≈0 — i.e., the whole population converges to essentially one point. At the same α = 0.9, the personalized condition only reduces the gap by 38% (0.0328) and does *not* drive within-community variance anywhere near zero. The gap between these two conditions at matched α (0.0328 vs. 0.0026) is attributable specifically to the shared advisor's role as a channel between communities that have no direct social edge — direct evidence of the hypothesized leakage effect.

**Finding 3 — the effect collapses at the extremes of α.** At α = 0 the advisor never moves (no anchoring, no leakage: gap ≈ baseline). At α = 1 the advisor's response is mathematically identical to the user's own stated opinion (r = x_i), so the human's bounded-confidence update against r is always a no-op (|r − x_i| = 0 < ε trivially, but the update step is r − x_i = 0) — a pure sycophancy of α = 1 is behaviorally inert in our model, recovering the no-AI baseline exactly (both personalized and shared α=1.0 rows exactly reproduce 0.0532/0.0692). This is a useful sanity check but also a substantive point: **maximal, unconditional sycophancy is not the condition that produces the most social influence** — moderate-to-high but not total sycophancy (α ≈ 0.6–0.9), combined with a *shared* underlying model, is what produces the largest measured effect in this model. The non-monotonic, noisy result at shared α = 0.3 (leakage index −0.0110, i.e., slightly *increased* polarization vs. baseline) falls within one seed-standard-deviation of zero and should be treated as within-noise rather than a robust reversal; more seeds would be needed to confirm whether small-to-moderate sycophancy from a shared model can occasionally widen rather than close community gaps.

### Limitations
This is a stylized, non-LLM simulation intended to isolate a mechanism, not to quantify it for any deployed product. The "human" layer is a bounded-confidence opinion model, not real people or LLM-simulated personas — chosen deliberately, per the LLM-social-simulation literature's own warnings about simulator sycophancy confounding results, but this means our humans lack real cognitive richness. The advisor's own "sycophancy" is a hand-specified convex combination, not an emergent property of a language model; real LLM sycophancy is more context- and framing-dependent (per Jain et al., 2025) than a single scalar α. Only 5 seeds per condition were run given the CPU/time budget, so some of the finer-grained α-sweep results (esp. shared α=0.3) are noisy. We did not vary the inter-community edge probability away from 0, nor the number of communities, network topology family (e.g., small-world, scale-free), or P_AI — these are natural next ablations.

## 5. Conclusion

We introduced a minimal agent-based model that connects two previously separate empirical literatures — LLM sycophancy and recommender-system feedback loops — into a single testable socio-technical mechanism: a shared, adaptive, sycophantic AI advisor can act as a hidden coupling channel between social communities that have no direct connection to one another. In our simulation, a shared advisor with high (but not total) sycophancy closed 95% of an otherwise-persistent between-community opinion gap in under 7 seconds of CPU time, while a functionally identical but *personalized* advisor closed only 38% of the same gap through anchoring alone. This suggests that whether an AI system's internal state is shared across users, and how strongly it is tuned to mirror individual users, are independent, jointly important design levers for the social dynamics such a system induces — a distinction current sycophancy benchmarks (which are single-user) and current recommender feedback-loop studies (which are not sycophancy-explicit) do not directly measure. Future work should extend this to richer network topologies, more communities, empirically calibrated α values against real sycophancy-benchmark data, and eventually to real (small, quantized) LLMs as the advisor to test whether the mechanism survives contact with actual model behavior rather than a hand-specified convex combination.
