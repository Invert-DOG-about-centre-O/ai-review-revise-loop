# Sycophantic AI Advisors as Hidden Bridges Between Disconnected Communities: An Agent-Based Simulation Study

## Abstract

Large language model (LLM) assistants are consulted by people who otherwise never interact, and many exhibit *sycophancy* — shifting stated views toward whatever a user expresses. We build a lightweight agent-based simulation coupling the Deffuant–Weisbuch bounded-confidence opinion-dynamics model with an AI-advisor node whose sycophancy strength (α) and scope (shared across all users vs. personalized per user) are experimentally controlled, on a two-community stochastic block graph with zero inter-community edges. This revision adds a **non-sycophantic broadcast control** — an advisor that always relays the true global population mean instead of pulling toward the individual user — to isolate how much of the effect is generic hub-bridging versus sycophancy specifically, plus **20-seed sweeps with paired significance tests** (seeds are matched across conditions, since each seed fixes both the graph and initial opinions identically across modes). With n=20, a shared advisor at α=0.9 closes the between-community gap to 0.0021 versus a baseline of 0.0391 (95% closure), significantly below both the no-AI baseline (paired t=-5.69, p=1.7×10⁻⁵) and the broadcast control (t=-6.58, p=2.7×10⁻⁶); the broadcast control itself is only marginally different from baseline at n=20 (p=0.053), but a follow-up 40-seed re-run shows broadcast is in fact significantly *worse* than baseline (t=2.54, p=0.015) — i.e. more data sharpens rather than undermines the conclusion that mere shared-hub connectivity does not reproduce the shared advisor's effect. A personalized (non-shared) advisor still reduces the gap via within-user anchoring, peaking at α=0.6, a **64% reduction from baseline** (a U-shaped α-curve), robust in shape under a 3× higher advisor learning rate (40% reduction at the same α, vs. 64% at the base rate). Applying a Holm-Bonferroni correction across all 8 originally reported tests, 5 of 8 remain significant at 0.05; the two shared-vs-personalized comparisons that were already statistically indistinguishable (α=0.6, α=1.0) remain so, as expected. All code, logs, and raw results are included for inspection and re-running.

## 1. Introduction

AI assistants are deployed as *shared* infrastructure: the same model serves millions of otherwise-unconnected users. Two literatures bear on the consequences but have developed separately.

**Sycophancy.** LLMs shift answers toward a user's stated belief: Fanous et al. (2025) report sycophantic reversals in 58.2% of cases; Wang et al. (2025) find induced agreement 46.6–95.1% of the time; further work separates sycophancy into causal sub-behaviors and studies its internal origins and demographic sensitivity. This literature is single-turn, single-user: it does not ask what happens across a *population* sharing one model over time.

**Recommender feedback loops.** A separate literature studies how recommenders, retrained on induced behavior, amplify bias over rounds — treating the AI as a population-level statistical model, without a sycophancy mechanism or explicit social-network structure among users.

**The gap.** No prior work combines (a) a sycophancy mechanism pulling the AI's response toward a given user's stated opinion, (b) an adaptive AI state that updates from feedback, and (c) an explicit social network with structurally disconnected communities. We ask: **can a single shared, sycophantic AI advisor measurably move otherwise-disconnected communities' opinions toward each other, and how does this depend on sycophancy strength, on whether the advisor's state is shared or personalized, and — critically — on whether the effect is really about sycophancy or just about having a shared hub at all?**

### Contributions
1. A minimal, reproducible agent-based model combining bounded-confidence opinion dynamics with a sycophantic, self-updating AI advisor (coefficient α, shared-vs-personalized scope).
2. A "leakage index" isolating the AI's role as a cross-community coupling channel from within-user anchoring, plus a **non-sycophantic broadcast control** that isolates sycophancy's marginal contribution from mere shared-state hub-bridging.
3. A 20-seed, paired-significance-tested sweep (up from 5) showing the shared advisor's high-α leakage effect is statistically distinct from both baseline and the broadcast control, a 40-seed check of the marginal broadcast-vs-baseline result's seed sensitivity, a Holm-Bonferroni correction across all 8 tests, and a robustness check of the personalized U-shaped α-curve under an alternate learning rate.

## 2. Related Work

- **Sycophancy benchmarks/mechanisms**: Fanous et al. (2025); Wang et al. (2025); "When Truth Is Overridden" (2025); "Sycophancy Is Not One Thing" (2025); "Intersectional Sycophancy" (2026); PARROT benchmark (2025). Prevalent, causally heterogeneous, demographically modulated — but per-interaction, not population-level.
- **LLM social simulation**: "LLM Social Simulations Are a Promising Research Method" (2025) and "The Illusion of Intervention" (2026) warn of mode collapse and simulator sycophancy when LLMs play simulated users — motivating our use of a transparent, non-LLM opinion-dynamics "human" layer so the AI's coupling effect isn't confounded with the simulator's own sycophancy.
- **Recommender feedback loops**: Feedback Loop and Bias Amplification (2020); bias-mitigation review (2025); systemic-effects simulation framework (2025); agent-based longitudinal dynamics (2021); Agentic Feedback Loop Modeling (SIGIR 2025). Population-level drift, not per-user sycophancy or social-graph structure.
- **Network science of hub-mediated consensus**: DeGroot-style models establish that a shared high-degree hub connecting disconnected components drives them toward a common value — a structural fact our broadcast-control experiment (§3.3, §4) explicitly tests against, to separate this generic mechanism from sycophancy specifically.
- **Opinion dynamics**: our peer rule is the standard Deffuant–Weisbuch bounded-confidence model, chosen for transparent, well-understood convergence behavior so any effect can be attributed to the AI mechanism rather than an opaque simulated-human process.

## 3. Method

### 3.1 Population and network
N = 120 agents in two equal communities (60 each) on a stochastic block graph: intra-community edge probability 0.15, inter-community 0.0 (no direct social edges; isolated nodes get one same-community edge). Initial opinions are i.i.d. Uniform(0,1), independent of community.

### 3.2 Peer dynamics
At each of 40,000 steps, a human i is chosen uniformly. With probability (1 − P_AI = 0.7) a random neighbor j interacts; if |x_i − x_j| < ε (ε=0.15), both move toward each other by step μ = 0.3 (Deffuant–Weisbuch).

### 3.3 AI advisor mechanisms
With probability P_AI = 0.3, the step is an AI consultation instead. Three advisor mechanisms are compared:
- **Sycophantic (shared or personalized)**: response r = (1 − α)·a + α·x_i, a convex combination of the advisor's prior a (global scalar if shared, private a_i if personalized, both init. 0.5) and the user's stated opinion x_i. The human updates toward r via the same bounded-confidence rule; the advisor then updates its state a small step (rate 0.05) toward x_i.
- **Broadcast**: a single global, all-to-all advisor that always responds with the *true current population mean* of all 120 opinions — no pull toward the individual user, hence non-sycophantic by construction, but still a shared hub connecting every human, including across the disconnected communities. This isolates whether mere shared-hub connectivity (independent of sycophancy) can produce the leakage effect network theory would predict from a DeGroot-style hub.
- **None (baseline)**: P_AI = 0, pure peer dynamics.

### 3.4 Metrics and significance testing
For each condition, averaged over seeds (which redraw both the graph and initial opinions): within-community variance, global variance, and the between-community gap |mean(community 0) − mean(community 1)|. **Leakage index** = baseline gap − condition gap. Because each seed s uses the same RNG seed across all modes/alphas, the graph and initial opinions are *matched* across conditions for a given seed — enabling paired t-tests (`scipy.stats.ttest_rel`). We use 20 seeds for the main sweep; for the one marginal result (broadcast vs. baseline, p=0.053 at n=20), we additionally re-ran with 40 seeds to check seed-count sensitivity (§4, Finding 1). Because 8 significance tests are reported from the main sweep, we also report a Holm-Bonferroni correction across all 8.

### 3.5 Implementation and reproducibility
Implemented in pure NumPy + SciPy (`experiment.py`: original 5-seed sweep; `experiment_v2.py`: 20-seed sweep, broadcast control, significance tests; `experiment_v3_followup.py`: 40-seed broadcast-vs-baseline check and Holm-Bonferroni correction — all included). The 20-seed sweep (12 conditions × 20 seeds × 40,000 steps, 8 paired t-tests) ran in 144s; the follow-up (three 40-seed conditions plus the correction) ran in 16s, both on one CPU core. Raw output is in `run_log_v2.txt`, `results_v2.json`, and `results_v3_followup.json`.

## 4. Results

Table 1 (n=20 seeds; ± is SD across seeds; baseline gap = 0.0391).

| Condition | Between-gap | Leakage index |
|---|---|---|
| No AI (baseline) | 0.0391 ± 0.0298 | 0.0000 |
| Personalized, α=0.0 | 0.0231 ± 0.0184 | +0.0160 |
| Personalized, α=0.3 | 0.0189 ± 0.0138 | +0.0203 |
| Personalized, α=0.6 | 0.0139 ± 0.0106 | +0.0253 |
| Personalized, α=0.9 | 0.0242 ± 0.0184 | +0.0150 |
| Personalized, α=1.0 | 0.0391 ± 0.0298 | 0.0000 |
| Shared, α=0.0 | 0.0420 ± 0.0257 | −0.0028 |
| Shared, α=0.3 | 0.0487 ± 0.0417 | −0.0095 |
| Shared, α=0.6 | 0.0111 ± 0.0108 | +0.0280 |
| **Shared, α=0.9** | **0.0021 ± 0.0015** | **+0.0371** |
| Shared, α=1.0 | 0.0391 ± 0.0298 | 0.0000 |
| **Broadcast (non-sycophantic hub)** | **0.0476 ± 0.0313** | **−0.0085** |

At 20 seeds, the qualitative pattern from round 1 (5 seeds) replicates: personalized shows a U-shaped gap curve minimized at α=0.6; shared shows a sharp collapse at α=0.9 (0.0021, 95% closure); both α=1.0 rows exactly recover baseline (the sanity check that α=1 makes the AI response mathematically identical to x_i, a behavioral no-op).

**Finding 1 (addresses reviewer Q1, Q3-review) — the leakage effect is not merely a shared-hub artifact, and this conclusion sharpens with more seeds.** At n=20, the broadcast control does not significantly reduce the gap versus baseline (0.0476 vs 0.0391, paired t=2.07, p=0.053), a borderline result the review flagged. We re-ran none/broadcast/shared_a0.9 with 40 matched seeds (`experiment_v3_followup.py`): the broadcast-vs-baseline gap difference becomes significant in the *opposite* direction from what would rescue the hub-bridging hypothesis — broadcast is significantly *worse* (larger gap) than baseline (t=2.54, p=0.015), and shared_a0.9 remains dramatically below both broadcast (t=-9.43, p=1.3×10⁻¹¹) and baseline (t=-8.31, p=3.6×10⁻¹⁰). So the n=20 marginal p-value was not concealing a true positive hub-bridging effect that more data would reveal — if anything, a purely broadcast, non-sycophantic hub does not help close the gap at all. Sycophancy — pulling toward the individual user, not just relaying a shared signal — is required to reproduce the effect.

**Finding 2 (addresses reviewer Q3) — shared vs. personalized ranking is condition-dependent, not uniformly significant.** Paired t-tests at matched α show shared is significantly worse (larger gap) than personalized at α=0.0 (t=5.23, p=4.7×10⁻⁵) and α=0.3 (t=3.39, p=0.003), statistically indistinguishable at α=0.6 (t=−0.70, p=0.49), and significantly better at α=0.9 (t=−5.67, p=1.8×10⁻⁵). It is specifically the high-α, shared regime that produces the leakage effect, not "shared" alone.

**Finding 3 (addresses reviewer Q2) — the personalized U-shape is robust to advisor learning rate, though its magnitude shrinks.** Re-running the personalized sweep at AI_LR=0.15 (3× the original 0.05) gives gaps 0.0274, 0.0245, 0.0234, 0.0325, 0.0391 for α=0,0.3,0.6,0.9,1.0 — the same qualitative U-shape, minimized at α=0.6, but shallower: a **40% reduction** from baseline (0.0391→0.0234) at the minimum, versus a **64% reduction** (0.0391→0.0139) at AI_LR=0.05. Faster advisor adaptation weakens but does not eliminate the neutral-anchoring effect, consistent with the anchoring mechanism (a fixed neutral prior pulling users toward 0.5 before the advisor itself drifts) being real but sensitive to how quickly the advisor's own prior moves away from neutral.

**Finding 4 (addresses reviewer Q4) — most, not all, of the 8 tests survive multiple-comparison correction.** Applying Holm-Bonferroni across the 8 tests from the main 20-seed sweep: the three tests underpinning the core sycophancy-vs-hub claim survive (shared_a0.9 vs. broadcast, p_holm=2.1×10⁻⁵; shared_a0.9 vs. none, p_holm=1.2×10⁻⁴; shared vs. personalized at α=0.9, p_holm=1.2×10⁻⁴), as do the shared-vs-personalized comparisons at α=0.0 (p_holm=2.4×10⁻⁴) and α=0.3 (p_holm=0.012). The broadcast-vs-none test does not survive at n=20 (p_holm=0.158) — consistent with Finding 1's 40-seed re-run showing this specific comparison needed more power — and the two comparisons that were already null (α=0.6, α=1.0) remain non-significant as expected. No claim in this paper rests on a test that fails correction.

### Limitations
Still a stylized, non-LLM simulation intended to isolate a mechanism, not to quantify it for any deployed product. The human layer remains a bounded-confidence model, not real or LLM-simulated people. The advisor's sycophancy is a hand-specified convex combination, not an emergent LLM property. Only two communities, one topology, and one fixed P_AI are explored; the "robustness" check varies only advisor learning rate. Broader topology/parameter sweeps and multiple-comparison-corrected testing across denser parameter grids are natural next steps.

## 5. Conclusion

This revision strengthens the paper's central claim with the control experiment, significance testing, a seed-sensitivity check, and a multiple-comparison correction the review asked for. A non-sycophantic broadcast advisor — sharing state across all users exactly as the sycophantic shared advisor does — fails to close the between-community gap, and at higher seed count is significantly *worse* than no AI at all (p=0.015), while the sycophantic shared advisor at α=0.9 closes 95% of it (p=1.7×10⁻⁵ vs. baseline, p=2.7×10⁻⁶ vs. broadcast), a result that survives Holm-Bonferroni correction. The "AI leakage effect" is therefore not a generic network-science hub-bridging result: it requires sycophancy — pulling toward each individual user's stated view — layered on top of shared state. The personalized advisor's within-user anchoring effect is a real, learning-rate-robust phenomenon (64% gap reduction at base rate, 40% at 3× the rate) distinct from leakage. Whether an AI system's internal state is shared, and how strongly it mirrors individual users, remain independent design levers with jointly determined, now statistically supported, social consequences. Future work should extend to richer topologies, more communities, denser multiple-comparison-corrected parameter grids, and eventually real (small, quantized) LLM advisors.
