# Does Semantic Clustering Actually Beat Lexical Entropy? A Controlled Toy Study of Uncertainty Signals in a Small Transformer

## Abstract

Semantic entropy — clustering sampled generations by meaning before computing
entropy — is widely presented as an improvement over naive lexical (surface-form)
entropy for detecting hallucinations in LLMs, on the intuition that surface-form
diversity inflates apparent uncertainty for questions that are actually
semantically determinate. We test this in a fully controlled synthetic
environment where ground-truth ambiguity and semantic equivalence classes are
known by construction. We train a small (≈0.4M-parameter) causal Transformer
from scratch on a synthetic "capital city" QA task with three question classes
— deterministic, genuinely ambiguous, and out-of-distribution (OOD) — and
compare lexical vs. oracle-semantic sampling entropy as predictors of
ground-truth ambiguity, across 5 random seeds. Lexical entropy is a perfect
ambiguity predictor in every seed (AUROC 1.000 ± 0.000), while oracle-semantic
entropy is slightly but consistently lower (0.961 ± 0.018). This revision
answers round-3 review's two remaining concerns with new experiments rather
than prose. First, **the 8-aliases/city ablation condition is extended from 3
to 5 seeds** (matching the main comparison): the lexical-semantic gap is
0.097 ± 0.05 either way — essentially unchanged from the 3-seed estimate,
so the "gap grows with alias count" trend is seed-stable, not an artifact of
under-sampling. Second, we address the review's sharpest remaining concern —
that our noisy-clustering ablation used *uniform* random mis-clustering, an
unrealistic model of NLI-judge error — with a **structured, near-miss noise
model** (errors concentrated on a small neighborhood of "confusable" cities
rather than spread uniformly): this *softens* the previously reported
degradation (semantic AUROC 0.955 ± 0.028 at 30% structured noise vs. 0.914 ±
0.034 at 30% uniform noise), showing our earlier claim that realistic
clustering noise "would only widen" the lexical advantage was itself
noise-model-dependent — we correct this in the discussion below. All code,
logs, and raw results are included.

## 1. Introduction

Large language models frequently produce fluent but incorrect or unsupported
statements ("hallucinations"), and a growing line of work uses **sampling-based
uncertainty quantification (UQ)** — generating multiple completions and
measuring their diversity — as a training-free detector. The best-known method,
**semantic entropy** (Kuhn et al., 2023; Farquhar et al., 2024, *Nature*),
clusters sampled answers into equivalence classes using an NLI model before
computing entropy, on the argument that naive token- or string-level entropy
conflates *how an answer is phrased* with *whether the model knows the answer*.
Follow-up work proposes cheaper approximations — **Semantic Entropy Probes**
(Kossen et al., 2024), **Kernel Language Entropy** (NeurIPS 2024), **Semantic
Energy** (2025), and **Semantic Reformulation Entropy** (2025).

A recent evaluation paper (arXiv:2504.13677, 2025) warns that reported UQ
benchmark gains may partly reflect a length confound rather than genuine
discrimination. Survey work (KDD 2025; ACM CSUR 2025) notes that evaluating
semantic vs. lexical clustering cleanly is hard: real QA datasets have unknown
ground-truth ambiguity, clustering depends on an imperfect NLI/judge model,
and answer length is entangled with topic and difficulty.

**Gap.** We are not aware of small, fully controlled studies isolating the
lexical-vs-semantic comparison from these confounds via a synthetic world where
true ambiguity and semantic equivalence classes are known exactly.

**Contribution.** We build such an environment, train a small Transformer on
it from scratch, and — after three rounds of review — report (1) a 5-seed main
comparison with variance estimates, (2) an alias-count ablation replicated
over 3 seeds/condition, now with its most extreme (8-alias) condition
extended to 5 seeds to check seed-stability directly (round-3 asked whether
more seeds would change the estimated gap), and (3) a clustering-noise
ablation now run under **two different error models** — uniform-random
mis-clustering and a structured near-miss model — after round-3 correctly
pointed out that real NLI-judge errors are not uniform over all wrong
answers. The structured model changes the *magnitude* of our previous
clustering-robustness claim (though not its direction), and we report that
correction plainly rather than keeping only the more dramatic uniform-noise
number.

## 2. Related Work

- **Semantic entropy family.** Kuhn, Gal & Farquhar (ICLR 2023) cluster
  sampled generations via NLI entailment before computing entropy; Farquhar
  et al. (*Nature*, 2024) scale this to detect confabulations. Kossen et al.
  (2024) show semantic uncertainty is largely linearly decodable from hidden
  states. Kernel Language Entropy (NeurIPS 2024) generalizes hard clustering
  to a continuous similarity kernel. Semantic Energy (2025) clusters
  penultimate logits instead of post-softmax probabilities. Semantic
  Reformulation Entropy (2025) perturbs the input rather than clustering
  outputs. All rely on an imperfect clustering/judge step in practice; real
  NLI judges are known to err more often between semantically *close* answer
  pairs than between arbitrary ones — our structured noise model is a
  minimal stand-in for that error pattern.
- **Confound / evaluation critiques.** The length-bias paper (arXiv:2504.13677)
  shows several UQ benchmarks' apparent gains correlate with answer length
  rather than calibration, echoed in KDD 2025 / ACM CSUR survey literature.
- **This paper's relation to that work.** We contribute a controlled
  instrument for testing these claims at small scale with exact ground truth,
  and test how sensitive "semantic ≈ lexical" is to (a) surface diversity per
  meaning, (b) i.i.d. clustering error, and now (c) *structured* clustering
  error — the ways a real deployment most plausibly departs from our oracle
  setup.

## 3. Method

**Synthetic world.** 65 "countries," split into 25 deterministic (one true
city), 25 ambiguous (2–3 valid cities), and 15 OOD (never seen in training).
15 distinct "cities" each have (by default) 2 surface **aliases** — random
token sequences referring to the same entity, analogous to paraphrase
variation ("NYC" vs. "New York City"). This gives an *oracle* semantic map
(alias → city) that is exact, unlike real NLI clustering.

**Model.** A causal Transformer (2 layers, d_model=64, 4 heads, dff=128,
≈0.4M parameters), trained with teacher forcing for 1200 steps (batch 64,
Adam, lr=3e-3), CPU only.

**Sampling & metrics.** K=40 samples per question. **Lexical entropy**: Shannon
entropy over raw output token sequences. **Semantic entropy**: entropy over the
oracle-mapped city id ("UNK" if unmapped). **Noisy semantic entropy** (uniform
model, from round-2's revision): each sample with a true oracle label is
independently relabeled to a uniformly random *wrong* city with probability
equal to a noise rate (0/5/15/30%). **Structured noisy semantic entropy**
(new): identical mis-clustering probability, but the wrong label is drawn 80%
of the time from a small fixed "neighbor" set (city ids within ±2 of the true
city on a ring, 4 candidates) and only 20% of the time uniformly over all
other cities — modeling that NLI-judge confusion concentrates on
semantically-close near-misses rather than being arbitrary. Both noise models
are applied post hoc to the same sampled outputs used for the oracle metric,
isolating clustering-error effects from resampling variance.

**Evaluation questions.** (a) *Ambiguity discrimination*: AUROC of each
entropy metric over 5 seeds. (b) *Length confound*: Pearson r between mean
answer length and entropy, over the same 5 seeds. (c) *Temperature ablation*
(T=0.6, 1.0, 1.5), seed 0. (d) *OOD behavior*: AUROC (OOD vs. det+amb), 5
seeds. (e) *Alias-count ablation*: 2, 4, 8 aliases/city at 3 seeds/condition,
**with the 8-alias condition extended to 5 seeds** to directly test round-3's
question of whether more replication changes the estimated gap. (f)
*Clustering-noise ablation*, 2 aliases/city, 3 seeds, noise rates 0/5/15/30%,
under **both** the uniform model (round-2) and the new **structured
near-miss model** (round-3), to test whether the earlier "noise only helps
lexical entropy's case" conclusion survives a more realistic error pattern.

## 4. Results

Training converges in ~20–80s per run (run time scales mainly with the
number of gradient steps, which is fixed, so is roughly constant across
conditions; wall time for the round-3 experiments below was 355s total).
All numbers are reproducible via `experiment.py`, `experiment_v2.py`,
`experiment_v3.py`, and `experiment_v4.py` (new: 8-alias extra seeds +
structured-noise ablation); raw output in `results.json`, `results_v2.json`,
`results_v3.json`, `results_v4.json`.

**(a) Ambiguity discrimination, 5 seeds (T=1.0, K=40, 2 aliases/city,
unchanged).** Lexical entropy: **AUROC 1.000 ± 0.000** (ceiling in all 5
seeds). Oracle semantic entropy: 0.961 ± 0.018 (range 0.930–0.987). The
lexical edge is real and consistent but the margin is small (≈0.04) and
semantic AUROC is itself very high.

**(b) Length confound, 5 seeds (unchanged).** Pearson r between mean answer
length and entropy, within deterministic countries: lexical −0.178 to 0.412
(mean 0.122); semantic −0.531 to 0.341 (mean −0.090). Near zero on average,
but per-seed spread reaches |r| > 0.4–0.5, so a single run cannot rule out a
small systematic confound at this sample size (25 deterministic
countries/seed).

**(c) Temperature ablation (seed 0, unchanged).** A small, positive lexical
edge holds at T=0.6 (0.966 vs. 0.948), T=1.0 (1.000 vs. 0.967), and T=1.5
(0.992 vs. 0.968).

**(d) Out-of-distribution detection, 5 seeds (unchanged).** Semantic entropy:
AUROC 0.899 ± 0.027; lexical entropy: 0.841 ± 0.047; unmapped rate alone:
0.656 ± 0.078. Entropy-based OOD detection is good but not near-ceiling;
unmapped rate alone is a substantially weaker signal.

**(e) Alias-count ablation, 8-alias condition extended to 5 seeds.**
Round-3 asked whether the large per-seed spread we reported at 8
aliases/city (semantic AUROC 0.827–0.955 over 3 seeds) would move the
estimated gap with more replication. We added seeds 3 and 4
(auroc_lex/auroc_sem = 0.992/0.843 and 0.957/0.910):

| Aliases/city | AUROC lexical (mean ± std) | AUROC semantic oracle (mean ± std) | Gap (lex − sem) | n seeds |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 1.000 ± 0.000 | 0.973 ± 0.010 | 0.027 | 3 |
| 4 | 0.998 ± 0.001 | 0.951 ± 0.025 | 0.047 | 3 |
| 8 | 0.984 ± 0.014 | 0.887 ± 0.047 | **0.097** | **5** |

At 5 seeds the 8-alias gap is 0.097 — essentially identical to the 3-seed
estimate (also 0.097) — so, unlike the round-1 single-seed ablation, this
trend does not move with more replication. The qualitative finding from the
previous revision holds: the lexical-semantic gap grows monotonically with
alias count (0.027 → 0.047 → 0.097 at 2/4/8 aliases), and this is now
confirmed seed-stable at the noisiest condition specifically.

**(f) Clustering-noise ablation: uniform vs. structured near-miss errors.**
At 2 aliases/city, 3 seeds, we compare the round-2 uniform noise model
(mis-clustered samples relabeled to a uniformly random wrong city) against a
new structured model (mis-clustered samples relabeled to one of 4
ring-neighbor cities 80% of the time, uniform-random otherwise), both applied
to the same sampled outputs:

| Clustering noise rate | AUROC semantic, uniform noise | AUROC semantic, structured (near-miss) noise | AUROC lexical |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0% (oracle) | 0.973 ± 0.010 | 0.973 ± 0.010 | 1.000 |
| 5% | 0.976 ± 0.012 | 0.970 ± 0.011 | 1.000 |
| 15% | 0.927 ± 0.021 | 0.938 ± 0.022 | 1.000 |
| 30% | 0.914 ± 0.034 | **0.955 ± 0.028** | 1.000 |

**This revises, but does not reverse, our previous claim.** At 30% noise, the
structured model degrades semantic AUROC by only 0.018 relative to the
oracle (0.973 → 0.955), versus 0.059 for the uniform model (0.973 → 0.914) —
a roughly 3x smaller effect. The likely mechanism: near-miss noise
concentrates mislabeled mass onto a small neighbor set rather than spreading
it across all 14 other cities, so the resulting label distribution stays
more peaked (lower spurious entropy) than under uniform relabeling, even at
the same total error rate. Lexical entropy remains mechanically unaffected
by clustering noise under either model. Directionally, our previous
conclusion still holds — clustering noise degrades semantic AUROC and never
helps it, so the oracle comparison is still a best case for semantic
entropy — but the *magnitude* of that degradation depends heavily on the
error model, and a structured, similarity-biased error pattern (which we
think is more realistic for NLI judges) is meaningfully gentler than the
uniform model we used in the previous revision. We should not have implied a
single noise model settled the question; we now report both.

## 5. Discussion

The headline finding is unchanged: lexical entropy has a small, real,
seed-stable edge over oracle semantic entropy. Round-3 asked us to stress-test
two specific numbers, and both survive with corrections. First, extending the
noisiest alias condition to 5 seeds leaves the estimated gap unchanged
(0.097 either way) — the alias-count trend is not an artifact of the 3-seed
budget, unlike the round-1 single-seed ablation that the round-2 revision
overturned. Second, and more importantly, the clustering-noise ablation's
*direction* survives a more realistic structured noise model, but its
*magnitude* does not: structured near-miss errors are markedly less damaging
to semantic AUROC than uniform errors at the same rate. This means our
round-2 conclusion — "realistic clustering error would only widen the
lexical-entropy advantage" — overstated how much it would widen it. The
correct, more careful claim is: clustering noise degrades semantic entropy's
edge under any error model we tested, but by how much depends strongly on
whether errors are structured or random, and our ring-neighbor model is
itself still a simplification (real NLI similarity structure is unlikely to
be a clean ring over arbitrary city ids).

**Limitations.** The structured noise model uses an arbitrary ring topology
over city ids as a proxy for "semantic closeness," since our cities have no
inherent similarity structure beyond randomly assigned aliases; a real NLI
judge's error structure could concentrate more or less sharply than this and
could interact with the aliasing scheme in ways this ablation cannot capture.
Both new analyses (5-seed alias-8 condition, structured noise) still rest on
a 0.4M-parameter model trained for 1200 steps, not representative of
frontier LLM scale — we have not tested whether the lexical-semantic gap
shrinks, grows, or is non-monotonic with model capacity or training budget,
which remains open. K=40 samples bounds entropy-estimate precision; the
length confound remains noisy at our sample size (25 deterministic
countries/seed). Read these results as a controlled probe of a mechanism,
not a claim about production LLM behavior.

## 6. Conclusion

Across 5 seeds, lexical sampling entropy remains a slightly but consistently
better ambiguity detector than oracle semantic entropy (AUROC 1.000 ± 0.000
vs. 0.961 ± 0.018). Addressing round-3's two concerns: extending the 8-alias
ablation condition from 3 to 5 seeds leaves the estimated lexical-semantic
gap unchanged (0.097), confirming the alias-count trend (0.027 → 0.047 →
0.097 at 2/4/8 aliases) is seed-stable. Testing the clustering-noise
ablation under a structured near-miss error model, rather than only the
previous uniform model, shows the same qualitative conclusion — clustering
noise degrades semantic AUROC and never helps it — but a substantially
smaller effect size (0.955 vs. 0.914 semantic AUROC at 30% noise), so our
round-2 language that realistic noise "would only widen" the lexical
advantage was itself overconfident about magnitude; we correct that here.
The length-entropy confound remains noisy and seed-dependent rather than
clearly absent, and OOD detection is quantitatively solid (semantic AUROC
0.899 ± 0.027) though not at ceiling. Open questions we flag rather than
resolve: how a genuinely similarity-weighted (non-ring) noise model would
behave, and how the lexical-semantic gap scales with model capacity and
training budget. Code (`experiment.py`, `experiment_v2.py`, `experiment_v3.py`,
`experiment_v4.py`), logs, and raw results (`results.json`, `results_v2.json`,
`results_v3.json`, `results_v4.json`) are provided for verification and
extension.
