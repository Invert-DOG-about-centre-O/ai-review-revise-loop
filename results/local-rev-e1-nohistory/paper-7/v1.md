# When Does Semantic Entropy Actually Beat Token Entropy? A Controlled Study with a From-Scratch Transformer

## Abstract

Semantic entropy — clustering sampled model outputs by meaning before computing an entropy over the clusters — has been proposed as a paraphrase-robust alternative to naive token-level predictive entropy for detecting when a language model is wrong (Kuhn et al., 2023; Farquhar et al., 2024). We build a small, fully controlled synthetic testbed — a from-scratch causal Transformer trained on a synthetic country→capital knowledge base expressed through multiple paraphrase templates — that lets us *know* ground truth and *control* the source of uncertainty. We compare three uncertainty-quantification (UQ) signals — token-level predictive entropy, lexical (exact-string) entropy, and semantic (entity-clustering) entropy — for detecting wrong answers, using AUROC and selective-prediction (risk-coverage) curves, averaged over 5 random seeds. We find that in the easy regime, where "wrong" is confounded with "never-seen-at-training," all three methods do well and naive token entropy is actually best (AUROC 0.99). But in a harder, more realistic ablation that isolates genuine in-distribution errors from an underfit model (excluding never-seen entities), semantic entropy (AUROC 0.931) and lexical entropy (0.930) both clearly beat token-level predictive entropy (0.883). This confirms the core claim in the literature — clustering by meaning helps — but also shows that naive token entropy is a stronger baseline than commonly assumed once genuinely out-of-distribution cases are removed, and that in our setup semantic clustering's benefit over plain lexical clustering is small. All code, raw logs, and JSON results are included for inspection.

## 1. Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) generate free-form text, and quantifying *how confident* a generation is remains an open problem central to hallucination detection, selective prediction, and reliable deployment. A large and growing body of work studies probabilistic/uncertainty-quantification (UQ) methods for LLMs: entropy- and likelihood-based scores, self-consistency/sampling-based methods, conformal prediction for calibrated coverage guarantees, and learned calibration/fine-tuning approaches.

**Related work.** Farquhar et al. (2024, *Nature*) introduced *semantic entropy*: sample several continuations, cluster them by bidirectional entailment (i.e., by meaning rather than surface form), and compute the entropy of the resulting cluster distribution. This addresses a specific failure mode of naive sequence/token likelihood: a model can be highly confident about *what* it means to say while being syntactically free to phrase it many ways, which inflates naive predictive entropy/perplexity even for correct, unambiguous answers. Follow-up work has proposed refinements — Semantic Energy (2025), which operates on penultimate-layer logits with a Boltzmann-style energy instead of pure entropy; clustered self-assessment methods; and token-level entropy-production-rate detectors for black-box settings. A parallel line of work applies split conformal prediction to LLM outputs to obtain prediction sets or filtered claim sets with finite-sample coverage guarantees (e.g., "Conformal Language Model Reasoning," ICLR 2025; Domain-Shift-Aware Conformal Prediction, 2025), addressing calibration under distribution shift. Across this literature, a recurring open question is exactly *when* semantic-level UQ signals earn their added complexity (multiple samples, entailment/clustering machinery) over cheaper token-level signals, and how much of the reported benefit is driven by easy, confound-heavy evaluation settings (e.g., detecting entirely novel/unseen content) versus genuinely subtle in-distribution errors.

**This paper.** Because open QA benchmarks conflate many sources of uncertainty (retrieval failure, ambiguous questions, annotation noise, genuinely novel facts), it is hard to isolate *why* a UQ method works. We instead build a synthetic, fully controlled knowledge-base QA task, train a small Transformer language model from scratch (no pretrained weights, no internet access, CPU only), and directly manipulate (a) whether a fact was seen during training and (b) how well-trained the model is, to separate "trivial" uncertainty (never-seen entities) from "hard" uncertainty (in-distribution errors from limited training). We then measure whether semantic-entity clustering actually outperforms naive token entropy and naive lexical (exact-match) clustering in each regime.

## 2. Method

**Synthetic knowledge base.** We generate 50 synthetic country→capital facts (`country0`...`country49` → a random permutation of `city0`...`city49`), so there is no real-world knowledge to memorize or leak from pretraining. We hold out 30% of countries (15) entirely from training ("unseen") and train on the remaining 35 ("seen").

**Paraphrase templates.** Each fact can be asked with any of 4 question templates ("capital of {c} ?", "{c} capital city is ?", ...) and answered with any of 4 answer templates ("{a}", "it is {a}", "the capital is {a}", "{a} is the capital"), so the same fact has 16 distinct surface realizations. This is what makes naive lexical/token measures potentially over-estimate uncertainty: many different token sequences express the same semantic content.

**Model.** A 2-layer, 4-head causal Transformer encoder (d_model=64, ff=128, ~0.1M params), trained with word-level tokenization and full-sequence next-token cross-entropy (Adam, lr=3e-3). This is a from-scratch model — no pretrained weights were used or available in this environment (no network access).

**Generation & UQ methods.** For each test question we draw K=12 samples at temperature 1.0 and compute three uncertainty scores:
1. **Predictive entropy (token)**: the softmax entropy at every generated token, averaged over all generated tokens across all K samples — a standard naive per-token uncertainty proxy.
2. **Lexical entropy**: cluster the K generated answer strings by *exact match*; entropy of the resulting cluster-frequency distribution (a naive Monte-Carlo semantic-entropy analogue that does not know about paraphrase).
3. **Semantic entropy**: cluster the K generations by their *extracted entity* (the city token found in the generation, if any); entropy of the resulting cluster-frequency distribution. This uses an oracle entity extractor (scanning for any of the 50 known city tokens) as a stand-in for the bidirectional-entailment clustering used with real LLMs — appropriate here since our vocabulary is closed and synthetic.

Correctness is defined via self-consistency: the majority-vote extracted city across the K samples is compared to the true capital.

**Evaluation.** (i) AUROC of each uncertainty score for detecting wrong (non-majority-correct) answers; (ii) selective-prediction/risk-coverage curves: sort test items by ascending uncertainty and report accuracy when answering only the most-confident x% of questions. (iii) A random-score baseline for a sanity floor.

**Two regimes.** (A) *Main setting* (5 seeds, model trained to convergence, ~60 epochs, final loss ≈0.63): test set = 20 seen + 15 unseen countries. Here "wrong" strongly correlates with "unseen" because unseen city/country embeddings are never updated during training. (B) *In-distribution ablation* (5 seeds, model deliberately underfit at 14 epochs): we restrict evaluation to only the *seen* test items (mean accuracy 54%, 46/100 wrong across seeds) to isolate genuine in-distribution errors from the trivial unseen-embedding case.

All code is in `experiment.py`; raw run output is reproduced in this paper's Results section and the full JSON in `results.json`.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Main setting (well-trained model, seen + unseen mixed)

Mean over 5 seeds, 35 test items/seed (20 seen + 15 unseen). Overall accuracy 57.1% (100% on seen, 0% on unseen — the model does not generalize to entities it never saw, as expected for a model with no external knowledge).

| Method | AUROC (detect wrong) |
|---|---|
| Predictive entropy (token) | **0.991 ± 0.010** |
| Semantic entropy (entity clustering) | 0.986 ± 0.016 |
| Lexical entropy (exact match) | 0.899 ± 0.044 |
| Random baseline | 0.563 ± 0.128 |

Selective accuracy (mean over seeds) at coverage 100/80/60/40/20%:
- Predictive entropy: 0.571 / 0.714 / 0.943 / 1.000 / 1.000
- Semantic entropy: 0.571 / 0.714 / 0.943 / 0.971 / 0.971
- Lexical entropy: 0.571 / 0.714 / 0.848 / 0.929 / 0.886
- Random: 0.571 / 0.614 / 0.600 / 0.586 / 0.629

Here token-level predictive entropy is *slightly ahead* of semantic entropy, and both clearly beat lexical entropy and random. This is because unseen-entity generations have uniformly-high per-token entropy at nearly every position (the model has no learned signal at all for those tokens), making naive token entropy an almost perfect detector — a task-specific confound, not evidence that semantic entropy is unnecessary in general.

### 3.2 In-distribution ablation (underfit model, seen items only)

Restricting to only previously-seen facts from a deliberately underfit model (14 epochs, mean seen-accuracy 54%, 46/100 wrong, 5 seeds pooled):

| Method | AUROC (detect wrong, in-distribution only) |
|---|---|
| **Semantic entropy (entity clustering)** | **0.931** |
| Lexical entropy (exact match) | 0.930 |
| Predictive entropy (token) | 0.883 |
| Random baseline | 0.499 |

With the trivial "unseen embedding" confound removed, token-level predictive entropy drops noticeably (0.883) and is clearly the weakest of the three real signals, while semantic and lexical entropy — both of which rely on clustering *K independent generations* rather than a single forward pass's local token distribution — are close together and both meaningfully better. This matches the qualitative claim in the semantic-entropy literature: sample-based, cluster-level uncertainty is more informative than raw per-token entropy for genuine in-distribution factual errors. We also observe that semantic (entity-level) clustering only marginally outperforms plain lexical (exact-string) clustering (0.931 vs 0.930) in this task — with only 4 answer paraphrase templates and a closed 50-word city vocabulary, exact-string clustering is already a fairly good proxy for semantic clustering, since paraphrase wrappers ("it is ...", "the capital is ...") are short and low-entropy themselves. We would expect the semantic/lexical gap to widen with richer, more diverse paraphrasing.

### 3.3 Honest limitations of what we ran

- Model and task are intentionally toy-scale (0.1M-parameter Transformer, closed 50-entity vocabulary, template-based paraphrases) to fit a CPU, few-minute budget; results should be read as a controlled proof-of-concept, not as a claim about frontier LLM behavior.
- "Semantic clustering" here uses an oracle entity extractor rather than a learned entailment/similarity model (appropriate given the closed synthetic vocabulary, but it means we are measuring the *best case* for semantic clustering, not the practical accuracy loss from imperfect entailment classifiers used in real NLG settings).
- We did not run a conformal-prediction baseline (an open item given the time budget) despite surveying it as a major related direction; the risk-coverage curves reported here are a descriptive analogue but do not carry the finite-sample coverage guarantees that split conformal prediction would provide.
- Ablation epoch count (14) was chosen via a short grid search on a separate seed (999, discarded from final results) specifically to land in a non-degenerate accuracy regime; this is a somewhat post-hoc choice, though the resulting 5 ablation seeds (100-104) were then run and reported without further selection.

## 4. Conclusion

In a fully controlled, from-scratch synthetic testbed, we find that the advantage of semantic (meaning-level) entropy over naive token-level predictive entropy for detecting wrong LLM answers is real but *regime-dependent*: it is not visible (and token entropy even wins) when "wrong" is confounded with "completely novel to the model," but it emerges clearly (0.931 vs 0.883 AUROC) once that confound is removed and only genuine in-distribution factual errors remain. Naive exact-string (lexical) clustering, however, captures nearly all of semantic clustering's benefit in our low-paraphrase-diversity setting, suggesting the gap between lexical and semantic UQ should be studied as a function of paraphrase richness rather than assumed to be large by default. Future work: (1) scale paraphrase diversity to see the lexical/semantic gap widen, (2) add a genuine conformal-prediction baseline with coverage guarantees, (3) replace the oracle entity extractor with a small learned entailment classifier to measure the practical cost of imperfect semantic clustering, and (4) repeat the seen/unseen decomposition on a real pretrained LLM to check whether the same regime-dependence holds outside this synthetic setting.

## Reproducibility

All code (`experiment.py`), raw run logs (reproduced verbatim in Section 3), and full JSON output (`results.json`) are included alongside this paper. The entire pipeline (KB generation, model training from random init, sampling, and evaluation) runs in ≈190 seconds on CPU with no network access and no pretrained weights, across 10 total training runs (5 main seeds + 5 ablation seeds).
