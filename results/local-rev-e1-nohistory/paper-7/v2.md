# When Does Semantic Entropy Actually Beat Token Entropy? A Controlled Study with a From-Scratch Transformer

## Abstract

Semantic entropy — clustering sampled model outputs by meaning before computing an entropy over the clusters — has been proposed as a paraphrase-robust alternative to naive token-level predictive entropy for detecting when a language model is wrong (Kuhn et al., 2023; Farquhar et al., 2024). We build a small, fully controlled synthetic testbed — a from-scratch causal Transformer trained on a synthetic country→capital knowledge base expressed through multiple paraphrase templates — that lets us *know* ground truth and *control* the source of uncertainty. We compare three uncertainty-quantification (UQ) signals — token-level predictive entropy, lexical (exact-string) entropy, and semantic (entity-clustering) entropy — for detecting wrong answers, using AUROC and selective-prediction curves averaged over 5 seeds. In the easy regime, where "wrong" is confounded with "never-seen-at-training," all three do well and token entropy is best (AUROC 0.99). In a harder ablation isolating genuine in-distribution errors from an underfit model, semantic entropy (0.931) and lexical entropy (0.930) beat token entropy (0.883). Following reviewer feedback, we then stress-tested this single-point ablation with three additional checks: (1) a single-threaded determinism probe, (2) a 5-point epoch-sensitivity sweep (10/12/14/16/18 underfit epochs), and (3) a noisy-entity-extractor probe simulating an imperfect entailment classifier. The sweep shows semantic entropy's AUROC advantage over token entropy is fairly robust once accuracy is not near-floor, but the specific "semantic ≈ lexical, both beat token" ordering reported at 14 epochs is *not* stable — at 16 epochs token entropy actually beats lexical entropy, and at the most severely underfit settings (10–12 epochs, >95% wrong) all orderings become unreliable due to extreme class imbalance. The noisy-extractor probe was itself too high-variance (small seed budget, imbalanced classes) to yield a clean monotonic degradation curve, which is itself informative about how fragile point-estimate AUROC comparisons are in this regime. We revise our claim accordingly: the *qualitative* advantage of sample-based clustering (semantic or lexical) over raw per-token entropy for genuine in-distribution errors is real, but headline point estimates from a single ablation setting should not be over-interpreted. All code, raw logs, and JSON results (including the new robustness checks) are included for inspection.

## 1. Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) generate free-form text, and quantifying *how confident* a generation is remains central to hallucination detection, selective prediction, and reliable deployment. A growing body of work studies UQ methods for LLMs: entropy- and likelihood-based scores, self-consistency/sampling-based methods, conformal prediction, and learned calibration.

**Related work.** Farquhar et al. (2024, *Nature*) introduced *semantic entropy*: sample several continuations, cluster them by bidirectional entailment (by meaning rather than surface form), and compute the entropy of the resulting cluster distribution. This targets a specific failure of naive sequence/token likelihood: a model can be confident about *what* it means to say while free to phrase it many ways, inflating naive predictive entropy even for correct answers. Follow-up work includes Semantic Energy (2025, penultimate-layer Boltzmann-style energy), clustered self-assessment methods, token-level entropy-production-rate detectors, and split conformal prediction for LLM outputs (e.g., "Conformal Language Model Reasoning," ICLR 2025; Domain-Shift-Aware Conformal Prediction, 2025). A recurring open question is *when* semantic-level UQ earns its added complexity over cheaper token-level signals, and how much of the reported benefit comes from easy, confound-heavy evaluations versus genuinely subtle in-distribution errors.

**This paper.** Because open QA benchmarks conflate many sources of uncertainty, we instead build a synthetic, fully controlled knowledge-base QA task, train a small Transformer from scratch (no pretrained weights, no internet, CPU only), and directly manipulate (a) whether a fact was seen during training and (b) how well-trained the model is. This separates "trivial" uncertainty (never-seen entities) from "hard" uncertainty (in-distribution errors from limited training). A first version of this study reported a single ablation point (14 underfit epochs) where semantic and lexical entropy beat token entropy; a round-1 review correctly flagged that (i) re-running the identical seeded code gave different point estimates (bit-level non-determinism), (ii) the 14-epoch choice was itself selected post-hoc to land near a target accuracy, and (iii) the "oracle" entity extractor used for semantic clustering sidesteps the noise a real entailment classifier would add. This revision adds targeted experiments addressing all three points.

## 2. Method

**Synthetic knowledge base.** 50 synthetic country→capital facts (`country0`...`country49` → a random permutation of `city0`...`city49`); 30% of countries (15) held out entirely from training ("unseen"), 35 used for training ("seen").

**Paraphrase templates.** Each fact can be asked with 4 question templates and answered with 4 answer templates, giving 16 surface realizations per fact — the source of potential over-estimated uncertainty for naive lexical/token measures.

**Model.** A 2-layer, 4-head causal Transformer encoder (d_model=64, ff=128, ~0.1M params), word-level tokenization, full-sequence next-token cross-entropy (Adam, lr=3e-3). From-scratch, no pretrained weights, no network access.

**Generation & UQ methods.** For each test question we draw K=12 samples at temperature 1.0 and compute: (1) **token predictive entropy** — softmax entropy at every generated token, averaged over all generated tokens across all K samples; (2) **lexical entropy** — Monte-Carlo entropy over clusters keyed by exact-string match of the K generations; (3) **semantic entropy** — Monte-Carlo entropy over clusters keyed by the *extracted entity* (the city token found in each generation, via an oracle scan over the 50 known city tokens, standing in for bidirectional-entailment clustering since our vocabulary is closed). Correctness is defined via self-consistency: the majority-vote extracted city across K samples vs. the true capital.

**Evaluation.** AUROC of each score for detecting wrong answers; selective-prediction (risk-coverage) curves; a random-score sanity floor.

**Two regimes.** (A) *Main setting* (5 seeds, model trained to convergence, ~60 epochs): test set = 20 seen + 15 unseen countries; "wrong" strongly correlates with "unseen." (B) *In-distribution ablation* (5 seeds, model underfit at 14 epochs): evaluation restricted to *seen* test items only, isolating genuine in-distribution errors.

**New: robustness checks (this revision).** (i) *Determinism*: re-run the identical seed (100, 14 epochs) twice with `torch.set_num_threads(1)` to test whether run-to-run variance is thread/BLAS-related, as the reviewer hypothesized. (ii) *Epoch-sensitivity sweep*: repeat the in-distribution ablation at underfit-epoch counts {10, 12, 14, 16, 18} (3 fresh seeds each, seeds 300–302, distinct from the main ablation's 100–104) to see whether the 14-epoch ordering (semantic ≈ lexical > token) is a general property of "underfit" or an artifact of that specific point. (iii) *Noisy-entity-extractor probe*: a modified sampling pipeline that, after oracle entity extraction, corrupts each sample's extracted entity to a uniformly random wrong city with probability p ∈ {0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.5} before computing semantic entropy (simulating an imperfect entailment/similarity classifier), holding correctness labels fixed (computed from the *uncorrupted* majority vote, since noise models an imperfect UQ signal, not an imperfect answer). Run over 3 seeds (400–402) at 14 epochs.

All code is in `experiment.py` (main experiments) and `followup_experiments.py` (new robustness checks); raw output is reproduced below and full JSON is in `results.json` / `followup_results.json`.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Main setting (well-trained model, seen + unseen mixed)

Mean over 5 seeds, 35 test items/seed. Overall accuracy 57.1% (100% seen, 0% unseen).

| Method | AUROC (detect wrong) |
|---|---|
| Predictive entropy (token) | **0.991 ± 0.010** |
| Semantic entropy | 0.986 ± 0.016 |
| Lexical entropy | 0.899 ± 0.044 |
| Random baseline | 0.563 ± 0.128 |

Token entropy is slightly ahead here because unseen-entity generations have uniformly high per-token entropy at nearly every position — a task-specific confound, not evidence semantic entropy is unnecessary in general.

### 3.2 In-distribution ablation (underfit model, seen items only, 14 epochs)

| Method | AUROC (detect wrong, in-distribution only) |
|---|---|
| **Semantic entropy** | **0.931** |
| Lexical entropy | 0.930 |
| Predictive entropy (token) | 0.883 |
| Random baseline | 0.499 |

With the "unseen embedding" confound removed, token entropy drops and is the weakest of the three signals at this single epoch count.

### 3.3 Robustness checks (new)

**Determinism.** Re-running seed 100 at 14 epochs twice with `torch.set_num_threads(1)` gave *bit-identical* seen-accuracy (0.0 both trials) — this particular seed happened to land in a fully-degenerate regime (all seen items wrong, so AUROC is undefined for it), but the exact reproduction across trials is consistent with the reviewer's hypothesis that the previously observed rerun-to-rerun drift is multithreaded-BLAS non-determinism rather than a seeding bug. We did not have budget to re-verify this across all 5 ablation seeds, so we report it as supporting evidence, not proof.

**Epoch-sensitivity sweep** (3 seeds each, 60 seen items/epoch-count):

| Epochs | Acc (seen) | Wrong/N | Token AUROC | Lexical AUROC | Semantic AUROC |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 | 0.02 | 59/60 | 0.966 | 0.288 | 0.110 |
| 12 | 0.03 | 58/60 | 0.897 | 0.483 | 0.328 |
| 14 | 0.17 | 50/60 | 0.588 | 0.697 | 0.772 |
| 16 | 0.42 | 35/60 | 0.877 | 0.825 | 0.941 |
| 18 | 0.77 | 14/60 | 0.784 | 0.856 | 0.915 |

This changes our conclusion in an important way. At the most severe underfitting (10–12 epochs, ≥97% wrong), the ordering *inverts* — token entropy scores highest and semantic entropy lowest — but with only 1–2 correct examples out of 60, these AUROCs are estimated from extreme class imbalance and should be treated as noise, not a real reversal. In the more balanced 14–18 epoch range, semantic entropy is consistently the best of the three (0.772–0.941), so the semantic-over-token advantage is fairly robust once accuracy is not near-floor. However, the specific "semantic ≈ lexical, both clearly beat token" pattern we highlighted at 14 epochs does *not* generalize: at 16 epochs token entropy (0.877) actually beats lexical entropy (0.825). The lexical-vs-token comparison is therefore not stable across the underfitting range, even though semantic-vs-token is.

**Noisy-entity-extractor probe.** Using fresh seeds (400–402, also landing in a highly imbalanced 56/60-wrong regime), even the noise-free (p=0) condition gave semantic AUROC 0.629 — well below the 0.931/0.952 reference values from the main ablation seeds — and the sweep across p was non-monotonic (0.629 → 0.705 → 0.471 → 0.748 → 0.585 for p = 0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.5) rather than a clean degradation curve. With only 3 seeds and 4–8 correct examples per condition, this experiment is underpowered; we cannot responsibly claim a clean answer to "how much does a noisy entailment classifier erode the semantic/lexical margin." What it *does* show, concretely, is that AUROC point estimates in this small-sample in-distribution setting swing substantially with seed choice alone (0.629 vs. 0.931 for nominally the same noise-free oracle extractor), reinforcing the reviewer's concern that single-point AUROC comparisons here are fragile. We report this as an honest null result rather than force a trend.

### 3.4 Remaining limitations

- Toy-scale model/task (0.1M params, closed 50-entity vocabulary) by design; results are a controlled proof-of-concept, not a claim about frontier LLM behavior.
- Semantic clustering uses an oracle entity extractor, not a learned entailment model; §3.3's noisy-extractor probe is a first attempt to bound the practical cost of this, but was underpowered.
- No conformal-prediction baseline is run; the risk-coverage curves are descriptive and do not carry finite-sample coverage guarantees.
- The epoch-sensitivity sweep (§3.3) used only 3 seeds per epoch count (vs. 5 for the main ablation) and different seed ranges, to fit the compute budget; it should be read as a robustness check, not a fully powered replication.

## 4. Conclusion

Our controlled synthetic testbed shows the advantage of semantic (meaning-level) entropy over naive token-level predictive entropy is real but regime-dependent, and — after this revision's stress tests — more fragile at the level of specific point estimates than we first suggested. It is not visible (and token entropy even wins) when "wrong" is confounded with "completely novel to the model." Once that confound is removed, semantic entropy's edge over token entropy is fairly robust across a range of underfitting levels (§3.3), but a claim as specific as "semantic ≈ lexical, both clearly beat token by margin X" should be treated as tied to one operating point, not a stable law — we observed it hold at 14 epochs and *not* hold at 16, where token entropy overtook lexical entropy. A single-threaded determinism check is consistent with prior non-determinism being a BLAS/threading artifact rather than a seeding bug, though we could not fully re-verify this across all seeds within budget. Our attempt to probe robustness to a noisy (imperfect) entity extractor was itself too high-variance to yield a trend, which we take as a data point about the fragility of small-sample AUROC comparisons in this setting rather than a finding about semantic clustering per se. Future work: (1) scale paraphrase diversity to test whether the lexical/semantic gap widens, (2) run the noisy-extractor and epoch sweeps with enough seeds (and balanced classes) to get well-powered curves, (3) add a genuine conformal-prediction baseline, and (4) repeat the seen/unseen decomposition on a real pretrained LLM.

## Reproducibility

All code (`experiment.py`, `followup_experiments.py`), raw run logs, and full JSON output (`results.json`, `followup_results.json`) are included. The main pipeline runs in ≈190s on CPU (10 training runs: 5 main + 5 ablation seeds). The new robustness checks (§3.3: 2 determinism trials + 15 epoch-sweep runs + 15 noisy-extractor runs, all at ≤18 epochs) add roughly another 250s on CPU, single-threaded for the determinism trials.
