# Algorithmic Sycophancy and Opinion Polarization: A Simulation Study of Agreement-Seeking Recommenders

## Abstract

Large language model (LLM) "sycophancy" — a model's tendency to preferentially agree with a user's stated views — is well studied within a single conversation. We ask a socio-technical question one level up: what happens to a *population's* opinions when a recommendation algorithm exhibits the same agreement-seeking bias, choosing what to show each user based on how well it matches that user's current stance? We build a lightweight, fully synthetic bounded-confidence opinion-dynamics simulation (300 agents, 150 rounds, 20 seeds) in which a tunable "sycophancy strength" *s* interpolates between a calibrated recommender (shows content sampled from the true population distribution) and a fully sycophantic one (always shows each agent the population's opinion nearest to its own). Contrary to the intuitive hypothesis that agreement-seeking curation should *increase* polarization, we find the opposite in our model: increasing *s* reduces final-round opinion variance (0.182 → 0.062) because full sycophancy is a self-referential near-fixed-point that freezes the population near its initial distribution. A full battery of Bonferroni-corrected pairwise Welch tests shows this effect is statistically reliable only at the high end (s ≥ 0.75); intermediate strengths are not distinguishable from calibrated once multiple comparisons are corrected for. We also test — per reviewer request — whether "freezing" is a near-tautological artifact of nearest-neighbor search in a dense, self-referential content pool: across pool sizes from 5 to 300 agents, and with a fixed external corpus fully decoupled from agent opinions, sycophantic(1.0) variance remains 3–10× lower than calibrated in every setting, indicating the mechanism is structural rather than a density artifact. A bridging recommender that deliberately shows opposing content increases variance while lowering bimodality. We discuss why "freezing, not polarizing" complicates the informal analogy between chatbot sycophancy and feed-ranking harms, and what the toy model still omits.

## 1. Introduction

Socio-technical research on AI asks how algorithmic behavior interacts with human social dynamics, not just how a model performs in isolation. Two literatures bear on this. First, work on *sycophancy* in conversational LLMs documents models shifting positions, softening disagreement, or flattering users under social pressure, degrading factual reliability and, per recent HCI work, perceived authenticity and trust (When Flattery Backfires, CHI 2026). Multi-turn benchmarks such as SYCON-Bench show sycophancy compounding under pushback (e.g., an LLM's sycophancy rate roughly doubling, 18% vs. 9%), and taxonomy work argues sycophancy is a "fragmented construct" spanning opinion-shifting, excessive validation, and face-preservation (Cheng et al.'s Social Sycophancy Scale; "What Counts as AI Sycophancy?", 2025-2026).

Second, a recommender-systems literature studies how algorithmic curation shapes collective opinion — echo chambers, filter bubbles, polarization — often via bounded-confidence (Hegselmann-Krause) opinion-dynamics simulation. Findings here are mixed: some report limited short-term polarization from filter-bubble personalization, others find algorithmic amplification of confirmation bias and homogenization traps.

**Gap.** These literatures share a mechanism — an algorithm choosing what a user sees based on agreement with their current state — but are rarely connected explicitly, and the recommender literature typically parameterizes curation via engagement/similarity scores rather than a construct borrowed from the sycophancy literature. We treat "sycophancy strength" as a first-class, sweepable simulation parameter, alongside a bridging baseline and two robustness checks: a bounded-confidence-tolerance ablation and a pool-structure ablation (sparsity and decoupling) that directly tests whether the effect is a nearest-neighbor artifact.

**Contribution.** (1) A minimal, reproducible bounded-confidence simulation operationalizing "algorithmic sycophancy" as a tunable content-selection bias. (2) A sweep showing higher sycophancy strength yields *lower* final polarization, validated with Bonferroni-corrected pairwise tests across all conditions and metrics. (3) A pool-structure ablation (size 5–300, plus a fixed external corpus) showing the freezing effect is not merely an artifact of a dense, self-referential pool. (4) A bridging-vs-sycophancy contrast and a tolerance ablation clarifying mechanism and sensitivity. (5) Explicit discussion of what the toy model omits.

## 2. Method

**Population and opinions.** N = 300 agents each hold a scalar opinion $x_i \in [-1, 1]$, initialized i.i.d. $x_i \sim \text{clip}(\mathcal{N}(0, 0.25), -1, 1)$, so any polarization observed is induced by the dynamics, not present at t = 0.

**Recommenders.** At each of T = 150 rounds, a *content pool* is the current vector of agents' opinions (agents are simultaneously content sources). Each agent receives one recommendation $r_i$ from one of three distinct strategies (we drop the "random" row from v1 since it is code-identical to "calibrated" — both sample uniformly from the pool — and report calibrated only):
- **calibrated:** $r_i$ drawn uniformly from the pool — non-personalized, accuracy-faithful baseline.
- **sycophantic(s):** with probability $s$, $r_i$ = the pool item nearest to the agent's own current opinion; otherwise calibrated sampling. Swept $s \in \{0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0\}$.
- **bridging(b=0.5):** with probability $b$, $r_i$ = the pool item nearest to $-x_i$ (opposing content); otherwise calibrated.

**Update rule.** Hegselmann-Krause-style bounded confidence: if $|r_i - x_i| \le \varepsilon$ (default $\varepsilon=0.35$), $x_i \leftarrow x_i + \alpha(r_i - x_i)$, $\alpha=0.3$; otherwise a small backfire repulsion $x_i \leftarrow x_i - \gamma\,\text{sign}(r_i-x_i)|r_i-x_i|$, $\gamma=0.05$. Opinions clipped to $[-1,1]$.

**Metrics.** Opinion variance, Sarle's bimodality coefficient (BC; >5/9 conventionally suggestive of bimodality), and mean absolute opinion ("extremity"), all at round 150, averaged over 20 seeds (main sweep) or 10 seeds (ablations).

**Statistical testing (revised).** Rather than a single Welch's t-test, we run pairwise Welch tests between calibrated and every other condition (bridging, sycophantic at all 5 strengths) across all three metrics (18 tests) and apply a Bonferroni correction ($\alpha = 0.05/18 = 0.0028$).

**Pool-structure ablation (new).** To test whether "freezing" is simply a near-tautological consequence of nearest-neighbor search in a dense, self-referential pool (raised by review), we rerun calibrated and sycophantic(1.0) with (a) the content pool subsampled each round to $K \in \{5, 20, 50, 150, 300\}$ agents without replacement, and (b) a *fixed external corpus* of 300 values drawn once from the initial distribution and held constant thereafter, fully decoupled from the agents' evolving opinions (10 seeds each).

**Tolerance ablation.** Calibrated vs. sycophantic(1.0) across $\varepsilon \in \{0.15, 0.25, 0.35, 0.5, 0.7\}$ (10 seeds each).

**Compute.** Pure NumPy/SciPy, CPU only. Main sweep 80.7s; tolerance ablation 16.8s; pairwise-test + pool-structure experiments 121.4s. All code and raw results are included (`simulation.py`, `extra_experiments.py`, `ablation_eps.py`, `plot_results.py`, `results.json`, `extra_experiments_results.json`, `ablation_eps_results.json`, logs, `results_plot.png`).

## 3. Results

**Main sweep (Table 1, N=20 seeds, mean ± SD, round 150):**

| Condition | Variance | Bimodality coeff. | Mean \|opinion\| |
|---|---|---|---|
| calibrated | 0.182 ± 0.077 | 0.562 ± 0.272 | 0.421 ± 0.213 |
| bridging (b=0.5) | 0.319 ± 0.028 | 0.319 ± 0.027 | 0.330 ± 0.028 |
| sycophantic (s=0.0) | 0.181 ± 0.073 | 0.618 ± 0.266 | 0.452 ± 0.220 |
| sycophantic (s=0.25) | 0.169 ± 0.051 | 0.531 ± 0.287 | 0.371 ± 0.166 |
| sycophantic (s=0.5) | 0.145 ± 0.021 | 0.333 ± 0.166 | 0.244 ± 0.060 |
| sycophantic (s=0.75) | 0.139 ± 0.025 | 0.259 ± 0.139 | 0.199 ± 0.050 |
| sycophantic (s=1.0) | 0.062 ± 0.004 | 0.358 ± 0.034 | 0.200 ± 0.006 |

Mechanistically, full sycophancy (s=1) recommends each agent the pool item closest to its own opinion — by construction $|r_i-x_i|\approx 0$ every round — so updates are tiny and the population stays close to its initial (mildly dispersed, unimodal) state. Partial sycophancy mixes this freezing with calibrated exposure, producing intermediate variance/extremity.

**Bonferroni-corrected significance (18 tests, α=0.0028; full table in `extra_experiments_results.json`).** Only the high end of the sweep survives correction: sycophantic(1.0) is significant on variance (t=-6.95, p=1.2e-6) and extremity (p=1.7e-4) but not bimodality (p=3.5e-3, just above threshold); sycophantic(0.75) is significant on extremity and bimodality (p=1.7e-4, 1.3e-4) but not variance (p=0.026, uncorrected-only); sycophantic(0.5) is significant only on extremity (p=1.7e-3); s=0.0 and s=0.25 are not distinguishable from calibrated on any metric. Bridging is significant on variance and bimodality but not extremity. **Revised claim:** the sample-mean trend across s is monotonic in variance and extremity, but only s ≥ 0.75 is a statistically reliable departure from calibrated once we correct for 18 hypotheses — intermediate strengths (0.25, 0.5) produce visually suggestive but not reliably distinguishable shifts given 20-seed noise, a more conservative reading than our single-test original draft.

**Pool-structure ablation (addressing "is freezing just nearest-neighbor density?").** 10 seeds; variance at round 150:

| Pool | calibrated var | sycophantic(1.0) var | ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| K=5 (sparse, self-ref.) | 0.342 ± 0.239 | 0.036 ± 0.050 | 9.5× |
| K=20 | 0.313 ± 0.173 | 0.035 ± 0.008 | 8.9× |
| K=50 | 0.252 ± 0.121 | 0.049 ± 0.005 | 5.1× |
| K=150 | 0.174 ± 0.069 | 0.059 ± 0.004 | 3.0× |
| K=300 (full, self-ref.) | 0.186 ± 0.066 | 0.063 ± 0.004 | 3.0× |
| Fixed external corpus | 0.242 ± 0.070 | 0.062 ± 0.004 | 3.9× |

The reviewer is right that at K=300 the pool is dense enough that "nearest neighbor" is nearly a no-op, which partially explains the magnitude of freezing. But the effect does *not* disappear as the pool sparsifies: at K=5, where the nearest neighbor in a random 5-item subsample is often far from an agent's own opinion, sycophantic(1.0) variance is still ~9.5× lower than calibrated, and lower in absolute terms than at K=300. The fixed, fully decoupled external corpus (never updated from agent opinions — no self-reference at all) shows the same qualitative freezing (0.062 vs. 0.242). We conclude the freezing effect is not merely a density artifact: even against a static, exogenous corpus, always routing an agent to its closest-available match systematically suppresses net opinion movement, since rejected (out-of-tolerance) moves become rare once matches are close by construction. We do concede the *magnitude* is inflated by pool density at K≥150, and revise our framing accordingly: freezing is structural to closest-match selection, but density modulates its strength.

**Bridging vs. sycophancy.** Bridging increases variance (agents pushed toward the opposite pole and partially comply) while decreasing bimodality relative to calibrated — more spread-out but less two-camp-clustered, consistent with a diversity-forcing rather than consensus- or polarization-inducing intervention.

**Why bimodality is non-monotonic while variance/extremity are monotonic (reviewer question 3).** Variance and extremity both measure *spread from center* and respond directly to how far agents move each round — both shrink smoothly as freezing dominates. Bimodality coefficient measures *distributional shape* (skew and kurtosis), which is far more sensitive to small, sometimes idiosyncratic clustering near the frozen initial distribution than to overall spread: a population can be nearly frozen (low variance) yet retain two small residual bumps from the initial Gaussian's finite-sample asymmetry (moderate BC), which is exactly what we see at s=1.0 (var=0.062 but BC=0.358, comparable to mid-sweep values). The two families of metrics track different aspects of the same frozen configuration and need not covary monotonically — a point we did not make clearly enough in the original submission.

**Tolerance ablation** (calibrated vs. sycophantic(1.0), 10 seeds): calibrated variance ranges from 0.997 (ε=0.15, near-total polarization, classic Hegselmann-Krause) down to 0.004 (ε=0.7, consensus), while sycophantic(1.0) variance is exactly invariant at 0.061 across all five tolerances — recommended content is always within ~0 distance of the agent's own opinion regardless of tolerance width, confirming the freezing mechanism is structural rather than an artifact of the ε=0.35 used in the main sweep.

## 4. Discussion and Limitations

The headline result — a purely agreement-seeking recommender *reduces* measured polarization, while a narrow-tolerance calibrated recommender can produce near-total polarization on its own — runs counter to the informal claim that "sycophantic algorithms polarize society." The effect is about *movement*: full sycophancy suppresses opinion change (a degenerate, low-diversity-of-experience fixed point), arguably still a socio-technical harm (stagnation, reduced belief updating, entrenchment) even though it is not "polarization" by variance/bimodality. We now show this is statistically reliable only at high sycophancy strengths under correction for multiple comparisons, and that it is not a nearest-neighbor density artifact — it persists under pool sparsification and full decoupling from agent opinions, though density modulates its magnitude. This distinction between *polarizing* and *freezing* remains, we think, the paper's main empirical contribution: a metric tracking only variance/bimodality will call a sycophantic system "safe" when it may be suppressing productive opinion change.

**Limitations.** This is a deliberately minimal, synthetic model, not a claim about real platforms. Opinions are one-dimensional; real belief systems are multidimensional and issue-coupled. We do not model network topology, engagement-driven feedback loops, adversarial actors, or heterogeneous susceptibility. The bounded-confidence update and backfire term are stylized. "Sycophancy strength" is a hand-set probability, not derived from an actual LLM-based recommender or real engagement data; connecting it to a measured property of a deployed system (e.g., via the sycophancy scales cited above) is important future work, as is extending the pool-structure ablation to a dynamic (slowly drifting) external corpus rather than a static one.

## 5. Conclusion

We connected the LLM-sycophancy literature to the recommender-polarization literature by treating "agreement-seeking curation strength" as an explicit, sweepable parameter in a bounded-confidence opinion-dynamics simulation. Across a 20-seed sweep with full Bonferroni-corrected significance testing, a tolerance ablation, and a new pool-structure ablation (sparsity K=5–300 plus a fixed external corpus), we find sycophancy strength ≥0.75 reliably reduces final opinion variance and extremity relative to calibrated, because full sycophancy is a near-fixed-point of the dynamics rather than a polarizing force — structural to closest-match content selection, not an artifact of pool density, though density modulates its size. A narrow-tolerance calibrated recommender can independently produce near-total polarization, and a bridging recommender increases spread while reducing bimodality. We argue "freezing vs. polarizing" is a useful, testable distinction for socio-technical evaluation of agreement-seeking AI systems, and release all code and raw results for reuse.
