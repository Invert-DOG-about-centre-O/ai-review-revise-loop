# Algorithmic Sycophancy and Opinion Polarization: A Simulation Study of Agreement-Seeking Recommenders

## Abstract

LLM "sycophancy" — a model's tendency to preferentially agree with a user's stated views — is well studied within a single conversation. We ask a socio-technical question one level up: what happens to a *population's* opinions when a recommendation algorithm exhibits the same agreement-seeking bias, choosing what to show each user based on how well it matches that user's current stance? We build a lightweight, fully synthetic bounded-confidence opinion-dynamics simulation (300 agents, 150 rounds, 20 seeds) with a tunable "sycophancy strength" *s* interpolating between a calibrated recommender (samples the true population distribution) and a fully sycophantic one (always shows the population's opinion nearest to the agent's own). Contrary to the intuition that agreement-seeking curation should *increase* polarization, we find the opposite: increasing *s* reduces final-round opinion variance (0.182 → 0.062), because full sycophancy is a self-referential near-fixed-point that freezes the population near its initial distribution. Bonferroni-corrected pairwise Welch tests show this is reliable only at s ≥ 0.75. We test whether freezing is a nearest-neighbor density artifact (pool sizes 5–300, a fixed external corpus, and — new in this revision — a *drifting* external corpus that is decoupled from agent opinions but not static): freezing persists in every setting, 3–11× lower variance than calibrated. We further test — new in this revision — whether freezing is an artifact of magnitude-scaled updates, by rerunning the sweep under a sign-based, fixed-step update rule whose movement size does not depend on |recommendation − opinion|: sycophantic(1.0) still freezes (var 0.063 vs. 0.482 calibrated, t=-7.90, p=2.4e-5), indicating the effect is a property of closest-match content selection itself, not of any one update-magnitude formula. A bridging recommender that shows opposing content increases variance while lowering bimodality. We discuss why "freezing, not polarizing" complicates the informal analogy between chatbot sycophancy and feed-ranking harms, why realistic sycophancy rates reported in the LLM literature likely fall in the statistically invisible region of our sweep, and what the toy model still omits.

## 1. Introduction

Socio-technical research on AI asks how algorithmic behavior interacts with human social dynamics, not just how a model performs in isolation. Two literatures bear on this. First, work on *sycophancy* in conversational LLMs documents models shifting positions, softening disagreement, or flattering users under social pressure, degrading factual reliability and, per recent HCI work, perceived authenticity and trust (When Flattery Backfires, CHI 2026). Multi-turn benchmarks such as SYCON-Bench show sycophancy compounding under pushback (an LLM's sycophancy rate roughly doubling, ~9% → ~18%), and taxonomy work argues sycophancy is a "fragmented construct" (Cheng et al.'s Social Sycophancy Scale; "What Counts as AI Sycophancy?").

Second, a recommender-systems literature studies how algorithmic curation shapes collective opinion — echo chambers, filter bubbles, polarization — often via bounded-confidence (Hegselmann-Krause) simulation, with mixed findings: some report limited short-term polarization from personalization, others find amplified confirmation bias and homogenization.

**Gap.** These literatures share a mechanism — an algorithm choosing what a user sees based on agreement with their current state — but are rarely connected explicitly. We treat "sycophancy strength" as a first-class, sweepable simulation parameter, alongside a bridging baseline and robustness checks that directly probe whether the resulting effect is a modeling artifact.

**Contribution.** (1) A minimal, reproducible bounded-confidence simulation operationalizing "algorithmic sycophancy." (2) A sweep showing higher sycophancy strength yields *lower* final polarization, with Bonferroni-corrected pairwise tests. (3) A pool-structure ablation (size 5–300, static and now drifting external corpus) showing freezing is not a density or staleness artifact. (4) A new alternate-update-rule ablation showing freezing is not an artifact of magnitude-scaled updates. (5) A discussion of where realistic LLM sycophancy rates would fall on our sweep.

## 2. Method

**Population and opinions.** N = 300 agents each hold a scalar opinion $x_i \in [-1, 1]$, initialized i.i.d. $x_i \sim \text{clip}(\mathcal{N}(0, 0.25), -1, 1)$, so any polarization observed is induced by the dynamics, not present at t = 0.

**Recommenders.** At each of T = 150 rounds, a *content pool* is (by default) the current vector of agents' opinions. Each agent receives one recommendation $r_i$ from one of three strategies:
- **calibrated:** $r_i$ drawn uniformly from the pool — non-personalized, accuracy-faithful baseline.
- **sycophantic(s):** with probability $s$, $r_i$ = the pool item nearest to the agent's own current opinion; otherwise calibrated sampling. Swept $s \in \{0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0\}$.
- **bridging(b=0.5):** with probability $b$, $r_i$ = the pool item nearest to $-x_i$ (opposing content); otherwise calibrated.

**Update rule (main).** Hegselmann-Krause-style bounded confidence: if $|r_i - x_i| \le \varepsilon$ (default $\varepsilon=0.35$), $x_i \leftarrow x_i + \alpha(r_i - x_i)$, $\alpha=0.3$; otherwise a small backfire repulsion $x_i \leftarrow x_i - \gamma\,\text{sign}(r_i-x_i)|r_i-x_i|$, $\gamma=0.05$. Opinions clipped to $[-1,1]$.

**Metrics.** Opinion variance, Sarle's bimodality coefficient (BC; >5/9 conventionally suggestive of bimodality), and mean absolute opinion ("extremity"), at round 150.

**Statistical testing.** Pairwise Welch tests between calibrated and every other condition (bridging, sycophantic at 5 strengths) across all three metrics (18 tests), Bonferroni-corrected ($\alpha = 0.05/18 = 0.0028$).

**Pool-structure ablations.** (a) content pool subsampled each round to $K \in \{5,20,50,150,300\}$ agents; (b) a *static* external corpus of 300 values drawn once and held fixed; (c) new: a *drifting* external corpus, decoupled from agent opinions but itself random-walking each round ($\sigma=0.01$/round), so it is neither self-referential nor artificially static.

**Alternate update-rule ablation (new).** To separate "closest-match selection suppresses movement" from "our specific magnitude-scaled update rule suppresses movement when fed near-zero-distance inputs," we rerun calibrated vs. sycophantic(1.0) under a rule whose step size does *not* scale with $|r_i-x_i|$: within tolerance, $x_i \leftarrow x_i + \eta\,\text{sign}(r_i-x_i)$ with a *fixed* $\eta=0.03$ (direction only, magnitude constant); outside tolerance, the same backfire term as before.

**Tolerance ablation.** Calibrated vs. sycophantic(1.0) across $\varepsilon \in \{0.15,0.25,0.35,0.5,0.7\}$.

**Compute.** Pure NumPy/SciPy, CPU only, 10–20 seeds per condition. All code and raw results are included (`simulation.py`, `extra_experiments.py`, `ablation_eps.py`, `ablation_v3.py`, `plot_results.py`, `*_results.json`, logs).

## 3. Results

**Main sweep (Table 1, 20 seeds, mean ± SD, round 150):**

| Condition | Variance | Bimodality coeff. | Mean \|opinion\| |
|---|---|---|---|
| calibrated | 0.182 ± 0.077 | 0.562 ± 0.272 | 0.421 ± 0.213 |
| bridging (b=0.5) | 0.319 ± 0.028 | 0.319 ± 0.027 | 0.330 ± 0.028 |
| sycophantic (0.0) | 0.181 ± 0.073 | 0.618 ± 0.266 | 0.452 ± 0.220 |
| sycophantic (0.25) | 0.169 ± 0.051 | 0.531 ± 0.287 | 0.371 ± 0.166 |
| sycophantic (0.5) | 0.145 ± 0.021 | 0.333 ± 0.166 | 0.244 ± 0.060 |
| sycophantic (0.75) | 0.139 ± 0.025 | 0.259 ± 0.139 | 0.199 ± 0.050 |
| sycophantic (1.0) | 0.062 ± 0.004 | 0.358 ± 0.034 | 0.200 ± 0.006 |

Full sycophancy (s=1) recommends each agent the pool item closest to its own opinion, so $|r_i-x_i|\approx 0$ every round, updates are tiny, and the population stays near its initial state. Partial sycophancy mixes freezing with calibrated exposure, producing intermediate values.

**Bonferroni-corrected significance (18 tests, α=0.0028).** Only the high end survives correction: sycophantic(1.0) is significant on variance (t=-6.95, p=1.2e-6) and extremity (p=1.7e-4) but not bimodality (p=3.5e-3); sycophantic(0.75) is significant on extremity and bimodality but not variance (p=0.026, uncorrected-only); sycophantic(0.5) is significant only on extremity; s=0.0 and 0.25 are not distinguishable from calibrated on any metric. Bridging is significant on variance and bimodality but not extremity. The sample-mean trend is monotonic in variance/extremity, but only s ≥ 0.75 is a reliable departure from calibrated once corrected for 18 hypotheses.

**Realistic sycophancy rates vs. our sweep (reviewer question).** SYCON-Bench reports LLM sycophancy *rates* of roughly 9–18% under pushback — a different quantity from our $s$ (a per-recommendation probability of pure closest-match selection, not a rate of position-flipping under argument), so a literal $s \leftarrow$ rate substitution isn't principled. But taking 9–18% as a rough plausibility anchor places a realistic system near $s \in [0.1, 0.2]$ — below our smallest tested nonzero strength (0.25) and inside the region we show is statistically indistinguishable from calibrated after correction. This is a cautionary, not a reassuring, result: if this mechanism operates at rates comparable to measured LLM sycophancy, a variance/bimodality audit at our sample size would not detect it, even though the underlying freezing dynamic is real at those strengths in the raw (uncorrected) means.

**Pool-structure ablation, including new drift test.** 10 seeds, variance at round 150:

| Pool | calibrated var | sycophantic(1.0) var | ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| K=5 (sparse) | 0.342 ± 0.239 | 0.036 ± 0.050 | 9.5× |
| K=20 | 0.313 ± 0.173 | 0.035 ± 0.008 | 8.9× |
| K=50 | 0.252 ± 0.121 | 0.049 ± 0.005 | 5.1× |
| K=150 | 0.174 ± 0.069 | 0.059 ± 0.004 | 3.0× |
| K=300 (full) | 0.186 ± 0.066 | 0.063 ± 0.004 | 3.0× |
| Static external corpus | 0.242 ± 0.070 | 0.062 ± 0.004 | 3.9× |
| **Drifting external corpus (new)** | **0.304 ± 0.070** | **0.062 ± 0.005** | **4.9×** |

The drifting corpus (decoupled from agent opinions *and* not static — it random-walks with $\sigma=0.01$/round) rules out the concern that freezing depended on the external corpus being artificially frozen in place: sycophantic(1.0) variance is unchanged (0.062) whether the corpus is static or itself moving, while calibrated variance is if anything *higher* against a drifting corpus (0.304, t=-10.97, p=1.5e-6 vs. sycophantic). Combined with the K=5 sparse-pool result (freezing persists even when nearest-neighbor search is far from exact), this confirms freezing is not an artifact of pool density or corpus staleness.

**Alternate update-rule ablation (new; reviewer question 3).** Under the sign-based, fixed-step rule ($\eta=0.03$, magnitude independent of $|r_i-x_i|$), calibrated variance is 0.482 ± 0.168 and bimodality 0.889 ± 0.197 (more polarized than the magnitude-scaled rule, since fixed-size steps let agents walk further per accepted move); sycophantic(1.0) variance is 0.063 ± 0.004 and bimodality 0.355 ± 0.040 (t=-7.90, p=2.4e-5) — freezing of essentially the same magnitude as the main sweep. Since this rule's step size doesn't depend on recommendation distance, this rules out "freezing is trivially a magnitude-scaled update fed near-zero-distance inputs": with constant step size, sycophantic(1.0) still suppresses variance ~7.6× relative to calibrated. What transfers across rules is that closest-match selection makes almost every recommendation land within tolerance on the same side each round, capping *how far* an agent travels regardless of step size per move — freezing is about direction/coverage, a structural property of the selection strategy, not of our original update formula.

**Bridging vs. sycophancy.** Bridging increases variance (agents pushed toward the opposite pole and partially comply) while decreasing bimodality relative to calibrated — more spread-out but less two-camp-clustered, consistent with a diversity-forcing rather than polarization-inducing intervention.

**Why bimodality is non-monotonic while variance/extremity are monotonic.** Variance and extremity measure spread from center and shrink smoothly as freezing dominates. Bimodality (skew/kurtosis-based) is more sensitive to small residual clustering from the initial Gaussian's finite-sample asymmetry: a population can be nearly frozen (low variance) yet retain two small residual bumps (moderate BC), exactly what we see at s=1.0 (var=0.062, BC=0.358). The two metric families track different aspects of the same frozen configuration and need not covary monotonically.

**Tolerance ablation.** Calibrated variance ranges from 0.997 (ε=0.15, near-total polarization) down to 0.004 (ε=0.7, consensus), while sycophantic(1.0) variance is invariant at 0.061 across all five tolerances — recommended content stays within ~0 distance of the agent's own opinion regardless of tolerance width, confirming freezing is structural rather than an artifact of the ε=0.35 default.

## 4. Discussion and Limitations

The headline result — a purely agreement-seeking recommender *reduces* measured polarization, while a narrow-tolerance calibrated recommender can produce near-total polarization on its own — runs counter to the informal claim that "sycophantic algorithms polarize society." The effect is about *movement*: full sycophancy suppresses opinion change (a degenerate, low-diversity-of-experience fixed point), arguably still a socio-technical harm (stagnation, reduced belief updating, entrenchment) even though it is not "polarization" by variance/bimodality. This revision shows the effect (i) is statistically reliable only at high sycophancy strengths under multiple-comparison correction, (ii) is not a nearest-neighbor density or corpus-staleness artifact (persists under sparsification, decoupling, and drift), and (iii) is not an artifact of our specific magnitude-scaled update rule (persists under a fixed-step, sign-based rule). These three ablations narrow the space of "this is just a modeling artifact" explanations considerably, while the realistic-rate calculation is a genuine limitation: if real agreement-seeking curation operates at LLM-sycophancy-comparable strengths, our own statistical framework says it would be hard to detect via variance/bimodality auditing alone.

**Limitations.** This is a deliberately minimal, synthetic model, not a claim about real platforms. Opinions are one-dimensional; real belief systems are multidimensional and issue-coupled. We do not model network topology, engagement-driven feedback loops, adversarial actors, or heterogeneous susceptibility. "Sycophancy strength" is a hand-set probability, not derived from an actual LLM-based recommender or real engagement data; the SYCON-Bench comparison is a plausibility anchor, not a calibration. Connecting $s$ to a measured property of a deployed system, and extending the alternate-rule/drifting-corpus ablations to richer (e.g., DeGroot-averaging, network-structured) dynamics, remain future work.

## 5. Conclusion

We connected the LLM-sycophancy literature to the recommender-polarization literature by treating "agreement-seeking curation strength" as an explicit, sweepable parameter in a bounded-confidence opinion-dynamics simulation. Across a 20-seed sweep with Bonferroni-corrected significance testing, a tolerance ablation, a pool-structure ablation (sparsity, static and drifting external corpora), and a new alternate-update-rule ablation, we find sycophancy strength ≥0.75 reliably reduces final opinion variance and extremity relative to calibrated, because full sycophancy is a near-fixed-point of the dynamics rather than a polarizing force — structural to closest-match content selection and robust to how the update rule translates recommendation proximity into movement, not an artifact of pool density, corpus staleness, or our original update formula. A narrow-tolerance calibrated recommender can independently produce near-total polarization, and a bridging recommender increases spread while reducing bimodality. Realistic LLM sycophancy rates (9–18%) plausibly fall in the range our own corrected tests cannot statistically distinguish from calibrated — a caution about audit sensitivity, not a reassurance. We argue "freezing vs. polarizing" is a useful, testable distinction for socio-technical evaluation of agreement-seeking AI systems, and release all code and raw results for reuse.
