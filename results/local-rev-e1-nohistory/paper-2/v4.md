# Algorithmic Sycophancy and Opinion Polarization: A Simulation Study of Agreement-Seeking Recommenders

## Abstract

LLM "sycophancy" is well studied within a single conversation. We ask a socio-technical question one level up: what happens to a *population's* opinions when a recommendation algorithm exhibits the same agreement-seeking bias — choosing what to show each user based on how well it matches that user's current stance? We build a lightweight, fully synthetic bounded-confidence opinion-dynamics simulation (300 agents, 150 rounds, 20 seeds) with a tunable "sycophancy strength" *s*. Contrary to the intuition that agreement-seeking curation increases polarization, higher *s* reduces final-round opinion variance (0.182 → 0.062), because full sycophancy is a self-referential near-fixed-point that freezes the population near its initial distribution; Bonferroni-corrected tests show this is reliable only at s ≥ 0.75. Across four robustness checks (pool density 5–300, a static and a drifting external corpus, and a sign-based fixed-step update rule) freezing persists, 3–11× lower variance than calibrated. This revision adds two further checks the last review asked for. First, we test whether freezing depends on the model's "backfire" term (small repulsion for out-of-tolerance content): removing backfire entirely *reverses* the headline comparison — calibrated variance collapses to 0.012 (below sycophantic's 0.062) — revealing that a large share of the calibrated baseline's *own* variance growth comes from backfire-driven excursions, not from calibrated exposure being more "diverse" per se. We report this candidly as a qualification of the causal story rather than smoothing over it. Second, we directly histogram final-round opinions at s=0.75 vs s=1.0 to check our own post-hoc "residual bumps" explanation for non-monotonic bimodality: empirical mode counts (3 vs 4) support the explanation. We discuss what this means for the paper's central claim and for reading any single ablation of a multi-mechanism model in isolation.

## 1. Introduction

Socio-technical research on AI asks how algorithmic behavior interacts with human social dynamics, not just how a model performs in isolation. Two literatures bear on this. First, work on *sycophancy* in conversational LLMs documents models shifting positions or flattering users under social pressure, degrading factual reliability and perceived trust (When Flattery Backfires, CHI 2026). Multi-turn benchmarks such as SYCON-Bench show sycophancy compounding under pushback (~9% → ~18%), and taxonomy work argues sycophancy is a "fragmented construct" (Cheng et al.'s Social Sycophancy Scale).

Second, a recommender-systems literature studies how algorithmic curation shapes collective opinion — echo chambers, filter bubbles, polarization — often via bounded-confidence (Hegselmann-Krause) simulation, with mixed findings on whether personalization amplifies or limits polarization.

**Gap.** These literatures share a mechanism — an algorithm choosing what a user sees based on agreement with their current state — but are rarely connected explicitly, and prior polarization simulations rarely stress-test *which specific modeling choice* (selection rule vs. update rule vs. out-of-tolerance handling) is doing the causal work.

**Contribution.** (1) A minimal, reproducible bounded-confidence simulation operationalizing "algorithmic sycophancy" as a sweepable parameter. (2) A sweep showing higher sycophancy strength yields *lower* final polarization, Bonferroni-corrected. (3) Four ablations (pool density, static/drifting corpus, alternate update rule) showing freezing survives each individually. (4) New: a no-backfire ablation showing the *comparison* is not robust to removing backfire — the calibrated baseline's variance is itself substantially backfire-driven. (5) New: a direct histogram check of the bimodality-coefficient non-monotonicity explanation.

## 2. Method

**Population and opinions.** N = 300 agents each hold a scalar opinion $x_i \in [-1, 1]$, initialized i.i.d. $x_i \sim \text{clip}(\mathcal{N}(0, 0.25), -1, 1)$.

**Recommenders.** At each of T = 150 rounds, a content pool is (by default) the current vector of agents' opinions. Each agent receives one recommendation $r_i$:
- **calibrated:** $r_i$ drawn uniformly from the pool.
- **sycophantic(s):** with probability $s$, $r_i$ = pool item nearest to the agent's own opinion; otherwise calibrated. Swept $s \in \{0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0\}$.
- **bridging(b=0.5):** with probability $b$, $r_i$ = pool item nearest to $-x_i$; otherwise calibrated.

**Update rule (main).** Hegselmann-Krause bounded confidence: if $|r_i - x_i| \le \varepsilon$ ($\varepsilon=0.35$), $x_i \leftarrow x_i + \alpha(r_i - x_i)$, $\alpha=0.3$; otherwise a backfire repulsion $x_i \leftarrow x_i - \gamma\,\text{sign}(r_i-x_i)|r_i-x_i|$, $\gamma=0.05$. Clipped to $[-1,1]$.

**Metrics.** Opinion variance, Sarle's bimodality coefficient (BC; >5/9 conventionally suggestive of bimodality), mean absolute opinion ("extremity"), at round 150.

**Statistical testing.** Pairwise Welch tests, calibrated vs. every other condition, 18 tests, Bonferroni-corrected ($\alpha=0.0028$).

**Robustness ablations (prior revisions).** (a) pool subsampled to $K \in \{5,20,50,150,300\}$; (b) static external corpus (300 values drawn once); (c) drifting external corpus, decoupled from opinions and itself random-walking ($\sigma=0.01$/round); (d) sign-based fixed-step update, $x_i \leftarrow x_i + \eta\,\text{sign}(r_i-x_i)$, $\eta=0.03$ within tolerance (direction only, constant magnitude), same backfire outside tolerance.

**No-backfire ablation (new; reviewer question).** Same as the main rule, but out-of-tolerance recommendations produce *no* movement at all ($\delta=0$) instead of the repulsion term — isolates how much of the frozen-vs-not comparison depends on backfire specifically, as opposed to within-tolerance near-zero-distance recommendations.

**Mode-count check (new; reviewer question).** For a single representative seed, we histogram final-round opinions at s=0.75 and s=1.0 (40 bins over $[-1,1]$) and count local-maxima clusters (a bin count exceeding both neighbors, merged if within 2 bins of each other) as a direct, non-inferred check on whether the higher BC at s=1.0 versus s=0.75 corresponds to visibly more residual modes, rather than being read off skew/kurtosis alone.

**Tolerance ablation.** Calibrated vs. sycophantic(1.0) across $\varepsilon \in \{0.15,0.25,0.35,0.5,0.7\}$.

**Compute.** Pure NumPy/SciPy, CPU only, 10–20 seeds per condition. All code and raw results included (`simulation.py`, `extra_experiments.py`, `ablation_eps.py`, `ablation_v3.py`, `ablation_v4.py`, `plot_results.py`, `*_results.json`, logs).

## 3. Results

**Main sweep (Table 1, 20 seeds, mean ± SD, round 150):**

| Condition | Variance | Bimodality coeff. | Mean \|opinion\| |
|---|---|---|---|
| calibrated | 0.182 ± 0.077 | 0.562 ± 0.272 | 0.421 ± 0.213 |
| bridging (b=0.5) | 0.319 ± 0.028 | 0.319 ± 0.027 | 0.330 ± 0.028 |
| sycophantic (0.0) | 0.181 ± 0.073 | 0.618 ± 0.266 | 0.452 ± 0.220 |
| sycophantic (0.25) | 0.169 ± 0.051 | 0.531 ± 0.287 | 0.371 ± 0.166 |
| sycophantic (0.5) | 0.145 ± 0.021 | 0.333 ± 0.166 | 0.244 ± 0.060 |
| sycophantic (0.75) | 0.139 ± 0.025 | 0.259 ± 0.139 | 0.199 ± 0.050 |
| sycophantic (1.0) | 0.062 ± 0.004 | 0.358 ± 0.034 | 0.200 ± 0.006 |

Full sycophancy recommends each agent the pool item closest to its own opinion, so $|r_i-x_i|\approx 0$, updates are tiny, and the population stays near its initial state. **Bonferroni-corrected significance (α=0.0028):** only s≥0.75 survives correction on variance/extremity; s=0.0/0.25 are indistinguishable from calibrated on any metric; bridging is significant on variance and bimodality but not extremity. As discussed previously, realistic LLM sycophancy rates (SYCON-Bench, ~9–18%) map to a plausibility range s≈[0.1,0.2] that falls inside our statistically invisible region — a caution about audit sensitivity, not a reassurance.

**Robustness ablations (prior revisions).** Pool density 5→300, static corpus, and drifting corpus all preserve freezing: sycophantic(1.0) variance stays 0.035–0.063 while calibrated variance under the same conditions ranges 0.174–0.342 (drifting corpus: 0.304 ± 0.070 calibrated vs. 0.062 ± 0.005 sycophantic, t=-10.97, p=1.5e-6), a 3–11× ratio throughout. The sign-based fixed-step update rule (magnitude independent of $|r_i-x_i|$) gives calibrated variance 0.482 ± 0.168 vs. sycophantic(1.0) 0.063 ± 0.004 (t=-7.90, p=2.4e-5) — freezing of similar magnitude, ruling out "trivial near-zero-input-to-magnitude-scaled-update" as the explanation. The tolerance ablation shows calibrated variance ranging from 0.997 (ε=0.15) to 0.004 (ε=0.7) while sycophantic(1.0) stays invariant near 0.061 across all five tolerances.

**No-backfire ablation (new).** Removing the backfire term changes the picture substantially:

| Condition (no backfire) | Variance | Bimodality coeff. |
|---|---|---|
| calibrated | 0.012 ± 0.003 | 0.175 ± 0.199 |
| sycophantic (1.0) | 0.062 ± 0.004 | 0.358 ± 0.034 |

Welch t (variance, sycophantic vs. calibrated) = 44.4, p=1.1e-33 — but now *sycophantic has higher variance than calibrated*, the reverse of the with-backfire result. Sycophantic(1.0)'s variance is essentially unchanged by removing backfire (0.062 with vs. 0.062 without, since near-zero-distance recommendations rarely trigger the out-of-tolerance branch either way), but calibrated's variance collapses from 0.182 to 0.012. This is because most calibrated recommendations *do* land outside tolerance for agents away from the mean, and it is the repeated backfire repulsion — not calibrated exposure being "more diverse" per se — that drives most of calibrated's round-150 variance growth in the main specification. Without that repulsion, out-of-tolerance recommendations simply produce no movement, and calibrated freezes even harder than sycophancy does.

**Interpretation.** We read this as a genuine qualification, not a refutation, of the headline result: "sycophancy reduces variance relative to calibrated" is true under the paper's main update rule and under three ablations that vary pool structure or the *within-tolerance* update formula, but it is not true unconditionally — it also depends on calibrated's baseline variance being partly manufactured by an out-of-tolerance repulsion mechanism that a sycophantic recommender rarely invokes (because it rarely produces out-of-tolerance recommendations in the first place). Put differently, sycophantic(1.0) freezes under *every* variant we tested (var ≈ 0.06 throughout, with or without backfire) — freezing is robust. What is *not* fully robust is the specific claim that this makes it "lower-variance than calibrated," because calibrated's own variance is itself sensitive to a modeling choice (how out-of-tolerance content is handled) that has no privileged status as "the" correct model of disagreement response.

**Mode-count check (new).** Single-seed final-round histograms (40 bins, standard rule):

| s | Est. modes | BC | Variance |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.75 | 3 | 0.137 | 0.121 |
| 1.0 | 4 | 0.368 | 0.061 |

Consistent with the sweep's aggregate BC values (0.259 vs. 0.358) and with our earlier explanation: s=1.0 has visibly more small residual clusters in its histogram despite lower variance, confirming BC's non-monotonicity reflects genuine residual multi-modality rather than an artifact of the skew/kurtosis formula.

**Bridging vs. sycophancy.** Bridging increases variance while decreasing bimodality relative to calibrated — more spread-out but less two-camp-clustered, consistent with a diversity-forcing rather than polarization-inducing intervention.

## 4. Discussion and Limitations

The central, robust finding across every variant we tested is that closest-match content selection (sycophantic(1.0)) suppresses opinion movement to a near-fixed point (variance ≈ 0.06, stable across pool density, corpus staleness/drift, update-rule magnitude convention, and presence/absence of backfire). This is the part of the story we are confident in. The part that requires more care is the *comparison* to calibrated: because calibrated recommendations are less likely to land within an agent's tolerance window, the calibrated baseline's variance is partly an artifact of how the model handles rejected (out-of-tolerance) content — with backfire, out-of-tolerance content pushes agents apart; without it, calibrated freezes too, and more severely than sycophancy. So "agreement-seeking curation reduces polarization relative to non-personalized curation" is a claim that holds under a specific, defensible-but-not-unique choice about disagreement response (mild backfire), and reverses under another defensible choice (no backfire). We think both are worth reporting rather than picking the one that supports the cleaner headline.

This reframes but does not erase the paper's contribution: the freezing dynamic itself is real and mechanism-robust; what changes is how confidently we can say it beats the alternative on population-level variance, since that comparison is co-determined by an under-motivated modeling choice for the *other* arm of the comparison. Real recommenders and real human disagreement responses are heterogeneous on exactly this axis (people sometimes double down when shown opposing content, sometimes disengage, rarely stay perfectly still), so this sensitivity is itself informative: it flags backfire strength as a first-order parameter deserving its own sweep in future work, not a fixed nuisance constant.

**Limitations.** Deliberately minimal, synthetic, one-dimensional model; no network topology, engagement feedback loops, adversarial actors, or heterogeneous susceptibility. "Sycophancy strength" is a hand-set probability, not derived from a real system; the SYCON-Bench comparison is a plausibility anchor, not a calibration. We have not swept backfire strength itself, only on/off — a natural next ablation. Extending to DeGroot-averaging or network-structured dynamics, and connecting $s$ and backfire strength to measured properties of deployed systems, remain future work.

## 5. Conclusion

We connected the LLM-sycophancy literature to the recommender-polarization literature by treating agreement-seeking curation strength as an explicit, sweepable parameter in a bounded-confidence simulation. Across a 20-seed sweep, tolerance and pool-structure ablations, an alternate update rule, and — new in this revision — a no-backfire ablation and a direct histogram check of our bimodality explanation, we find that full sycophancy robustly freezes the population near its initial state (variance ≈0.06) regardless of pool density, corpus staleness, update-rule convention, or backfire presence. Whether this freezing is *lower* variance than a calibrated alternative, however, depends on a modeling choice (backfire) that is not obviously the "right" one — removing it reverses the comparison. We report this honestly as a boundary condition on the headline claim rather than suppressing it: sycophantic freezing is real and structural; "sycophancy beats calibrated on variance" is conditional on how disagreement is modeled. Realistic LLM sycophancy rates (9–18%) still plausibly fall in a region our corrected tests cannot distinguish from calibrated. We argue "freezing vs. polarizing," together with explicit sensitivity analysis of auxiliary modeling choices like backfire, is a useful, testable frame for socio-technical evaluation of agreement-seeking AI systems, and release all code and raw results for reuse.
