# When Calibration Fights Fidelity: Temperature Scaling Can Move Language Model Predictions Away From the True Generative Distribution

## Abstract

Calibration methods for large language models (LLMs) — temperature scaling, conformal prediction, verbalized confidence — are almost always evaluated against proxies for truth: sampled correctness labels, human judgments, or downstream accuracy. We never get to see whether a calibrated model's *full predictive distribution* is actually closer to the process that generated the data, because for real text that process is unknown. We build a synthetic testbed where it is known: a small causal Transformer is trained from scratch to predict the next token of sequences generated by a latent-mode Markov mixture, whose exact Bayesian posterior predictive distribution we can compute analytically at every position. This lets us measure calibration error and distributional fidelity (KL divergence to the true generative distribution) simultaneously and exactly, rather than approximately. We find that temperature scaling fit by validation NLL — the standard LLM calibration recipe — reliably *improves* top-1 confidence calibration (oracle-ECE drops from 0.030 to 0.006 in our best-trained model) while simultaneously *increasing* KL divergence to the true distribution (0.046 → 0.052), i.e., it makes the model's stated confidence more trustworthy while making its full belief state less accurate. Split conformal prediction, by contrast, hits its target coverage (90.0% observed vs. 90% nominal) regardless of temperature, because rank-based nonconformity scores are invariant to the monotonic rescaling temperature applies to logits. We also show both effects strengthen with training budget: undertrained models have no calibration/fidelity gap to exploit, but well-trained ones do. This suggests that scalar temperature scaling optimizes a proxy that can trade away distributional accuracy for reportable confidence — a distinction invisible in standard LLM calibration benchmarks but visible here.

## 1. Introduction

Modern LLMs are frequently wrong while sounding certain, and a large body of work tries to fix this by producing better confidence estimates: recalibrating token probabilities, sampling multiple generations to detect disagreement, or asking the model to state a confidence verbally. Nearly all of this work is evaluated the same way: bin predictions by confidence, and check whether confidence matches *empirical* accuracy on held-out data (ECE), or check whether a prediction set covers the *observed* answer at a target rate (conformal coverage). Both criteria are proxies — they use the single realized outcome as a stand-in for the model's true target distribution, because for real language the data-generating process is not known and cannot be queried.

This matters because a calibration method could, in principle, satisfy a proxy metric (matching confidence to observed correctness frequency) while moving the model's full predictive distribution *further* from the true generative process — e.g., by sharpening or flattening probabilities in a way that happens to align average confidence with average accuracy without making the distribution itself more accurate. Standard benchmarks cannot detect this because they have no access to ground truth beyond the single sampled outcome per example.

**Related work.** LLM uncertainty quantification is an active area with several recent surveys categorizing the space by uncertainty source and elicitation method (Uncertainty Quantification and Confidence Calibration in LLMs: A Survey, KDD 2025; A Survey on Uncertainty Quantification of LLMs, arXiv:2412.05563). Verbalized/self-reported confidence has been studied as a cheap alternative to internal signals, but shown to only partially align with internal probabilities and to be sensitive to prompt design. Semantic entropy and its cheaper approximation, semantic entropy probes, detect hallucination by sampling multiple generations and measuring meaning-level disagreement, but are known to fail on confident hallucinations where the model is *consistently* wrong (low entropy, wrong answer). Temperature scaling remains the standard post-hoc calibration recipe inherited from the classification literature and is widely applied to LLM logits with little scrutiny of what it trades off. Conformal prediction has recently been adapted to LLMs to give finite-sample, distribution-free coverage guarantees for prediction sets over tokens or semantically-clustered generations (Conformal Language Modeling, ICLR 2024; ConU, EMNLP Findings 2024; CoVeR, arXiv:2509.04733), and is attractive precisely because its guarantee does not depend on the model being well-calibrated in the ECE sense. None of this literature, to our knowledge, has been able to check calibration methods against a *known* ground-truth predictive distribution, because real LLMs are trained on text whose generative process is inaccessible. Our contribution is a minimal synthetic environment that removes this obstacle: we construct a controllable generative process for which the exact Bayes-optimal predictive distribution is computable in closed form at every sequence position, letting us audit calibration methods against ground truth rather than against a proxy.

## 2. Method

**Generative process.** We define a latent-mode Markov mixture over a vocabulary of size $V=10$. There are $K=3$ hidden "modes," each with its own row-stochastic transition matrix $T_m$ ($V \times V$, rows drawn from a symmetric Dirichlet($\alpha=0.3$) — a low concentration chosen so the three matrices are visibly distinguishable from early tokens). To generate a sequence of length $T=40$: draw a mode $m \sim \mathrm{Uniform}\{1,\dots,K\}$ once per sequence (fixed prior $1/K$), draw the first token uniformly, then emit each subsequent token via $x_{t+1} \sim T_m(x_t, \cdot)$. The mode is never observed by the learner — it must be inferred implicitly from the emitted tokens, so early positions in a sequence carry genuine epistemic uncertainty (which mode?) on top of the chain's aleatoric uncertainty, and this epistemic component shrinks as more tokens are observed.

**Exact ground truth.** Because we know all $T_m$ and the mode prior, the true Bayesian posterior predictive $p_{\text{true}}(x_{t+1}\mid x_{1:t})$ is computable exactly via a forward filtering recursion (an HMM-style update): maintain a posterior over modes $p(m\mid x_{1:t})$, updated after each observed transition by Bayes' rule using $T_m(x_{t},x_{t+1})$ as the likelihood, and predict $p_{\text{true}}(x_{t+1}\mid x_{1:t}) = \sum_m p(m\mid x_{1:t})\,T_m(x_t,\cdot)$. This is the ceiling any predictor could achieve, and it is *exact*, not sample-estimated.

**Learner.** We train a small causal Transformer (2 layers, 4 heads, $d_{\text{model}}=48$, ~50K parameters) from scratch with next-token cross-entropy on 4,000 generated sequences, using AdamW (lr $3\times 10^{-3}$). No pretrained weights are used or available in this environment (offline, CPU-only). We evaluate its predictive distribution against $p_{\text{true}}$ on 1,200 held-out test sequences using two metrics computed exactly at every position:
- **KL$(p_{\text{true}} \,\|\, p_{\text{model}})$**, averaged over positions, split into early ($t<10$) vs. late ($t\ge10$) buckets to track the epistemic-uncertainty-reduction effect.
- **Oracle-ECE**: standard ECE machinery (bin by the model's top-1 confidence, compare to accuracy) but with accuracy replaced by the *exact* true probability $p_{\text{true}}$ assigns to the model's top-1 token — removing finite-sample noise from the usual ECE estimate.

**Calibration methods compared.**
1. *Raw* softmax (T=1), the uncalibrated baseline.
2. *Temperature scaling*: a single scalar $T^*$ fit by grid search (41 values in $[0.5,2.5]$) to minimize NLL of the *observed* next tokens on a held-out validation split of 800 sequences — the standard recipe from the LLM calibration literature.
3. *Split conformal prediction*: nonconformity score $s(x_{1:t}, x_{t+1}) = 1 - p_{\text{model}}(x_{t+1}\mid x_{1:t})$, calibrated on 800 held-out sequences to a target coverage of 90% via the standard finite-sample quantile correction, applied to both raw and temperature-scaled probabilities. We report empirical test-set coverage and average prediction-set size.

We also run an ablation over training budget (50–1,200 optimizer steps, batch size 64) to see whether the calibration/fidelity relationship depends on how well-trained the model is, using identical data (same seed) at each budget.

## 3. Results

**Main run (600 training steps).** Temperature scaling selected $T^*=1.05$ (val NLL 1.5923 vs. 1.5925 at $T{=}1$ — a negligible NLL improvement). Despite the tiny NLL gain, its effect on the two metrics diverged:

| Metric | Raw (T=1) | Scaled (T=1.05) | Uniform baseline | Oracle (true dist.) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| KL, early ($t<10$) | 0.0844 | 0.0886 | 1.3313 | 0.0000 |
| KL, late ($t\ge10$) | 0.0548 | 0.0616 | 1.8750 | 0.0000 |
| KL, overall | 0.0624 | 0.0685 | 1.7356 | 0.0000 |
| Oracle-ECE | 0.0178 | 0.0041 | — | — |

Temperature scaling *increased* KL divergence to the true distribution at every position bucket (worse fidelity) while *decreasing* oracle-ECE by 4.3× (better top-1 calibration). Both the raw and scaled models comfortably beat the uniform baseline and both show the expected epistemic-uncertainty pattern (KL is higher early in the sequence, when the mode is still ambiguous, than late).

Split conformal prediction hit its 90% target almost exactly in both cases: raw coverage 0.900 (set size 4.37/10 tokens), scaled coverage 0.900 (set size 4.37/10). Coverage and set size were essentially unaffected by temperature scaling, consistent with the fact that dividing logits by a positive scalar before softmax preserves the rank ordering of token probabilities, and split conformal's threshold-quantile construction is invariant to that reranking-free rescaling — its finite-sample guarantee holds regardless of how (mis)calibrated the underlying scores are.

**Ablation over training budget.** We retrained from scratch at five step budgets (same data/seed):

| Steps | $T^*$ | Train loss | KL raw | KL scaled | ECE raw | ECE scaled |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 50 | 1.00 | 1.864 | 0.6145 | 0.6145 | 0.0183 | 0.0183 |
| 150 | 1.00 | 1.777 | 0.2664 | 0.2664 | 0.0185 | 0.0185 |
| 300 | 1.00 | 1.620 | 0.1090 | 0.1090 | 0.0163 | 0.0163 |
| 600 | 1.10 | 1.569 | 0.0612 | 0.0730 | 0.0245 | 0.0107 |
| 1200 | 1.10 | 1.531 | 0.0457 | 0.0522 | 0.0298 | 0.0063 |

Two clear trends: (1) KL-to-truth falls monotonically with training as expected — the model's predictive distribution converges toward the true generative process. (2) The calibration/fidelity dissociation is a *late-training* phenomenon: at 50–300 steps the model is still underconfident/noisy enough that grid search selects $T^*=1$ (no correction helps), and raw and scaled columns are identical. Only once the model is well-trained enough to be measurably *overconfident* (600+ steps, matching the well-known overconfidence pattern in trained neural sequence models) does temperature scaling do anything — and when it does, it improves oracle-ECE at the cost of KL fidelity, with the gap widening as training continues (ECE improves 4.7× at 1200 steps while KL fidelity worsens by 14%). This suggests the dissociation is not a small-sample artifact of our particular run but a structural property of fitting a single scalar to minimize NLL against realized outcomes: it can sharpen or flatten the *whole* distribution to fix a *marginal* (top-1-vs-accuracy) statistic, without that sharpening making individual non-top tokens' probabilities more accurate.

**Limitations.** This is a small, fully synthetic testbed (10-token vocabulary, 3 modes, one architecture, one seed for the main run, single-scalar temperature scaling only) chosen to fit a CPU, few-minutes budget with no internet access — real LLM vocabularies, contexts, and failure modes are far richer, and we do not claim the effect size transfers quantitatively. We also only tested one conformal score function (probability of the true token) and did not explore multi-parameter recalibration (vector/matrix scaling) or verbalized confidence, both flagged as promising in the survey literature. The result should be read as an existence proof — temperature scaling *can* trade distributional fidelity for reportable calibration under realistic training dynamics — not as a claim about the average case in production LLMs.

## 4. Conclusion

We built a synthetic environment where the ground-truth predictive distribution of a sequence model is known exactly, letting us evaluate LLM calibration methods against truth rather than against sampled proxies for the first time in a controlled way. Temperature scaling, the default LLM calibration recipe, improved standard top-1 calibration substantially but simultaneously moved the model's full predictive distribution further from the true generative process — a trade-off invisible to standard ECE-style evaluation and one that only emerged once the underlying model was trained enough to be overconfident. Split conformal prediction achieved its coverage guarantee independent of this trade-off, consistent with its rank-invariance. Practically, this suggests that when the downstream use of an LLM's probabilities depends on the *shape* of the distribution (e.g., diverse sampling, uncertainty over reasoning branches, entropy-based routing) rather than just top-1 confidence, temperature scaling's NLL-based fitting objective should not be assumed harmless, and coverage-based methods may be a safer default. Future work: extend the testbed to matrix/vector scaling and verbalized confidence, use richer generative processes (e.g., context-free grammars) to test whether the dissociation grows with model/task complexity, and check whether it appears with pretrained LLMs on tasks with partially-known structure (e.g., synthetic arithmetic with a known answer distribution under label noise).
