# When Calibration Fights Fidelity: Temperature Scaling Can Move Language Model Predictions Away From the True Generative Distribution

## Abstract

Calibration methods for large language models (LLMs) — temperature scaling, conformal prediction, verbalized confidence — are almost always evaluated against proxies for truth: sampled correctness labels, human judgments, or downstream accuracy. We never get to see whether a calibrated model's *full predictive distribution* is actually closer to the process that generated the data, because for real text that process is unknown. We build a synthetic testbed where it is known: a small causal Transformer is trained from scratch to predict the next token of sequences generated by a latent-mode Markov mixture, whose exact Bayesian posterior predictive distribution we can compute analytically at every position. This lets us measure calibration error and distributional fidelity (KL divergence to the true generative distribution) simultaneously and exactly. Across 5 seeds at our main 600-step training budget, temperature scaling fit by validation NLL — the standard LLM calibration recipe — activates (selects $T^*\neq 1$) in 2/5 seeds, and whenever it activates it *increases* mean KL divergence to the true distribution (by 0.005–0.006) while *decreasing* oracle-ECE (by 2–3×); it never improves KL fidelity when active. Critically, the *KL-minimizing* scalar temperature is different from and on the opposite side of $T{=}1$ from the NLL-minimizing one (mean $T^*_{\text{KL}}=0.92$ vs. mean $T^*_{\text{NLL}}=1.02$ across seeds), and optimizing for KL fidelity directly makes oracle-ECE substantially *worse* (mean 0.045 vs. raw 0.020) — showing the dissociation is not an artifact of scalar rescaling per se but a genuine conflict between the two objectives. Split conformal prediction, by contrast, hits its target coverage (90.0% observed vs. 90% nominal) regardless of temperature, because rank-based nonconformity scores are invariant to the monotonic rescaling temperature applies to logits. We also show both effects strengthen with training budget: undertrained models have no calibration/fidelity gap to exploit, but well-trained ones do. This suggests scalar temperature scaling optimizes a proxy that can trade away distributional accuracy for reportable confidence — a distinction invisible in standard LLM calibration benchmarks but visible here.

## 1. Introduction

Modern LLMs are frequently wrong while sounding certain, and a large body of work tries to fix this by producing better confidence estimates: recalibrating token probabilities, sampling multiple generations to detect disagreement, or asking the model to state a confidence verbally. Nearly all of this work is evaluated the same way: bin predictions by confidence, and check whether confidence matches *empirical* accuracy on held-out data (ECE), or check whether a prediction set covers the *observed* answer at a target rate (conformal coverage). Both criteria are proxies — they use the single realized outcome as a stand-in for the model's true target distribution, because for real language the data-generating process is not known and cannot be queried.

This matters because a calibration method could, in principle, satisfy a proxy metric (matching confidence to observed correctness frequency) while moving the model's full predictive distribution *further* from the true generative process — e.g., by sharpening or flattening probabilities in a way that happens to align average confidence with average accuracy without making the distribution itself more accurate. Standard benchmarks cannot detect this because they have no access to ground truth beyond the single sampled outcome per example.

**Related work.** LLM uncertainty quantification is an active area with several recent surveys categorizing the space by uncertainty source and elicitation method (Uncertainty Quantification and Confidence Calibration in LLMs: A Survey, KDD 2025; A Survey on Uncertainty Quantification of LLMs, arXiv:2412.05563). Verbalized/self-reported confidence has been studied as a cheap alternative to internal signals, but shown to only partially align with internal probabilities and to be sensitive to prompt design. Semantic entropy and its cheaper approximation, semantic entropy probes, detect hallucination by sampling multiple generations and measuring meaning-level disagreement, but are known to fail on confident hallucinations where the model is *consistently* wrong (low entropy, wrong answer). Temperature scaling remains the standard post-hoc calibration recipe inherited from the classification literature and is widely applied to LLM logits with little scrutiny of what it trades off. Conformal prediction has recently been adapted to LLMs to give finite-sample, distribution-free coverage guarantees for prediction sets over tokens or semantically-clustered generations (Conformal Language Modeling, ICLR 2024; ConU, EMNLP Findings 2024; CoVeR, arXiv:2509.04733), attractive precisely because its guarantee does not depend on the model being well-calibrated in the ECE sense. None of this literature, to our knowledge, checks calibration methods against a *known* ground-truth predictive distribution, since real LLMs are trained on text whose generative process is inaccessible. Our contribution is a minimal synthetic environment that removes this obstacle: a controllable generative process whose exact Bayes-optimal predictive distribution is computable in closed form at every sequence position, letting us audit calibration methods against ground truth rather than a proxy.

## 2. Method

**Generative process.** We define a latent-mode Markov mixture over a vocabulary of size $V=10$. There are $K=3$ hidden "modes," each with its own row-stochastic transition matrix $T_m$ ($V \times V$, rows drawn from a symmetric Dirichlet($\alpha=0.3$) — a low concentration chosen so the three matrices are visibly distinguishable from early tokens). To generate a sequence of length $T=40$: draw a mode $m \sim \mathrm{Uniform}\{1,\dots,K\}$ once per sequence, draw the first token uniformly, then emit each subsequent token via $x_{t+1} \sim T_m(x_t, \cdot)$. The mode is never observed by the learner, so early positions carry genuine epistemic uncertainty (which mode?) on top of aleatoric uncertainty, shrinking as more tokens are observed.

**Exact ground truth.** Because we know all $T_m$ and the mode prior, the true Bayesian posterior predictive $p_{\text{true}}(x_{t+1}\mid x_{1:t})$ is computable exactly via forward filtering (HMM-style): maintain a posterior over modes, updated after each observed transition by Bayes' rule, and predict $p_{\text{true}}(x_{t+1}\mid x_{1:t}) = \sum_m p(m\mid x_{1:t})\,T_m(x_t,\cdot)$. This is the ceiling any predictor could achieve, and it is *exact*, not sample-estimated.

**Learner.** We train a small causal Transformer (2 layers, 4 heads, $d_{\text{model}}=48$, ~50K parameters) from scratch with next-token cross-entropy on 4,000 generated sequences, using AdamW (lr $3\times 10^{-3}$), CPU-only, no pretrained weights. We evaluate against $p_{\text{true}}$ on 1,200 held-out test sequences using two exact metrics at every position: **KL$(p_{\text{true}} \,\|\, p_{\text{model}})$** (early $t<10$ vs. late $t\ge10$ buckets), and **oracle-ECE** (standard ECE binning machinery, but with empirical accuracy replaced by the exact true probability $p_{\text{true}}$ assigns to the model's top-1 token, removing finite-sample noise).

**Calibration methods.** (1) *Raw* softmax ($T=1$). (2) *Temperature scaling fit by NLL*: scalar $T^*_{\text{NLL}}$ from a 41-point grid search over $[0.5,2.5]$ minimizing NLL of observed next tokens on an 800-sequence validation split — the standard LLM recipe. (3) *Temperature scaling fit by KL* (new in this revision): the same grid search, but selecting $T^*_{\text{KL}}$ to directly minimize mean KL$(p_{\text{true}}\|p_{\text{model}})$ on the test set. This is not a fittable real-world recipe (it requires ground truth), but characterizes the best any single scalar temperature could do for fidelity, letting us isolate whether the dissociation is inherent to scalar rescaling or specific to the NLL objective. (4) *Split conformal prediction*: nonconformity score $s = 1 - p_{\text{model}}(x_{t+1}\mid x_{1:t})$, calibrated on 800 held-out sequences to 90% target coverage, applied to raw and NLL-scaled probabilities.

**Seeds and robustness (new in this revision).** The original submission reported a single seed for the main 600-step run. In response to review, we rerun the entire main-run pipeline (fresh data generation, model, training, and both grid searches) at 5 independent seeds (0–4) to characterize how much of the reported gap survives reseeding, and we report mean ± std across seeds throughout. We also check sensitivity of the reported ECE improvement factor to the number of ECE bins ($n_{\text{bins}}\in\{5,10,15,20\}$).

We also run an ablation over training budget (50–1,200 optimizer steps) to see whether the calibration/fidelity relationship depends on how well-trained the model is, using identical data (same seed) at each budget.

## 3. Results

**Main run, 5 seeds at 600 steps.** Averaged across seeds: KL raw $=0.0661\pm0.0070$, KL scaled (NLL) $=0.0683\pm0.0054$, oracle-ECE raw $=0.0204\pm0.0046$, oracle-ECE scaled (NLL) $=0.0156\pm0.0079$. Grid search selected $T^*_{\text{NLL}}\neq1$ in only 2/5 seeds (seeds 0, 1: $T^*=1.05$; seeds 2–4: $T^*=1.00$, i.e. no correction — val-NLL was already minimized at $T=1$). This itself is informative: at 600 steps the model is only sometimes measurably overconfident enough for NLL-fitting to trigger a correction. But in every seed where it *did* trigger, the effect replicated in the same direction: KL rose (seed 0: $0.0589\to0.0637$; seed 1: $0.0610\to0.0671$) while oracle-ECE fell sharply (seed 0: $0.0221\to0.0092$; seed 1: $0.0165\to0.0056$). We never observed a seed where NLL-fit scaling improved both metrics or hurt both — when it does anything, it is always this same calibration-for-fidelity trade.

**KL-optimal vs. NLL-optimal temperature (addressing whether the trade-off is inherent to scalar rescaling).** Averaged across seeds, the KL-minimizing temperature was $T^*_{\text{KL}}=0.92\pm0.02$ — consistently *below* 1, i.e. sharpening the distribution, and on the *opposite side* of $T=1$ from the NLL-minimizing temperature ($T^*_{\text{NLL}}=1.02\pm0.02$, which flattens or leaves it unchanged). Using $T^*_{\text{KL}}$ instead of $T^*_{\text{NLL}}$ does improve mean KL (raw $0.0661\to$ KL-scaled $0.0617$, a genuine fidelity gain, versus NLL-scaling's $0.0661\to0.0683$ fidelity loss) — confirming a scalar temperature *can* help fidelity if fit against the right target. But it comes at a steep calibration cost: oracle-ECE under $T^*_{\text{KL}}$ is $0.0447\pm0.0052$, *more than double* the raw baseline ($0.0204$) and roughly 3× worse than NLL-scaled ECE ($0.0156$). So the dissociation is not simply "NLL-fitting is the wrong objective and a better one would fix everything" — the two objectives (match observed-token NLL vs. match the full distributional shape) pull the single scalar knob in opposite directions, and no single $T$ serves both. This directly answers whether the effect is fundamental to any scalar choice or an NLL-specific artifact: it is fundamental to the *conflict between objectives*, not fixable by picking a different scalar unless one already has access to ground truth (which real deployments do not).

| Metric (mean ± std, 5 seeds) | Raw ($T{=}1$) | Scaled by NLL ($T^*_{\text{NLL}}{\approx}1.02$) | Scaled by KL ($T^*_{\text{KL}}{\approx}0.92$) |
|---|---|---|---|
| KL, overall | 0.0661 ± 0.0070 | 0.0683 ± 0.0054 | 0.0617 ± 0.0050 |
| Oracle-ECE | 0.0204 ± 0.0046 | 0.0156 ± 0.0079 | 0.0447 ± 0.0052 |

**Bin-count sensitivity of the ECE improvement factor.** For the two seeds where NLL-scaling activated, the improvement ratio (ECE raw / ECE scaled) is sensitive to $n_{\text{bins}}$: seed 0 gives 2.84× at 5 bins, falling to 1.81× at 20 bins; seed 1 gives 5.91× at 5 bins, falling to 2.19× at 20 bins. The direction of the effect (scaling always improves oracle-ECE) is robust across bin counts, but the reported *magnitude* of improvement is a function of binning resolution and should not be over-interpreted as a precise number — we report it as "roughly 2–6×" rather than the single-seed "4.3×" figure from the original submission.

**Split conformal prediction** hit its 90% target almost exactly regardless of scaling (raw coverage 0.900, set size 4.37/10; scaled coverage 0.900, set size 4.37/10, in the original single-seed run; the invariance argument is deterministic and does not depend on seed), consistent with dividing logits by a positive scalar preserving token-probability rank order, which split conformal's quantile construction is invariant to.

**Ablation over training budget.** Retraining from scratch at five step budgets (same seed) shows KL-to-truth falling monotonically with training (0.61 at 50 steps to 0.046 at 1200 steps), while the calibration/fidelity dissociation is a *late-training* phenomenon: at 50–300 steps grid search selects $T^*=1$ (no correction helps, raw and scaled columns are identical), and only once the model is well-trained enough to be measurably overconfident (600+ steps) does scaling do anything — improving oracle-ECE 4.7× at 1200 steps while KL fidelity worsens 14%. This matches the multi-seed finding at 600 steps that the effect is present but seed-dependent in whether it *triggers* — consistent with it being tied to how overconfident the specific trained model happens to be, not a guaranteed property of any model at that step count.

**Limitations.** This remains a small, fully synthetic testbed (10-token vocabulary, 3 modes, one architecture, single-scalar temperature scaling, one conformal score function) chosen to fit a CPU, minutes-scale budget with no internet access. The 5-seed replication substantially strengthens the main-run claim over the original single-seed result but is still a modest sample; the training-budget ablation remains single-seed. We did not explore multi-parameter recalibration (vector/matrix scaling) or verbalized confidence. The result should be read as an existence proof — temperature scaling *can* trade distributional fidelity for reportable calibration, and the two natural fitting objectives (NLL vs. KL) genuinely conflict rather than one simply being a worse implementation of the other — not as a claim about the average-case magnitude in production LLMs.

## 4. Conclusion

We built a synthetic environment where the ground-truth predictive distribution of a sequence model is known exactly, letting us evaluate LLM calibration methods against truth rather than sampled proxies. Across 5 seeds, temperature scaling fit by validation NLL — the default LLM calibration recipe — improved oracle-ECE by roughly 2–6× whenever it activated, but simultaneously moved the model's full predictive distribution further from the true generative process; it activated in 2/5 seeds at our main training budget and became reliable only once the underlying model was well-trained enough to be overconfident. We additionally show the dissociation is not an artifact of the NLL objective specifically: the scalar temperature that best restores distributional fidelity ($T^*_{\text{KL}}\approx0.92$) sits on the opposite side of $T{=}1$ from the one that best matches observed-token likelihood ($T^*_{\text{NLL}}\approx1.02$), and optimizing for one measurably damages the other — a genuine two-objective conflict, not a fixable mis-specification. Split conformal prediction achieved its coverage guarantee independent of this trade-off, consistent with its rank-invariance. Practically, when downstream use of an LLM's probabilities depends on the *shape* of the distribution (diverse sampling, uncertainty over reasoning branches, entropy-based routing) rather than just top-1 confidence, temperature scaling's NLL-based fitting objective should not be assumed harmless, and coverage-based methods may be a safer default. Future work: extend to matrix/vector scaling and verbalized confidence, use richer generative processes (e.g., context-free grammars) to test whether the dissociation grows with task complexity, and check whether it appears with pretrained LLMs on tasks with partially-known structure.
