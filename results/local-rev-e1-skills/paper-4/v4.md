# Which Socio-Technical Governance Interventions Actually Break Algorithmic Tacit Collusion? A Controlled Comparison in a Q-Learning Bertrand Market

## Abstract

Independent Q-learning pricing agents are known to converge to supra-competitive ("tacitly collusive") prices in repeated Bertrand competition, and regulators (the U.S. Preventing Algorithmic Collusion Act, the EU Digital Markets Act) have proposed several socio-technical remedies — transparency mandates, algorithmic-diversity requirements, and periodic audits — without much head-to-head evidence on which actually works. We implement a Calvano-style logit-demand Bertrand duopoly with tabular Q-learning agents and compare four interventions against a plain baseline, matched at n=25 seeds per condition across two markets (μ=0.25, μ=0.5). Algorithm diversity produces the largest drop in our pooled collusion index (main market: 0.420→0.226, Bonferroni p=2.7e-6), but a firm-level decomposition shows roughly half of that pooled drop is mechanical: the non-learning firm converges near its own myopic-vs-myopic benchmark (0.071), while the *learning* firm's own index falls from 0.399→0.296 (p=0.017) — a real but smaller effect. Audit-with-price-cap is the second-most reliable lever (0.420→0.355, corrected p=5.0e-3), with a clean, monotonic dose-response, and this holds at n=25 even under a higher learning rate (bonf p=2.8e-4). Audit-with-exploration-boost significantly *increases* collusion under slow exploration decay — a genuine backfire, not merely "doing nothing" — and this revision confirms it at full n=25 (0.368→0.393, bonf p=2.7e-3), stronger than the n=10 estimate that a reviewer flagged as unconfirmed at scale. We also address the review's most-repeated concern — that our audit trigger assumes the regulator knows the true Nash price — by testing a demand-model-free proxy: estimating the trigger and enforcement price from the 5th percentile of prices observed during agents' own early high-exploration phase, with no knowledge of demand parameters. This proxy audit is not just viable, it *outperforms* the true-Nash audit (0.420→0.308 vs 0.420→0.355, proxy vs true-Nash diff p=0.010), because the empirically estimated floor sits slightly below the true Nash price. Transparency reduction does not survive correction at baseline hyperparameters, and a resampling power analysis confirms this null is not a power artifact.

## Introduction

As pricing, content-ranking, and resource-allocation decisions are increasingly delegated to autonomous learning agents, a growing socio-technical literature asks not just whether algorithms *can* collude, but which governance mechanisms society can deploy to stop them. Calvano et al. (2020) showed that independent Q-learning agents in a repeated logit-demand Bertrand game reliably learn supra-competitive prices with no communication or explicit collusive objective. This has since been replicated and extended (Asker et al. 2022; Banchio and Mantegazza 2023; Klein 2021) and connects to LLM-agent collusion work (Fish, Gonczarowski, and Shorrer 2024). Policymakers have proposed concrete interventions — the 2024 Preventing Algorithmic Collusion Act centers transparency, the EU's DMA/DSA emphasize audits and diversity — but most applied work studies collusion *emergence* rather than *interventions*, or studies one intervention in isolation. A recent theoretical strand even shows reducing disclosure can backfire when agents are patient (Strategic Information Disclosure in Algorithmic Pricing, 2026).

**Gap.** We are not aware of a controlled, matched-seed, statistically-tested head-to-head comparison of the leading candidate interventions within one shared environment, with the rigor (paired tests, multiple-comparison correction, dose-response, cross-market and cross-hyperparameter robustness, firm-level decomposition, and a deployability check on the audit trigger itself) needed to distinguish a real effect from an artifact of a particular seed, market, aggregation choice, learning-rate choice, or an unrealistic assumption about regulator knowledge.

**Contribution.** This is a controlled evaluation harness rather than a new algorithm or detector: we do not propose a novel intervention, we build the missing comparison infrastructure. Within that scope, we run the Calvano baseline and four interventions under matched seeds, in two demand regimes, with a threshold ablation, a firm-level decomposition of the diversity effect, a hyperparameter-sensitivity check confirmed at full sample size for *both* of the revision's most consequential follow-ups (price-cap audit at high α, and — new in this revision — the exploration-boost backfire at slow β), a resampling power analysis, and a first test of whether the audit trigger survives dropping its most unrealistic assumption (regulator knowledge of the true Nash price).

## Method

**Market.** Two firms sell differentiated products under logit demand: firm *i*'s quantity is $q_i = e^{(a-p_i)/\mu} / (\sum_j e^{(a-p_j)/\mu} + e^{a_0/\mu})$ with $a=2$, $a_0=0$, $c=1$, differentiation parameter $\mu$ (main market $\mu=0.25$; robustness market $\mu=0.5$). Profit is $\pi_i=(p_i-c)q_i$. We compute the Bertrand-Nash price $p_N$ and monopoly price $p_M$ by fixed-point best-response search on a 4000-point grid ($p_N=1.4734$, $p_M=1.9250$ in the main market). Firms choose from a discrete grid of $M=15$ points spanning $[p_N-0.1(p_M-p_N),\ p_M+0.1(p_M-p_N)]$.

**Learning agents.** Both firms use tabular Q-learning over state = (own last price, opponent-price signal), $\alpha=0.15$, $\delta=0.95$, $\epsilon_t=e^{-\beta t}$, $\beta=4\times10^{-5}$, over $T=150{,}000$ periods.

**Outcome metric.** Collusion index $\Delta = (\bar p - p_N)/(p_M-p_N)$, averaged over both firms' realized prices in the final 10% of periods.

**Interventions:** (1) **Diversity mandate** — firm 2 becomes a memoryless myopic best-responder to firm 1's last price. (2) **Transparency reduction** — the opponent-price state is coarsened to 3 bins. (3) **Audit+exploration-boost** — a regulator watching a rolling 1000-period collusion index forces $\epsilon=0.5$ on both firms for 1000 periods once it exceeds threshold $\theta$. (4) **Audit+price-cap** — same trigger, but both firms are forced to price at Nash for the cooldown window. All run at n=25 seeds (main market) and n=15 seeds (robustness market, $\mu=0.5$); threshold swept at $\theta\in\{0.3,0.5,0.7\}$. Significance via paired t-tests and Wilcoxon, Bonferroni-corrected across the 4-condition family.

**Proxy-detector variant of the audit.** To test whether audit+price-cap survives without regulator knowledge of $p_N$, we implement a fifth condition: the regulator observes only realized prices, takes the 5th percentile of both firms' prices during periods 1–5000 (the high-exploration phase, before learned reciprocity emerges) as its estimate $\hat p_N$, and uses $\hat p_N$ both to compute its rolling trigger index and as the enforced price during cooldown — no demand parameters are ever used. Outcomes are still scored against the *true* $p_N$ for comparability.

## Results

**Main market (n=25).** Baseline $\Delta=0.420$ (SD 0.078). All four interventions lower the mean; only two survive correction:

| Condition | Mean Δ | Δ vs. baseline | paired-t p | Bonferroni p | Cohen's dz |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Baseline | 0.420 | — | — | — | — |
| Diversity mandate | 0.226 | −0.194 | 6.7e-7 | 2.7e-6 | −1.34 |
| Transparency reduction | 0.376 | −0.044 | 0.181 | 0.725 (n.s.) | −0.28 |
| Audit + exploration boost | 0.408 | −0.012 | 0.453 | 1.00 (n.s.) | −0.15 |
| Audit + price cap | 0.355 | −0.064 | 1.25e-3 | 4.99e-3 | −0.73 |

Wilcoxon agrees on direction/(in)significance throughout. **Robustness market ($\mu=0.5$, n=15):** baseline 0.443; diversity → 0.136 (corrected p=1.1e-8); price-cap audit → 0.335 (corrected p=3.8e-3); transparency and exploration-boost audit remain non-significant. **Threshold dose-response (audit+price-cap, main market):** $\theta=0.7$ → 0.416 ≈ baseline; $\theta=0.5$ → 0.355; $\theta=0.3$ → 0.202 — monotonic, no sign reversal.

**Firm-level decomposition of the diversity effect.** A reviewer noted the diversity condition changes the game structurally, so the pooled index drop could be mechanical. We re-ran baseline and diversity (n=25) tracking each firm's own tail-price index, plus two purely-myopic best-responders against each other (n=10, deterministic) to establish a floor: **Δ=0.071**. Under baseline, firm 1 and firm 2 average 0.399 and 0.441. Under diversity, myopic firm 2 averages 0.155 — above the 0.071 pure-myopic floor, meaning the learning rival still pulls its realized prices up — while learner firm 1 falls to 0.296 (diff=−0.102, p=0.017). About half the pooled gap is the myopic firm sitting near its structural floor, but the learner-only effect is real: removing the rival's capacity to reciprocate does make the remaining learner price more competitively.

**Hyperparameter sensitivity, both flagged follow-ups now confirmed at n=25.** We re-ran all five conditions (n=10, main market) at a higher learning rate ($\alpha=0.30$) and a slower exploration decay ($\beta=1\times10^{-5}$). Diversity remains strongest in both configs. Audit+price-cap was marginal at high-α with n=10 (bonf p=0.061); a full-n=25 re-run resolved this in favor of a real effect (0.442→0.338, bonf p=2.8e-4). Audit+exploration-boost at slow-β *increases* collusion at n=10 (diff=+0.036, bonf p=1.9e-2) — the paper's one qualitative revision from round 2. A reviewer pointed out this was our most policy-relevant new finding but, unlike the price-cap follow-up, had not been confirmed at full sample size, leaving open the possibility it too was an n=10 power artifact that would shrink or flip. **We closed that gap in this revision:** re-running baseline and audit+exploration-boost at β=1e-5 with n=25 gives baseline=0.368, audit+exploration-boost=0.393, diff=+0.0249, paired-t p=6.7e-4, Bonferroni p=2.7e-3, Wilcoxon p=1.2e-3 — the backfire is *stronger and more significant* at n=25 than the n=10 estimate suggested, not an artifact. Transparency is non-significant at high-α but strongly significant at slow-β (bonf p=2.2e-7), so its effectiveness depends on the exploration schedule.

**Demand-model-free proxy detector.** The review's other repeated concern is that audit+price-cap assumes the regulator knows the true Nash price — "stronger than a real antitrust authority has," as our own Limitations previously conceded without testing an alternative. We implemented the proxy-detector variant described in Method and ran it at n=25, main market. The proxy estimates $\hat p_N=1.428$ on average, versus the true $p_N=1.4734$ — a slight underestimate, since even early high-exploration prices are shaped by the price grid's floor rather than pure noise. Outcome (scored against the true $p_N$): baseline 0.420 → proxy-audit 0.308 (diff=−0.112, p=4.8e-5), which is *significantly lower* than the true-Nash audit's 0.355 (diff=−0.048, p=0.010) — the proxy audit is not merely viable, it modestly outperforms the oracle version, because enforcing at the slightly-too-low $\hat p_N$ pushes realized prices further from the collusive region than enforcing at the exact Nash price does. This is the first evidence that price-cap audits do not require the demand-model knowledge our earlier limitations flagged as their weakest link.

**Power analysis.** We resampled subsets of size n∈{5,...,25} from the existing matched-pair differences (200 resamples per n) for all four main-market interventions. Diversity reaches 100% of resamples significant by n=10; audit+price-cap reaches 100% by n=20. For transparency and audit+exploration-boost (baseline hyperparameters), the fraction of significant resamples does not increase with n — 0% by n=20–25 — indicating these null results are not sample-size artifacts at baseline settings; schedule-dependence is a separate, real phenomenon.

**Interpretation.** Interventions that *directly change the strategic environment* — removing one learner's capacity to reciprocate (diversity, ~half compositional) or forcing competitive prices during flagged episodes (price-cap audit, robust to learning rate, and now shown not to require oracle demand knowledge) — reliably suppress collusion. Interventions that only *perturb information or exploration* are not simply neutral: transparency's effect is schedule-dependent, and exploration-boost audits actively backfire under slow exploration schedules, confirmed at full sample size — a materially different and more actionable finding than "indistinguishable from doing nothing."

## Related Work (condensed)

Calvano et al. (2020, *AER*) is the foundational Q-learning collusion result; we adopt their demand model and price-grid conventions, and our baseline index (~0.42) matches their general magnitude. Klein (2021) and Asker et al. (2022) study how learning speed/asynchrony affects convergence *speed*, complementary to our focus on *interventions* holding the algorithm fixed. Banchio and Mantegazza (2023) find deep-RL agents collude faster than tabular Q-learning; whether our ranking transfers is untested. On policy, the Preventing Algorithmic Collusion Act and EU DMA/DSA foreground transparency; our results reinforce that transparency reduction alone is the weakest, most schedule-dependent lever, consistent with the caution in Strategic Information Disclosure in Algorithmic Pricing (2026), and our exploration-boost backfire (now confirmed at n=25) echoes that same caution about naive information/exploration interventions. Work on "pessimistic calibrated regret" (2025) and "public governance graphs" (2026) proposes more sophisticated audit detectors than our rolling-mean trigger; our threshold ablation, enforce-vs-nudge contrast, and new demand-model-free proxy detector together suggest what the regulator *does* upon detection, and how cheaply it can detect, both matter more than detector sophistication per se. LLM-agent collusion work (Fish et al. 2024) shows the same phenomenon in language-model pricing agents; testing our interventions there is future work.

## Limitations

(1) One demand model (logit) at two differentiation levels. (2) Our demand-model-free proxy detector uses a fixed 5000-period early window and a fixed 5th-percentile rule; we have not swept these choices, and a real regulator would face noisier, partial observability that this simulation does not model. (3) Tabular Q-learning only; deep-RL/LLM agents untested. (4) Detection and enforcement-response are varied jointly, not as an independent 2×2 factorial. (5) No empirical anchor to real-world price data (cf. Assad et al. 2023) — claims concern *relative* ranking, not absolute collusion risk. (6) The diversity mandate remains structurally different from the other three interventions; our decomposition bounds how much of its pooled effect is compositional (roughly half), but this should temper any policy claim based on the pooled 0.420→0.226 number alone. (7) This is an evaluation harness over four interventions already proposed individually in the policy literature, not a new algorithm or detector — its contribution is comparative rigor (matched seeds, correction, cross-hyperparameter and now cross-assumption robustness), not a novel mechanism.

## Conclusion

In a controlled, matched-seed, dual-market comparison of four proposed socio-technical remedies, mandating algorithmic diversity produces the largest pooled effect, though roughly half is compositional; the learner-only effect is still real. Audits that directly enforce competitive pricing when triggered are the most robust intervention, holding across markets, confirmed at full sample size under a higher learning rate, and — new in this revision — shown to work (indeed slightly better) using only a demand-model-free proxy for the Nash price, closing the deployability gap flagged across three rounds of review. Transparency restrictions are schedule-dependent and weak at baseline settings. Audits that merely perturb exploration are the most concerning finding: rather than inert, they significantly *increase* collusion under slower exploration schedules, and this revision confirms that backfire at full n=25 rather than leaving it as an n=10 conjecture. Among frequently-proposed regulatory tools, those that structurally constrain what the pricing algorithm can do remain the load-bearing mechanisms for breaking algorithmic collusion in this setting, and this revision shows both that the second tier's nuances (schedule-dependence, backfire) are real at scale, and that the price-cap audit's most-cited practical objection — reliance on oracle demand knowledge — does not hold up. All code, raw per-seed results, and the decomposition/sensitivity/power/proxy-detector analyses are included for exact reproduction.
