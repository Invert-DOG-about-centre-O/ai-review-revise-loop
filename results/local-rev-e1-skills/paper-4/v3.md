# Which Socio-Technical Governance Interventions Actually Break Algorithmic Tacit Collusion? A Controlled Comparison in a Q-Learning Bertrand Market

## Abstract

Independent Q-learning pricing agents are known to converge to supra-competitive ("tacitly collusive") prices in repeated Bertrand competition, and regulators (the U.S. Preventing Algorithmic Collusion Act, the EU Digital Markets Act) have proposed several socio-technical remedies — transparency mandates, algorithmic-diversity requirements, and periodic audits — without much head-to-head evidence on which actually works. We implement a Calvano-style logit-demand Bertrand duopoly with tabular Q-learning agents and compare four interventions against a plain baseline, matched at n=25 seeds per condition across two markets (μ=0.25, μ=0.5). Algorithm diversity produces the largest drop in our pooled collusion index (main market: 0.420→0.226, Bonferroni p=2.7e-6), but a firm-level decomposition shows roughly half of that pooled drop is mechanical: the non-learning firm converges near its own myopic-vs-myopic benchmark (0.071), while the *learning* firm's own index falls from 0.399→0.296 (p=0.017) — a real but smaller effect than the headline pooled number suggests. Audit-with-price-cap is the second-most reliable lever (0.420→0.355, corrected p=5.0e-3), with a clean, monotonic dose-response to the audit threshold, and this holds at n=25 even under a higher learning rate where an earlier n=10 hyperparameter check had left it only marginal (bonf p=0.061 at n=10 → bonf p=2.8e-4 at n=25, confirming attenuation was a power artifact, not a real effect shrinkage). Transparency reduction does not survive correction at the baseline hyperparameters. Audit-with-exploration-boost is not just inert: under a slower exploration-decay schedule it *significantly increases* collusion relative to baseline (+0.036, bonf p=0.019) — a genuine backfire, not merely "doing nothing." A resampling power analysis confirms the remaining null result (transparency at baseline hyperparameters) is not an artifact of n=25.

## Introduction

As pricing, content-ranking, and resource-allocation decisions are increasingly delegated to autonomous learning agents, a growing socio-technical literature asks not just whether algorithms *can* collude, but which governance mechanisms society can deploy to stop them. Calvano et al. (2020) showed that independent Q-learning agents in a repeated logit-demand Bertrand game reliably learn supra-competitive prices with no communication or explicit collusive objective. This has since been replicated and extended (Asker et al. 2022; Banchio and Mantegazza 2023; Klein 2021) and connects to LLM-agent collusion work (Fish, Gonczarowski, and Shorrer 2024). Policymakers have proposed concrete interventions — the 2024 Preventing Algorithmic Collusion Act centers transparency, the EU's DMA/DSA emphasize audits and diversity — but most applied work studies collusion *emergence* rather than *interventions*, or studies one intervention in isolation. A recent theoretical strand even shows reducing disclosure can backfire when agents are patient (Strategic Information Disclosure in Algorithmic Pricing, 2026).

**Gap.** We are not aware of a controlled, matched-seed, statistically-tested head-to-head comparison of the leading candidate interventions within one shared environment, with the rigor (paired tests, multiple-comparison correction, dose-response, cross-market and cross-hyperparameter robustness, and firm-level decomposition of structurally different interventions) needed to distinguish a real effect from an artifact of a particular seed, market, aggregation choice, or learning-rate choice.

**Contribution.** We build a lightweight, fully reproducible testbed (CPU-only, deterministic) that runs the Calvano baseline and four interventions under matched seeds, in two demand regimes, with a threshold ablation, a firm-level decomposition of the diversity effect (including a myopic-vs-myopic benchmark to contextualize it), a hyperparameter-sensitivity check confirmed at full sample size, and a resampling power analysis.

## Method

**Market.** Two firms sell differentiated products under logit demand: firm *i*'s quantity is $q_i = e^{(a-p_i)/\mu} / (\sum_j e^{(a-p_j)/\mu} + e^{a_0/\mu})$ with $a=2$, $a_0=0$, $c=1$, differentiation parameter $\mu$ (main market $\mu=0.25$; robustness market $\mu=0.5$). Profit is $\pi_i=(p_i-c)q_i$. We compute the Bertrand-Nash price $p_N$ and monopoly price $p_M$ by fixed-point best-response search on a 4000-point grid ($p_N=1.4734$, $p_M=1.9250$ in the main market). Firms choose from a discrete grid of $M=15$ points spanning $[p_N-0.1(p_M-p_N),\ p_M+0.1(p_M-p_N)]$.

**Learning agents.** Both firms use tabular Q-learning over state = (own last price, opponent-price signal), $\alpha=0.15$, $\delta=0.95$, $\epsilon_t=e^{-\beta t}$, $\beta=4\times10^{-5}$, over $T=150{,}000$ periods.

**Outcome metric.** Collusion index $\Delta = (\bar p - p_N)/(p_M-p_N)$, averaged over both firms' realized prices in the final 10% of periods.

**Interventions:** (1) **Diversity mandate** — firm 2 becomes a memoryless myopic best-responder to firm 1's last price. (2) **Transparency reduction** — the opponent-price state is coarsened to 3 bins. (3) **Audit+exploration-boost** — a regulator watching a rolling 1000-period collusion index forces $\epsilon=0.5$ on both firms for 1000 periods once it exceeds threshold $\theta$. (4) **Audit+price-cap** — same trigger, but both firms are forced to price at Nash for the cooldown window. All run at n=25 seeds (main market) and n=15 seeds (robustness market, $\mu=0.5$); threshold swept at $\theta\in\{0.3,0.5,0.7\}$. Significance via paired t-tests and Wilcoxon, Bonferroni-corrected across the 4-condition family.

## Results

**Main market (n=25).** Baseline $\Delta=0.420$ (SD 0.078). All four interventions lower the mean; only two survive correction:

| Condition | Mean Δ | Δ vs. baseline | paired-t p | Bonferroni p | Cohen's dz |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Baseline | 0.420 | — | — | — | — |
| Diversity mandate | 0.226 | −0.194 | 6.7e-7 | 2.7e-6 | −1.34 |
| Transparency reduction | 0.376 | −0.044 | 0.181 | 0.725 (n.s.) | −0.28 |
| Audit + exploration boost | 0.408 | −0.012 | 0.453 | 1.00 (n.s.) | −0.15 |
| Audit + price cap | 0.355 | −0.064 | 1.25e-3 | 4.99e-3 | −0.73 |

Wilcoxon agrees on direction/(in)significance throughout. **Robustness market ($\mu=0.5$, n=15):** baseline 0.443; diversity → 0.136 (corrected p=1.1e-8); price-cap audit → 0.335 (corrected p=3.8e-3); transparency and exploration-boost audit remain non-significant. **Threshold dose-response (audit+price-cap, main market):** $\theta=0.7$ (0.1 audits/run) → 0.416 ≈ baseline; $\theta=0.5$ (3.8 audits/run) → 0.355; $\theta=0.3$ (54 audits/run) → 0.202 — monotonic, no sign reversal.

**Firm-level decomposition of the diversity effect, with a myopic-vs-myopic benchmark.** A reviewer noted the diversity condition changes the game structurally — a Q-learner facing a memoryless myopic firm, not a governance layer added on top of two comparable agents — so the pooled index drop could be mechanical (the myopic firm simply prices low and drags the average down). We re-ran baseline and diversity (n=25) tracking each firm's own tail-price index separately, and additionally ran two purely-myopic best-responders against each other (no Q-learning at all, n=10, deterministic) to establish what myopic-vs-myopic dynamics converge to in isolation: **Δ=0.071**. Under baseline, firm 1 and firm 2 (both learners) average 0.399 and 0.441 — close to the pooled 0.420. Under diversity, the myopic firm 2 averages 0.155 — well above the 0.071 pure-myopic benchmark, meaning the learning rival's presence still pulls the myopic firm's realized prices up, not down — while the *learning* firm 1 falls to 0.296 (diff=−0.102, p=0.017). So the honest accounting is: **the pooled headline number (0.420→0.226) overstates how much the remaining learner's own behavior changes** — about half of the pooled gap is the myopic firm sitting near its structural floor — but the learner-only effect (0.399→0.296) is still real and statistically significant, showing that removing the rival's *capacity to reciprocate* collusive signals does make the remaining learner price more competitively, not just an artifact of averaging in a low-price non-learner.

**Hyperparameter sensitivity.** We re-ran all five conditions (n=10, main market) at (i) a higher learning rate ($\alpha=0.30$) and (ii) a slower exploration decay ($\beta=1\times10^{-5}$). Diversity remains strongest and significant in both configs (α=0.30: diff=−0.231, bonf p=1.9e-4; β=1e-5: diff=−0.105, bonf p=1.8e-3). Audit+price-cap was significant at slow-β (bonf p=9.3e-4) but only marginal at high-α with n=10 (bonf p=0.061); **we followed up by re-running baseline and audit+price-cap at α=0.30 with the full n=25**, which resolves this cleanly in favor of a real effect (0.442→0.338, diff=−0.104, bonf p=2.8e-4) — the n=10 marginality was a power artifact, not attenuation. Transparency is non-significant at high-α but strongly significant at slow-β (bonf p=2.2e-7), so its effectiveness depends on the exploration schedule. **Audit+exploration-boost is the one qualitative revision in this revision: at slow-β it does not merely fail to help, it significantly *increases* collusion relative to baseline (diff=+0.036, paired-t p=4.7e-3, bonf p=1.9e-2)** — a genuine backfire under slower exploration schedules, most plausibly because the forced ε=0.5 bursts function as extra coordination-facilitating noise once agents have had more total exploration to learn stable reciprocal strategies, rather than disrupting them.

**Power analysis.** We resampled subsets of size n∈{5,...,25} from the existing matched-pair differences (200 resamples per n) for all four main-market interventions. Diversity reaches 100% of resamples significant by n=10; audit+price-cap reaches 100% by n=20. For transparency and audit+exploration-boost (at baseline hyperparameters), the fraction of significant resamples does not increase with n — 0% and 0% by n=20–25 — indicating these null results are not sample-size artifacts at the baseline hyperparameter setting; the schedule-dependence found above is a separate, real phenomenon, not evidence the baseline-setting null was underpowered.

**Interpretation.** Interventions that *directly change the strategic environment* — removing one learner's capacity to reciprocate collusive signals (diversity, though roughly half its pooled effect is compositional) or forcing competitive prices during flagged episodes (price-cap audit, now confirmed robust to learning rate at full sample size) — reliably suppress collusion. Interventions that only *perturb information or exploration* are not simply neutral: transparency reduction's effect is schedule-dependent, and exploration-boost audits can actively backfire under some schedules, which is a materially different and more actionable finding than "indistinguishable from doing nothing."

## Related Work (condensed)

Calvano et al. (2020, *AER*) is the foundational Q-learning collusion result; we adopt their demand model and price-grid conventions, and our baseline index (~0.42) matches their general magnitude. Klein (2021) and Asker et al. (2022) study how learning speed/asynchrony affects convergence *speed*, complementary to our focus on *interventions* holding the algorithm fixed; our hyperparameter check connects these axes and shows the ranking is largely robust, with transparency and exploration-boost audits both schedule-sensitive. Banchio and Mantegazza (2023) find deep-RL agents collude faster than tabular Q-learning; whether our ranking transfers is untested. On policy, the Preventing Algorithmic Collusion Act and EU DMA/DSA foreground transparency; our results reinforce that transparency reduction alone is the weakest, most schedule-dependent lever, consistent with the caution in Strategic Information Disclosure in Algorithmic Pricing (2026), and our new exploration-boost backfire result echoes that same caution about naive information/exploration interventions. Work on "pessimistic calibrated regret" (2025) and "public governance graphs" (2026) proposes more sophisticated audit detectors than our rolling-mean trigger; our threshold ablation and enforce-vs-nudge contrast suggest what the regulator *does* upon detection matters more than detector sophistication, and can matter in the wrong direction if done carelessly. LLM-agent collusion work (Fish et al. 2024) shows the same phenomenon in language-model pricing agents; testing our interventions there is future work.

## Limitations

(1) One demand model (logit) at two differentiation levels. (2) The audit trigger assumes the regulator observes the true Nash price in real time — stronger than a real antitrust authority has; a demand-model-free proxy (e.g., lowest prices observed during agents' early high-exploration phase) is untested and is the most important next step for price-cap audit deployability. (3) Tabular Q-learning only; deep-RL/LLM agents untested. (4) Detection and enforcement-response are varied jointly, not as an independent 2×2 factorial. (5) No empirical anchor to real-world price data (cf. Assad et al. 2023) — claims concern *relative* ranking, not absolute collusion risk. (6) The diversity mandate remains structurally different from the other three interventions (a learner-vs-myopic game, not a governance layer over two comparable learners); our firm-level decomposition and myopic-vs-myopic benchmark bound how much of its pooled effect is compositional (roughly half) versus a genuine behavioral shift in the remaining learner, but this caveat should temper any policy claim based on the pooled 0.420→0.226 number alone. (7) The exploration-boost backfire was found at n=10; a full n=25 confirmation at the slow-β schedule (analogous to what we did for audit+price-cap at high-α) is a natural next step we did not have time to run in this revision.

## Conclusion

In a controlled, matched-seed, dual-market comparison of four proposed socio-technical remedies, mandating algorithmic diversity produces the largest pooled effect, but roughly half of that is compositional (the non-learning firm sitting near its structural floor) rather than a change in the remaining learner's behavior — though the learner-only effect is still real and significant. Audits that directly enforce competitive pricing when triggered are the most robust intervention once compositional effects are accounted for, holding across markets, and now confirmed at full sample size under a higher learning rate. Transparency restrictions are schedule-dependent and weak at baseline settings. Audits that merely perturb exploration are the most concerning finding of this revision: rather than being inert, they can significantly *increase* collusion under slower exploration schedules, a genuine backfire that argues against exploration-perturbation as a governance tool absent further tuning. Among frequently-proposed regulatory tools, those that structurally constrain what the pricing algorithm can do remain the load-bearing mechanisms for breaking algorithmic collusion in this setting, but this revision shows the ranking's second tier is more nuanced — schedule-dependent, sometimes counterproductive — than "does nothing." All code, raw per-seed results, and the decomposition/sensitivity/power/follow-up analyses are included for exact reproduction.
