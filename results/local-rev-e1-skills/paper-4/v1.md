# Which Socio-Technical Governance Interventions Actually Break Algorithmic Tacit Collusion? A Controlled Comparison in a Q-Learning Bertrand Market

## Abstract

Independent Q-learning pricing agents are known to converge to supra-competitive ("tacitly collusive") prices in repeated Bertrand competition, and regulators (the U.S. Preventing Algorithmic Collusion Act, the EU Digital Markets Act) have proposed several socio-technical remedies — transparency mandates, algorithmic-diversity requirements, and periodic audits — without much head-to-head evidence on which actually works. We implement a Calvano-style logit-demand Bertrand duopoly with tabular Q-learning agents and compare four interventions against a plain baseline, matched at n=25 seeds per condition across two structurally different markets (differentiation parameter μ=0.25 and μ=0.5): (1) an **algorithm-diversity mandate** (one firm learns, the other is a static myopic best-responder), (2) a **transparency-reduction** mandate (agents observe only a coarse 3-bin signal of the rival's price instead of its exact value), (3) an **audit-with-exploration-boost** intervention (a regulator detects sustained supra-competitive pricing and forces temporary extra exploration), and (4) an **audit-with-price-cap** intervention (the regulator instead forces both firms to price at the Nash level for a cooldown window). Algorithm diversity produces the largest, most robust drop in our collusion index (main market: 0.420→0.226, paired t p=6.7e-7, Bonferroni-corrected across the 4-condition family p=2.7e-6, dz=−1.34; robustness market: p=1.1e-8). Audit-with-price-cap also significantly reduces collusion in both markets (main: 0.420→0.355, p=1.25e-3, corrected p=5.0e-3, dz=−0.73) and shows a clean, monotonic dose-response to the audit threshold (index 0.202/0.355/0.416 as the trigger threshold tightens from 0.7 to 0.5 to 0.3, tracking the number of triggered audits from 0.1 to 3.8 to 54 per run). Transparency reduction and audit-with-exploration-boost produce weaker effects that do **not** survive Bonferroni correction in either market. The exploration-boost audit is statistically indistinguishable from doing nothing, indicating that regulatory audits deter collusion only when they force compliance (price caps), not when they merely perturb learning.

## Introduction

As pricing, content-ranking, and resource-allocation decisions are increasingly delegated to autonomous learning agents, a growing socio-technical literature asks not just whether algorithms *can* collude, but which governance mechanisms society can deploy to stop them. Calvano et al. (2020) showed that independent Q-learning agents in a repeated logit-demand Bertrand game reliably learn supra-competitive prices with no communication or explicit collusive objective — a canonical example of an emergent, harmful multi-agent equilibrium that arises purely from the interaction of simple learning rules and market structure. This result has since been replicated and extended across richer algorithms and market settings (Asker et al. 2022; Banchio and Mantegazza 2023; Johnson et al. 2023) and connects to the wider literature on tacit collusion between LLM-based pricing agents (e.g. Fish, Gonczarowski, and Shorrer 2024, and follow-on work on LLM market-division and construction-bidding collusion), which finds the same qualitative phenomenon in modern language-model agents. In parallel, policymakers have proposed concrete socio-technical interventions: the 2024 Preventing Algorithmic Collusion Act centers transparency requirements, the EU's DMA/DSA emphasize algorithmic audits and diversity, and antitrust scholarship (OECD 2017; more recent work on regulation via calibrated regret and public governance graphs) has debated mandating algorithmic diversity or restricting information disclosure. However, most of this applied work either (a) studies collusion *emergence* itself rather than interventions, or (b) studies a single intervention in isolation. A recent theoretical strand (Strategic Information Disclosure in Algorithmic Pricing, 2026) even shows that *reducing* disclosure can sometimes backfire and strengthen collusion when agents are patient — underscoring that intuitions about which socio-technical levers help are not reliable without direct comparison.

**Gap.** We are not aware of a controlled, matched-seed, statistically-tested head-to-head comparison of the leading candidate interventions — diversity mandates, transparency restriction, and audit mechanisms — within one shared environment, evaluated with the rigor (paired tests, multiple-comparison correction, dose-response checks, cross-market robustness) needed to distinguish a real effect from an artifact of a particular random seed or market parameterization. This is exactly the sort of question the "socio-technical AI" framing is meant to address: the phenomenon (collusion) is purely algorithmic, but the candidate fixes are institutional/regulatory design choices layered on top of the algorithm, and their relative effectiveness is an empirical, not a purely theoretical, question.

**Contribution.** We build a lightweight, fully reproducible testbed (CPU-only, deterministic, <6 minutes total runtime) that runs the Calvano baseline and four interventions under matched seeds, in two demand regimes, with a threshold ablation for the best-performing audit variant, and report effect sizes and corrected significance for every comparison.

## Method

**Market.** Two firms sell differentiated products under logit demand: firm *i*'s quantity is $q_i = e^{(a-p_i)/\mu} / (\sum_j e^{(a-p_j)/\mu} + e^{a_0/\mu})$ with quality index $a=2$, outside-option quality $a_0=0$, marginal cost $c=1$, and differentiation parameter $\mu$ (main market $\mu=0.25$; robustness market $\mu=0.5$, i.e. less differentiated / closer to homogeneous Bertrand competition). Firm profit is $\pi_i=(p_i-c)q_i$. We compute the symmetric Bertrand-Nash price $p_N$ and the joint-profit-maximizing monopoly price $p_M$ numerically via fixed-point best-response search on a 4000-point fine grid ($p_N=1.4734$, $p_M=1.9250$ in the main market). Firms choose prices from a discrete grid of $M=15$ points spanning $[p_N-0.1(p_M-p_N),\ p_M+0.1(p_M-p_N)]$, following Calvano et al.'s standard discretization.

**Learning agents.** Both firms use tabular Q-learning over state = (own last price, opponent-price signal), with learning rate $\alpha=0.15$, discount factor $\delta=0.95$, and $\epsilon$-greedy exploration decaying as $\epsilon_t=e^{-\beta t}$, $\beta=4\times10^{-5}$, over $T=150{,}000$ periods. This reproduces the standard Calvano-style setup used across the replication literature.

**Outcome metric.** We report the **collusion index** $\Delta = (\bar p - p_N)/(p_M-p_N)$, averaged over both firms' realized prices in the final 10% of periods (15,000 periods) of each run; $\Delta=0$ is competitive (Nash), $\Delta=1$ is full monopoly collusion.

**Interventions (conditions), each compared against a plain two-Q-learner baseline:**
- **Diversity mandate**: firm 2 is replaced by a non-learning myopic best-responder (recomputes the profit-maximizing price against firm 1's *last* observed price every period, with no memory or adaptation) — modeling a regulatory requirement that competing firms not all deploy adaptive learning algorithms.
- **Transparency reduction**: both firms remain Q-learners, but the opponent-price component of the state is coarsened from the full 15-level grid to 3 bins — modeling a disclosure restriction on real-time competitor pricing data.
- **Audit + exploration boost**: a regulator monitors a rolling 1000-period average of the collusion index; if it exceeds a threshold $\theta$, the regulator forces $\epsilon=0.5$ (elevated exploration) on both firms for the next 1000 periods, then Q-learning resumes normally.
- **Audit + price cap**: identical trigger logic, but instead of boosting exploration, the regulator forces both firms to price *at the Nash level* for the 1000-period cooldown (Q-tables continue to update on the forced actions) — modeling a direct enforcement action (e.g. a temporary price freeze) rather than an indirect nudge.

All four interventions and the baseline are run at $n=25$ matched seeds (0–24) in the main market ($\mu=0.25$) and again at $n=15$ matched seeds in the robustness market ($\mu=0.5$) to check the results are not an artifact of one demand parameterization. We additionally sweep the audit threshold $\theta\in\{0.3,0.5,0.7\}$ for audit+price-cap at the same 25 matched seeds used in the main comparison. Significance is assessed with paired t-tests and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests against the baseline (same seed → same random market history up to the intervention), with Bonferroni correction applied within each market's family of 4 comparisons.

## Results

**Main market ($\mu=0.25$, n=25).** The plain baseline converges to substantial tacit collusion, $\Delta=0.420$ (SD 0.078) — about 42% of the way from competitive to fully monopolistic pricing, consistent with prior Calvano-style replications. All four interventions lower the mean index, but only two survive correction:

| Condition | Mean Δ | Δ vs. baseline | paired-t p | Bonferroni p (k=4) | Cohen's dz |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Baseline | 0.420 | — | — | — | — |
| Diversity mandate | 0.226 | −0.194 | 6.7e-7 | 2.7e-6 | −1.34 |
| Transparency reduction | 0.376 | −0.044 | 0.181 | 0.725 (n.s.) | −0.28 |
| Audit + exploration boost | 0.408 | −0.012 | 0.453 | 1.00 (n.s.) | −0.15 |
| Audit + price cap | 0.355 | −0.064 | 1.25e-3 | 4.99e-3 | −0.73 |

Wilcoxon signed-rank tests agree in direction and (in)significance for every condition (diversity p=1.2e-7; transparency p=0.21; explore-audit p=0.59; price-cap-audit p=4.1e-3).

**Robustness market ($\mu=0.5$, less differentiated, n=15).** The ranking replicates: baseline $\Delta=0.443$ (SD 0.066); diversity mandate drops it to 0.136 (p=2.8e-9, corrected p=1.1e-8); audit+price-cap drops it to 0.335 (p=9.4e-4, corrected p=3.8e-3); transparency reduction (0.386, p=0.039, corrected p=0.157) and audit+exploration-boost (0.453, p=0.603) remain non-significant after correction, with the exploration-boost audit's point estimate actually slightly *above* baseline.

**Threshold dose-response for audit+price-cap (n=25, main market).** Tightening the trigger threshold produces a clean, monotonic dose-response: $\theta=0.7$ (audits almost never fire, mean 0.1 per run) leaves the index at 0.416 — statistically indistinguishable from the no-audit baseline (0.420), a useful sanity check that the mechanism is inert when untriggered; $\theta=0.5$ (mean 3.8 audits/run) yields 0.355; $\theta=0.3$ (mean 54 audits/run, i.e. frequent enforcement) yields 0.202, comparable to the diversity mandate. More frequent, stricter enforcement produces proportionally larger reductions, with no sign reversal at any tested threshold.

**Interpretation.** The two interventions that *directly change the strategic environment* — removing one learner from the interaction entirely (diversity) or directly forcing competitive prices during flagged episodes (price-cap audit) — are the only ones that reliably suppress collusion in our setup. The two interventions that merely *perturb information or exploration* without changing what agents can ultimately settle on — coarsening the opponent-price signal, or transiently boosting exploration without forcing compliant behavior — leave the learned collusive equilibrium largely intact once the perturbation ends; agents re-discover it. This is consistent with recent theoretical results that information-restriction policies can be ineffective or even backfire (Strategic Information Disclosure in Algorithmic Pricing, 2026), and gives an empirical, comparative grounding to that concern.

## Related Work (extended)

Calvano, Calzolari, Denicolò, and Pastorello (2020, *American Economic Review*) is the foundational demonstration that independent Q-learning reaches supra-competitive Bertrand prices without communication; we adopt their demand model, price-grid discretization, and learning-rate/discount conventions directly, and our baseline collusion index (~0.42) is consistent with the general magnitude of collusion (well above zero, well below one) reported across their and follow-up parameterizations, though exact values are not directly comparable across differing grid sizes and horizons. Klein (2021) and Asker, Fershtman, and Pakes (2022) study how asynchronous updating and asymmetric learning speeds affect the *speed and stability* of convergence to collusive outcomes — a complementary axis to ours, which instead compares *governance interventions* holding the base learning algorithm fixed. Banchio and Mantegazza (2023) test more sophisticated policy-gradient/deep-RL pricing algorithms and find they collude faster and more severely than tabular Q-learning; because our interventions target the incentive/information structure of the game rather than a specific algorithm class, we expect our qualitative ranking (diversity, enforcement > information restriction > exploration nudges) to transfer, but did not test it directly — a concrete direction for follow-up work. On the policy side, the 2024 Preventing Algorithmic Collusion Act and EU DMA/DSA both foreground *transparency* as a primary remedy; our results suggest transparency reduction alone is the weakest of the four levers tested, aligning with the theoretical caution in "Strategic Information Disclosure in Algorithmic Pricing" (2026) that disclosure policy can have ambiguous or counterproductive effects depending on agent patience. Work on regulating collusion via "pessimistic calibrated regret" (2025) and "public governance graphs" (2026) proposes more sophisticated audit/regret-based detection mechanisms than our simple rolling-average trigger; our threshold ablation shows that even a simple detector, provided the *response* to detection is enforcement rather than mere perturbation, produces a robust, dose-responsive reduction — suggesting detector sophistication may matter less than what the regulator does once collusion is detected. Finally, LLM-agent collusion work (Fish et al. 2024; construction-bidding and Cournot-market collusion studies, 2025–2026) shows the same phenomenon with modern language-model pricing agents, not just tabular Q-learners; whether our four interventions transfer there is open (LLM API calls were outside our CPU-only, fixed-budget scope), but the mechanism-vs-nudge distinction we identify is a plausible hypothesis to test in that setting.

## Limitations

(1) We test one demand model (logit) at two differentiation levels; other demand systems (linear, CES) are untested. (2) Our audit trigger is a rolling-mean threshold on the collusion index itself, which assumes the regulator can observe true competitive benchmarks — stronger than a real antitrust authority typically has; more realistic detectors (e.g. price-correlation-based, without knowledge of $p_N$) are left to future work. (3) We use tabular Q-learning, the simplest agent class in this literature; whether our ranking holds for deep-RL or LLM-based pricing agents is untested here. (4) Detection and enforcement-response are varied jointly, not as independent factors; a full 2×2 (weak/strong detector × nudge/enforce) design is a natural next experiment. (5) As with all simulation-based collusion studies, mapping the index back to real-world market harm requires an empirical anchor (e.g. calibrating $\mu$ or $\beta$ against retail-market price data, as in Assad et al. 2023's study of German gasoline retail) that we do not attempt; our claims concern the *relative* ranking of interventions within a controlled simulated environment, not the absolute magnitude of real-world collusion risk.

## Conclusion

In a controlled, matched-seed, dual-market comparison of four proposed socio-technical remedies for algorithmic tacit collusion, mandating algorithmic diversity (not all competitors using adaptive learners) is the most effective and robust intervention we test, followed by audits that directly enforce competitive pricing when triggered. Transparency restrictions and audits that merely perturb exploration without enforcing compliance are statistically indistinguishable from doing nothing once multiple-comparison correction is applied, in both markets we tested. This suggests that, among frequently-proposed regulatory levers, the ones that structurally constrain what the pricing algorithm can do — not the ones that merely constrain what it can see or briefly destabilize its exploration — are the load-bearing mechanisms for breaking algorithmic collusion in this setting.
