# Which Socio-Technical Governance Interventions Actually Break Algorithmic Tacit Collusion? A Controlled Comparison in a Q-Learning Bertrand Market

## Abstract

Independent Q-learning pricing agents are known to converge to supra-competitive ("tacitly collusive") prices in repeated Bertrand competition, and regulators (the U.S. Preventing Algorithmic Collusion Act, the EU Digital Markets Act) have proposed several socio-technical remedies — transparency mandates, algorithmic-diversity requirements, and periodic audits — without much head-to-head evidence on which actually works. We implement a Calvano-style logit-demand Bertrand duopoly with tabular Q-learning agents and compare four interventions against a plain baseline, matched at n=25 seeds per condition across two markets (μ=0.25, μ=0.5). Algorithm diversity produces the largest, most robust drop in our collusion index (main market: 0.420→0.226, paired t p=6.7e-7, Bonferroni p=2.7e-6, dz=−1.34). Audit-with-price-cap also significantly reduces collusion (0.420→0.355, p=1.25e-3, corrected p=5.0e-3) with a clean, monotonic dose-response to the audit threshold. Transparency reduction and audit-with-exploration-boost do not survive correction in either market. Because a reviewer flagged that the diversity mandate changes the game structurally (a learner vs. a memoryless myopic firm), we decompose the effect by firm: the learning firm's *own* price index still falls significantly (0.399→0.296, p=0.017) when it faces a myopic rival, showing the effect is not purely mechanical averaging with a low-priced non-learner — though roughly half of the pooled drop does come from the myopic firm's low prices. We also test hyperparameter sensitivity (higher learning rate, slower exploration decay, n=10) and find the qualitative ranking (diversity > enforcement audit > transparency/exploration-nudge) is stable, and a resampling power analysis shows the non-significant conditions are not underpowered — at n=25, near-zero fractions of resamples reach significance even in the best case, so more seeds would not change the conclusion.

## Introduction

As pricing, content-ranking, and resource-allocation decisions are increasingly delegated to autonomous learning agents, a growing socio-technical literature asks not just whether algorithms *can* collude, but which governance mechanisms society can deploy to stop them. Calvano et al. (2020) showed that independent Q-learning agents in a repeated logit-demand Bertrand game reliably learn supra-competitive prices with no communication or explicit collusive objective. This has since been replicated and extended (Asker et al. 2022; Banchio and Mantegazza 2023; Klein 2021) and connects to LLM-agent collusion work (Fish, Gonczarowski, and Shorrer 2024). Policymakers have proposed concrete interventions — the 2024 Preventing Algorithmic Collusion Act centers transparency, the EU's DMA/DSA emphasize audits and diversity — but most applied work studies collusion *emergence* rather than *interventions*, or studies one intervention in isolation. A recent theoretical strand even shows reducing disclosure can backfire when agents are patient (Strategic Information Disclosure in Algorithmic Pricing, 2026).

**Gap.** We are not aware of a controlled, matched-seed, statistically-tested head-to-head comparison of the leading candidate interventions — diversity mandates, transparency restriction, and audit mechanisms — within one shared environment, with the rigor (paired tests, multiple-comparison correction, dose-response, cross-market and cross-hyperparameter robustness) needed to distinguish a real effect from an artifact of a particular seed, market, or learning-rate choice.

**Contribution.** We build a lightweight, fully reproducible testbed (CPU-only, deterministic) that runs the Calvano baseline and four interventions under matched seeds, in two demand regimes, with a threshold ablation, a firm-level decomposition of the diversity effect, a hyperparameter-sensitivity check, and a resampling power analysis, reporting effect sizes and corrected significance throughout.

## Method

**Market.** Two firms sell differentiated products under logit demand: firm *i*'s quantity is $q_i = e^{(a-p_i)/\mu} / (\sum_j e^{(a-p_j)/\mu} + e^{a_0/\mu})$ with $a=2$, $a_0=0$, $c=1$, and differentiation parameter $\mu$ (main market $\mu=0.25$; robustness market $\mu=0.5$). Profit is $\pi_i=(p_i-c)q_i$. We compute the Bertrand-Nash price $p_N$ and monopoly price $p_M$ by fixed-point best-response search on a 4000-point grid ($p_N=1.4734$, $p_M=1.9250$ in the main market). Firms choose from a discrete grid of $M=15$ points spanning $[p_N-0.1(p_M-p_N),\ p_M+0.1(p_M-p_N)]$.

**Learning agents.** Both firms use tabular Q-learning over state = (own last price, opponent-price signal), $\alpha=0.15$, $\delta=0.95$, $\epsilon_t=e^{-\beta t}$, $\beta=4\times10^{-5}$, over $T=150{,}000$ periods.

**Outcome metric.** Collusion index $\Delta = (\bar p - p_N)/(p_M-p_N)$, averaged over both firms' realized prices in the final 10% of periods.

**Interventions:** (1) **Diversity mandate** — firm 2 becomes a memoryless myopic best-responder to firm 1's last price. (2) **Transparency reduction** — the opponent-price state is coarsened to 3 bins. (3) **Audit+exploration-boost** — a regulator watching a rolling 1000-period collusion index forces $\epsilon=0.5$ on both firms for 1000 periods once it exceeds threshold $\theta$. (4) **Audit+price-cap** — same trigger, but both firms are forced to price at Nash for the cooldown window. All run at n=25 seeds (main market) and n=15 seeds (robustness market, $\mu=0.5$); threshold swept at $\theta\in\{0.3,0.5,0.7\}$. Significance via paired t-tests and Wilcoxon, Bonferroni-corrected across the 4-condition family.

## Results

**Main market (n=25).** Baseline $\Delta=0.420$ (SD 0.078). All four interventions lower the mean; only two survive correction:

| Condition | Mean Δ | Δ vs. baseline | paired-t p | Bonferroni p | Cohen's dz |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Baseline | 0.420 | — | — | — | — |
| Diversity mandate | 0.226 | −0.194 | 6.7e-7 | 2.7e-6 | −1.34 |
| Transparency reduction | 0.376 | −0.044 | 0.181 | 0.725 (n.s.) | −0.28 |
| Audit + exploration boost | 0.408 | −0.012 | 0.453 | 1.00 (n.s.) | −0.15 |
| Audit + price cap | 0.355 | −0.064 | 1.25e-3 | 4.99e-3 | −0.73 |

Wilcoxon agrees on direction/(in)significance throughout. **Robustness market ($\mu=0.5$, n=15):** baseline 0.443; diversity → 0.136 (corrected p=1.1e-8); price-cap audit → 0.335 (corrected p=3.8e-3); transparency and exploration-boost audit remain non-significant. **Threshold dose-response (audit+price-cap, main market):** $\theta=0.7$ (0.1 audits/run) → 0.416 ≈ baseline; $\theta=0.5$ (3.8 audits/run) → 0.355; $\theta=0.3$ (54 audits/run) → 0.202 — monotonic, no sign reversal.

**Firm-level decomposition of the diversity effect.** A reviewer noted the diversity condition changes the game structurally — a Q-learner facing a memoryless myopic firm, not a governance layer added on top of two comparable agents — so the pooled index drop could be mechanical (the myopic firm simply prices low and drags the average down) rather than evidence the *learner itself* becomes less collusive. We re-ran baseline and diversity (n=25, main market) tracking each firm's own tail-price index separately. Under baseline, firm 1 and firm 2 (both learners) average 0.399 and 0.441 — close to the pooled 0.420, as expected from symmetry. Under diversity, the myopic firm 2 averages 0.155 (it never learns to exploit softened competition, so it just best-responds toward low, near-Nash prices), while the *learning* firm 1 still falls to 0.296 (paired t vs. its own baseline value: diff=−0.102, p=0.017). So the effect is partly mechanical — the myopic firm's low prices pull down roughly half the pooled gap — but the learner's own equilibrium behavior genuinely shifts toward more competitive pricing when its rival cannot be tacitly coordinated with, which is itself informative: removing the rival's *capacity to reciprocate* collusive signals is enough to make the remaining learner behave more competitively, not just an artifact of averaging in a low-price non-learner.

**Hyperparameter sensitivity.** The main comparison uses one Calvano-style configuration. We re-ran all five conditions (n=10, main market) at (i) a higher learning rate ($\alpha=0.30$) and (ii) a slower exploration decay ($\beta=1\times10^{-5}$, more total exploration). The qualitative ranking is stable: diversity remains the strongest and most significant effect in both configs (α=0.30: diff=−0.231, bonf p=1.9e-4; β=1e-5: diff=−0.105, bonf p=1.8e-3). Audit+price-cap is directionally negative and significant at slow-β (bonf p=9.3e-4) though only marginal at high-α with n=10 (bonf p=0.061, likely underpowered at this reduced n rather than a reversed effect, given its n=25 significance in the main comparison). Audit+exploration-boost stays weakest and is never significant, even reversing sign (+0.036) under slow exploration decay — consistent with the main-text finding that it is indistinguishable from doing nothing. Transparency reduction is non-significant at high-α but strongly significant at slow-β (bonf p=2.2e-7), suggesting its effectiveness may depend more on the exploration schedule than the other interventions do — a caveat we did not have before and now flag explicitly rather than treating transparency's null result as hyperparameter-independent.

**Power analysis.** To address whether n=25/15 might be marginal for the two null results, we resampled subsets of size n∈{5,...,25} from the existing matched-pair differences (200 resamples per n, one-sample t-test against zero) for all four interventions. Diversity reaches 100% of resamples significant by n=10; audit+price-cap reaches 100% by n=20, confirming n=25 is comfortably powered for both positive results. For transparency and audit+exploration-boost, the fraction of significant resamples does not increase with n — it stays at or near 0% by n=20–25 (0% and 0%, down from small positive rates at n=5–10 that are consistent with false positives) — indicating the null results are not an artifact of insufficient sample size; the estimated effects are simply too small relative to seed-to-seed variance to be distinguishable from zero at any n in this range.

**Interpretation.** Interventions that *directly change the strategic environment* — removing one learner's capacity to reciprocate collusive signals (diversity) or forcing competitive prices during flagged episodes (price-cap audit) — reliably suppress collusion, and this holds up under firm-level decomposition, alternative hyperparameters, and power analysis. Interventions that only *perturb information or exploration* without changing what agents can ultimately settle on leave the learned equilibrium largely intact once the perturbation ends.

## Related Work (condensed)

Calvano et al. (2020, *AER*) is the foundational Q-learning collusion result; we adopt their demand model and price-grid conventions, and our baseline index (~0.42) matches their general magnitude. Klein (2021) and Asker et al. (2022) study how learning speed/asynchrony affects convergence *speed*, complementary to our focus on *interventions* holding the algorithm fixed; our hyperparameter check connects these axes and finds the ranking largely robust to learning rate/exploration schedule, with transparency as the most schedule-sensitive lever. Banchio and Mantegazza (2023) find deep-RL agents collude faster than tabular Q-learning; whether our ranking transfers is untested. On policy, the Preventing Algorithmic Collusion Act and EU DMA/DSA foreground transparency; our results reinforce that transparency reduction alone is the weakest, most schedule-dependent lever, consistent with the caution in Strategic Information Disclosure in Algorithmic Pricing (2026). Work on "pessimistic calibrated regret" (2025) and "public governance graphs" (2026) proposes more sophisticated audit detectors than our rolling-mean trigger; our threshold ablation suggests what the regulator *does* upon detection (enforce vs. nudge) matters more than detector sophistication. LLM-agent collusion work (Fish et al. 2024) shows the same phenomenon in language-model pricing agents; testing our interventions there is future work.

## Limitations

(1) One demand model (logit) at two differentiation levels. (2) The audit trigger assumes the regulator observes the true Nash price in real time — stronger than a real antitrust authority has. A more realistic price-cap could instead use the *lowest prices observed during agents' early high-exploration phase* as a demand-model-free proxy for competitive pricing; we did not implement this variant, and it is the most important untested step for the price-cap audit's real-world deployability. (3) Tabular Q-learning only; deep-RL/LLM agents untested, though Banchio and Mantegazza (2023) suggest deep-RL agents collude even more readily. (4) Detection and enforcement-response are varied jointly, not as an independent 2×2 factorial. (5) No empirical anchor to real-world price data (cf. Assad et al. 2023) — claims concern *relative* ranking, not absolute collusion risk. (6) Hyperparameter sensitivity used reduced n=10 for two configurations, not a full grid; the price-cap result at high learning rate was only marginal at this n, so larger-n confirmation is a natural next step.

## Conclusion

In a controlled, matched-seed, dual-market comparison of four proposed socio-technical remedies, mandating algorithmic diversity is the most effective and robust intervention, followed by audits that directly enforce competitive pricing when triggered. This holds after decomposing the diversity effect by firm (the learner itself, not just the myopic rival, prices more competitively), after varying learning-rate and exploration-schedule hyperparameters, and after a power analysis confirming the null results for transparency and exploration-boost audits are not sample-size artifacts. Transparency restrictions and exploration-perturbing audits remain statistically indistinguishable from doing nothing once corrected, though transparency's effectiveness appears more sensitive to the exploration schedule than the other levers. Among frequently-proposed regulatory tools, the ones that structurally constrain what the pricing algorithm can do — not the ones that merely constrain what it can see or briefly destabilize its exploration — are the load-bearing mechanisms for breaking algorithmic collusion in this setting. All code, raw per-seed results, and the new decomposition/sensitivity/power analyses are included for exact reproduction.
