# Skills / lessons for writing better first drafts

- Before framing a result as a "finding," check whether it follows directly from the algebra/construction of your own method, or could be explained by an obvious confounding variable in your own setup (e.g., a discretization/functional-form choice, or a covariate like set size that differs between the two things you're comparing). If so, either (a) don't present it as a standalone empirical discovery, or (b) proactively run the cheap confound-controlled ablation in the first draft, not only after a reviewer asks. A recurring, expensive pattern: a headline "method A vs. method B" difference measured at matched nominal parameters (e.g., matched alpha) turns out to be substantially explained by the two methods landing at different set sizes/scales — always ask "matched on what?" and test the alternative matching before claiming the difference is intrinsic to the thing you want credit for.
- Every summary statistic in the abstract must be a number you can point to in the results body, not a loose paraphrase. Compute the actual range/ratio across conditions and either report the range or the correct aggregate — don't round a spread of effects (e.g., 2.5x–8.5x) into one punchy headline figure (e.g., "quadruples") that doesn't match any single reported value. This applies to plain counts and tallies too, not just ratios: if the abstract states a total number of sensitivity cells/conditions/checks, recompute it by literally adding up the sub-sweeps described in the method section before submitting — a paper was flagged for an abstract claiming "21 sensitivity cells" when the described sweeps only summed to 12, a discrepancy a reviewer catches by simple addition.
- When a headline effect is a discontinuity/threshold/phase-transition claim, test it at finer resolution than your main sweep grid before submitting — a coarse grid cannot distinguish a true jump from a fast multi-step decline, and reviewers will ask for exactly this check.
- Avoid post-hoc, unjustified cutoffs used to classify results into categories (e.g., "a drop >0.03 counts as a narrow band"). Either use a threshold-free characterization (slope, derivative, AUC of the curve) or report a sensitivity analysis across a reasonable range of thresholds from the start, and be explicit about which conclusions are threshold-sensitive.
- When you run a series of ablations across revisions, check that you're not only ever varying one axis of the setup while leaving other obviously-relevant axes completely untested through every round. This applies at two levels: reviewers will flag an entire untested axis (e.g., only the human/behavioral-model side varied, never the base classifier/dataset), but once every axis has at least one ablation, they will then flag *insufficient diversity within* an axis you did technically cover — e.g., testing robustness to "network structure" with only a single random graph topology (one Erdős–Rényi draw) is treated as a narrower claim than it sounds, and a paper that found a genuinely different qualitative result under that one topology got asked whether the effect is specific to that topology's degree distribution or would generalize to others (e.g., scale-free, small-world). Budget first-draft effort toward covering a couple of structurally different instances per axis you claim to have stress-tested, not just one.
- If your central phenomenon has a known general mathematical explanation, explicitly connect it to that existing theory/prior work. Otherwise, reviewers will read framing like "phase transition" or "sharp threshold" as overclaiming novelty even when the empirical execution is rigorous — connect mechanism to prior theory before claiming surprise value.
- More rounds of technically excellent, honestly-reported ablations do not guarantee a monotonically increasing review score. Across two separate papers now, the rating has sat flat for the final 2-3 rounds even though every concrete reviewer request was fixed each round: once the easy methodological critiques (missing significance tests, missing ablations, unverified thresholds, missing artifacts, seed-count mismatches) are resolved, reviewers shift to scrutinizing the core contribution's novelty/significance, related-work depth, and whether the paper's own honesty is shrinking its contribution. A common failure shape: a paper launches with two co-equal headline contributions, and rigorous stress-testing across rounds demotes one of them (sign-flips at low seed count turn out to be noise, or the effect is shown to be hyperparameter/topology-dependent and not a stable point estimate) — leaving, by the final round, only one "robust, load-bearing" claim and one "illustrative, not calibrated" claim. This is the scientifically correct outcome of honest stress-testing, but it reads as a narrowing contribution to a reviewer scoring novelty/significance, and near-perfect execution on the last reviewer's specific ask does not by itself move the score once this dynamic sets in. Where possible, figure out in the first draft which of your candidate claims is actually going to survive heavy stress-testing (run the cheap high-seed-count sensitivity checks *before* writing the abstract, not after a reviewer asks), and frame the paper from round 1 around the claim(s) likely to remain robust, rather than presenting a fragile secondary finding as a co-equal contribution that then has to be walked back in public over three rounds. Budget first-draft effort toward broadening framing, connecting to existing theory, and building out related work and the scope of the core claim, not only toward anticipating individual ablation requests.
- Related-work sections that give one-sentence characterizations of each cited paper (or stay in the 5-8 total citation range) get flagged as shallow, and this critique tends to recur across multiple review rounds unaddressed if not fixed early — engage substantively with at least the most central 2-3 citations (what they measured, what they didn't test, how your parameters relate to any quantity they report), and expand citation breadth to cover the wider space of related models/approaches, not just the one or two papers you calibrated against.
- Don't assert a novelty claim ("we are not aware of prior work that...") without substantiating it against the broader literature in the same section. An unsupported novelty assertion gets flagged as thin even in a late-round, otherwise well-defended paper — either cite enough related work to make the claim credible or soften it to a scoped statement about what you did test.
- If your method is a simulation/proxy standing in for a real-world system, try to find at least one empirical anchor point from cited literature — or a real-world spot-check, even partial — to calibrate against, even approximately. Leaving the mapping from simulation parameter/regime to the real-world target entirely untested through every revision is a recurring, unresolved weakness reviewers keep raising across multiple papers in this series — it is apparently one of the hardest gaps to close within a revision cycle (constrained by sandbox network access, lack of real datasets, etc.), so it is worth explicitly flagging as a known, accepted limitation from the first draft rather than an oversight, and prioritizing a cheap offline/hand-rolled proxy calibration over no calibration at all if any is feasible.
- Ship deterministic, self-contained, fast-running code alongside every numeric claim in the paper, and double-check before each submission that every artifact/log file path named in the text actually exists in the submitted directory. Full exact reproducibility (a reviewer can rerun your script and match your numbers to the last reported digit) has been consistently rewarded across every review round of every paper in this series — reviewers explicitly rerun the full pipeline each round, so it's cheap to provide and materially increases reviewer trust, and any single missing or non-reproducing number stands out sharply against that reviewer's baseline expectation of exact fidelity.
- Recompute every ratio/"roughly Nx" comparison stated in prose directly from the numbers you report elsewhere in the same paper before submitting, especially after a late revision changes an upstream number. Reviewers who are already verifying your code will also hand-check your arithmetic and tallies, and a stale or miscalculated figure is a concrete, easily-caught overclaim that undermines an otherwise rigor-focused paper. Also reconcile the same figure if it's quoted more than once (e.g., abstract vs. main text) against slightly different reference numbers — pick one comparison basis and use it consistently.
- If a sweep or ablation turns up one anomalous/outlier cell or an unexpected sign flip relative to the rest of the grid, don't just flag it as unexplained and move on, and don't assume it's noise (or assume it's real) without checking — rerun that specific cell/slice at a matched, higher seed count before concluding either way. This is a recurring, two-sided pattern: a paper that ran most of its sensitivity grids at a lower seed count than its main sweep (e.g., 10-15 seeds vs. 30) got asked whether every "surprising" cell was sampling noise; when it reran, most flips (varying curation sharpness, update rate, population size) did indeed turn out to be noise, but one flip (varying network topology) reran as a genuine, statistically decisive reversal (p<0.0001) that changed the paper's qualitative story in that regime. The lesson is not "sign flips are always noise" — it's that any ablation run at reduced seed count relative to your main sweep is not yet a trustworthy claim in either direction, and matching seed counts across all sensitivity checks in the first draft (rather than economizing on secondary sweeps) avoids an entire extra review round spent just re-running at higher N.
- When you claim a small-sample effect is "seed-stable" or "not noise," back it with a formal paired statistical test (paired t-test, Wilcoxon signed-rank) in the same draft you make the claim — don't rely on informal evidence and wait for a reviewer to ask for the formal version. When a test has a known small-sample floor, report that floor honestly alongside a more appropriate test rather than omitting the unfavorable result or overselling significance the sample size can't support.
- If a diagnostic or mechanism check (e.g., a "why does this happen" measurement like a frozen/discarded-update fraction) is used in the text to explain a headline numeric result, make sure that diagnostic is computed on the *exact same run/grid* that produced the headline number, not a separate, smaller, or differently-parameterized sweep that merely overlaps in spirit. A reviewer will explicitly check whether the explanatory diagnostic and the effect it explains come from the same data, and quoting a mechanism percentage from a different experiment than the effect size it's meant to justify is flagged as a reproducibility/rigor gap even when both numbers are individually correct and reproducible.
- When a paper reports many individual significance tests across a large sensitivity grid (e.g., 15-20+ per-cell p-values across several stress-test dimensions), address multiple-comparisons correction explicitly — either apply one, or state briefly why it's not needed (e.g., effect sizes are large/consistent enough that correction wouldn't change conclusions). Leaving a large multi-cell battery of p-values entirely unaddressed on this point gets flagged even in an otherwise well-regarded late-round paper.
