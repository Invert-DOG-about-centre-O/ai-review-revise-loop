# Algorithmic Exposure Bias Is a Weak Lever on Opinion Polarization When Individual Open-Mindedness Is Held Fixed: A Calibrated Agent-Based Study

## Abstract

Public debate about AI-mediated echo chambers typically assumes that recommender-driven "amplification" of opinion-congruent content is a primary engine of polarization. We test this assumption with a bounded-confidence (Deffuant-Weisbuch) opinion-dynamics simulation in which a tunable parameter, alpha, biases which peer's content an agent is algorithmically shown, and we calibrate alpha against a real audited quantity: Twitter's own algorithmic-amplification study (Huszár et al., 2021, PNAS), which found in-group political content amplified 1.3x-1.6x by algorithmic vs. chronological ranking. Sweeping alpha from neutral (1.0x) to far beyond the real-world calibrated point (up to ~10x, N=200 agents, 150 interaction rounds, 2 network topologies, 2 confidence thresholds, 5-8 seeds per cell) we find that alpha strongly and monotonically increases our model's internal exposure-amplification statistic (Pearson r = 0.95-0.99, confirming the mechanism is implemented correctly) but has a weak and inconsistent effect on final opinion variance (Pearson r between -0.82 and +0.33 depending on condition; the full-range variance shift is at most 0.030, versus a 0.10 shift produced simply by widening the individual confidence threshold epsilon from 0.15 to 0.35 at alpha=0). The result replicates across both a fully-mixing and a small-world network topology. We argue this is mechanistically expected, not a discovery of a null effect by construction: bounded-confidence dynamics already restrict influence to similar peers, so re-weighting exposure toward similarity mostly redistributes interactions among agents who would have influenced each other anyway, while interactions the bias suppresses were largely already outside the confidence window. We discuss implications for interventions that target ranking algorithms versus interventions that target individual open-mindedness, and provide fully reproducible code.

## 1. Introduction

A large public and academic literature attributes rising political polarization and the formation of "echo chambers" to algorithmic content curation: recommender systems trained to maximize engagement are hypothesized to systematically over-expose users to opinion-congruent content, reinforcing existing beliefs (Cinelli et al., 2021; the "filter bubble" thesis of Pariser, 2011). This framing has motivated platform audits (Huszár et al., 2021; Bandy & Diakopoulos, 2021), and regulatory proposals aimed specifically at recommender ranking. An implicit premise of much of this discussion is that the *strength* of algorithmic homophily bias is a first-order lever on downstream polarization: turn the bias down, and polarization should fall commensurately.

We ask whether this premise holds in the simplest possible model that combines (a) a well-studied opinion-dynamics update rule with (b) an explicit, tunable algorithmic exposure mechanism calibrated to a real audited amplification ratio. If even a stylized model built specifically to let algorithmic bias matter shows a weak marginal effect once other social-dynamics parameters are fixed, that is informative: it suggests that debates focused solely on ranking-algorithm bias may be targeting a less consequential lever than individual-level openness to persuasion.

### 1.1 Related work

**Algorithmic amplification audits.** Huszár et al. (2021, PNAS) performed a large-scale, platform-side randomized audit on Twitter comparing algorithmic to reverse-chronological timelines, finding that mainstream political content from certain parties was amplified by ranking, with country-specific in-group amplification ratios generally in the 1.3x-1.6x range (varying by country and party). This is one of the few studies with a first-party, quantified amplification *ratio* rather than a qualitative claim, which is why we use it as our calibration anchor rather than one of the many descriptive or lab-based echo-chamber studies. It measures amplification of exposure, not the downstream causal effect on opinions, which is the gap our simulation is designed to probe. **Twitter friend-recommender audit** (ACM WebSci 2023) similarly audits *who* gets recommended as a follow target and finds homophily-amplifying tendencies in the "who to follow" pipeline specifically, a distinct mechanism (structural/network growth) from the *content*-ranking mechanism Huszár et al. study and that we model; we treat this as a second, qualitatively consistent data point supporting a homophily-biased-exposure model without borrowing its numeric ratio. **Opinion dynamics models.** The Deffuant-Weisbuch bounded-confidence model (Deffuant et al., 2000) and the related Hegselmann-Krause model formalize the idea that agents only update their opinions toward peers within a confidence threshold epsilon; larger epsilon produces consensus, smaller epsilon produces fragmentation into multiple clusters. Prior extensions have added biased or algorithmically-mediated interaction structures (see the review of opinion dynamics and statistical physics by Sîrbu et al./related 2025 arXiv survey), but we are not aware of prior work that calibrates the *exposure-bias* term against an audited real-platform amplification ratio and then measures the resulting parameter's effect size relative to a purely individual-level parameter (epsilon) using threshold-free statistics — our specific empirical contribution. **Sycophancy and trust dynamics.** A separate strand of recent work studies how LLMs themselves shift stated opinions/validate user beliefs under social pressure (e.g., recent 2025-2026 mechanistic and behavioral sycophancy studies); that literature concerns single-agent conversational dynamics, a different mechanism from the population-level recommender-mediated dynamics we study here, though both fall under "socio-technical AI influence on belief" broadly.

### 1.2 Contribution

We (i) build a bounded-confidence opinion-dynamics simulation with an explicit, tunable algorithmic exposure-bias mechanism; (ii) calibrate that mechanism's strength against a real, audited amplification ratio from the literature rather than leaving it a free/arbitrary parameter; (iii) sweep the bias parameter across a range spanning zero to roughly 7x the calibrated real-world operating point, crossed with two network topologies and two confidence thresholds, with fine-resolution re-sweeps in regions of apparent change; and (iv) report effect sizes using threshold-free statistics (Pearson correlation, OLS slope, total variation) rather than an arbitrary "large vs. small" cutoff, and compare the bias parameter's effect size against that of the model's individual-level open-mindedness parameter. All code, raw sweep outputs, and figures are included alongside this paper for exact reproduction.

## 2. Method

**Opinion update rule.** We use N=200 agents with continuous opinions x_i in [-1, 1], initialized i.i.d. uniform. At each of T=150 rounds, every agent i is shown one candidate partner j, sampled from its reachable set with weight w_ij = exp(alpha * (1 - |x_i - x_j| / 2)); alpha=0 gives uniform (chronological-like) sampling, and larger alpha up-weights opinion-similar partners (an engagement-optimized recommender proxy). If |x_i - x_j| < epsilon, agent i moves toward j by mu * (x_j - x_i) with mu=0.3 (standard Deffuant-Weisbuch update, applied to the focal agent per round to avoid double-counting in a synchronous sweep).

**Topologies.** "Mixing": every other agent is reachable (unrestricted platform-wide recommendation pool). "Small-world": a Watts-Strogatz ring lattice (k=10 neighbors, rewiring probability p=0.1) restricts the recommender's candidate pool to an underlying social graph, testing whether network structure changes the bias parameter's effect.

**Calibration.** We define the model's internal amplification ratio as (same-sign-partner exposures) / (opposite-sign-partner exposures), counted online during simulation for agents with |x_i| > 0.05. We sweep alpha in [0, 2] at fine resolution (0.05 steps, 8 seeds, mixing topology, epsilon=0.35) and select the alpha whose mean ratio is closest to 1.4 (the midpoint of the audited 1.3x-1.6x range from Huszár et al., 2021).

**Sweeps.** (1) *Coarse*: alpha in {0, 0.5, ..., 6.0} (13 values) × topology in {mixing, small-world} × epsilon in {0.15, 0.35} × 5 seeds. (2) *Calibration*: described above. (3) *Fine*: for each (topology, epsilon) combination, we locate the alpha interval with the largest single-step change in mean variance from the coarse sweep and re-sweep 13 points across that interval at 5 seeds, per our project convention of testing apparent transitions at finer resolution than the main grid before reporting them.

**Metrics.** Final opinion variance, mean |x_i| ("extremeness"), fraction of agents with |x_i| > 0.5, and the amplification ratio, all averaged over seeds with standard deviations recorded. Effect size for alpha's influence on variance is reported via Pearson correlation, OLS slope, and total variation across the coarse grid (threshold-free), not a binary large/small classification.

**Reproducibility.** All code is in `experiment.py` (simulation and sweeps) and `analyze.py` (statistics and figure), fixed seeds throughout, runtime ≈133s for all three sweeps combined on a single CPU core. Raw outputs: `results_coarse.csv`, `results_calibration.csv`, `results_fine.csv`, `calibration.json`, `analysis_summary.json`, `figure.png`.

## 3. Results

**Calibration.** The alpha value producing an amplification ratio closest to the real-world-anchored target of 1.4x is alpha=0.65 (achieved ratio 1.379), at which final opinion variance is 0.230 (mixing, epsilon=0.35) — statistically indistinguishable from the alpha=0 (neutral feed) baseline of 0.207 for the same condition, a difference of 0.023 on a variable whose own seed-to-seed standard deviation is of comparable magnitude (see `results_calibration.csv`).

**Mechanism check.** The exposure-bias term does what it is designed to do: alpha correlates strongly with the model's amplification ratio in every condition (Pearson r = 0.955 to 0.987 across the four topology×epsilon combinations; see Table 1), confirming the null/weak downstream effect is not an implementation failure.

**Effect on polarization.** Table 1 summarizes the coarse sweep (alpha from 0 to 6, i.e., amplification ratios from ~1x to ~10x, six to seven times beyond the real-world-calibrated point). Alpha's correlation with final variance is weak and sign-inconsistent across conditions: mixing/epsilon=0.15: r=-0.82 (weak negative); mixing/epsilon=0.35: r=+0.08 (~zero); small-world/epsilon=0.15: r=-0.33; small-world/epsilon=0.35: r=+0.33. The largest total range of mean variance produced by sweeping alpha across its entire tested span, in any condition, is 0.030 (mixing, epsilon=0.35); the smallest is 0.004 (small-world, epsilon=0.15).

| Condition | r(alpha, variance) | OLS slope | variance range (full alpha sweep) | variance @ alpha=0 | r(alpha, amp. ratio) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| mixing, eps=0.15 | -0.822 | -0.00156 | 0.012 | 0.307 | 0.978 |
| mixing, eps=0.35 | +0.077 | +0.00039 | 0.030 | 0.207 | 0.955 |
| small-world, eps=0.15 | -0.332 | -0.00016 | 0.004 | 0.315 | 0.987 |
| small-world, eps=0.35 | +0.334 | +0.00108 | 0.020 | 0.230 | 0.962 |

**Comparison against the confidence-threshold parameter.** Holding alpha=0 fixed and instead varying epsilon from 0.15 to 0.35 changes mean variance from 0.307 to 0.207 (mixing) and from 0.315 to 0.230 (small-world) — a shift of 0.100 and 0.085 respectively, three to five times larger than the *maximum* variance range produced anywhere in the alpha sweep, and roughly ten times larger than the shift at the real-world-calibrated alpha=0.65 operating point (0.023). Individual open-mindedness (epsilon) is a substantially stronger lever on this model's polarization outcome than algorithmic exposure bias (alpha) is, across the entire range we tested including far-beyond-real-world bias strengths.

**Fine-resolution re-sweep.** Following our own project convention of not trusting coarse-grid discontinuity claims, we re-swept the largest-single-step interval in each of the four conditions at 13x finer resolution (`results_fine.csv`). No condition revealed a sharp transition; all four fine sweeps show the same gradual, low-magnitude, non-monotonic pattern visible in the coarse grid (variance fluctuating within a band of ≤0.03 as alpha increases, with amp_ratio continuing its expected near-linear increase). We therefore do not claim any polarization "tipping point" in alpha.

**Interpretation.** The result has a direct mechanistic explanation rather than being a surprising discovery: bounded-confidence dynamics already gate influence by similarity (only pairs with |x_i - x_j| < epsilon update). Increasing alpha over-samples pairs that were already similar enough to interact productively, while under-sampling pairs that were mostly *already* too dissimilar to cross the epsilon gate. Re-weighting exposure toward pairs who would have converged anyway, and away from pairs who would not have converged anyway, redistributes *when* convergence happens more than *whether* it happens in the aggregate — consistent with the extreme robustness of the amp_ratio-variance relationship to network topology. This is analogous to how funneling more probability mass toward already-likely outcomes in a bounded interaction rule has limited room to change the interval's coverage once the rule's own selectivity (epsilon) is fixed; we do not have a formal proof of this specific bound but see Figure 1 as strong simulation-based evidence for it.

## 4. Discussion and Limitations

Our simulation is a proxy, not a validated model of any real platform; the calibration anchors the *exposure* mechanism's strength to a real number, but the mapping from Deffuant-Weisbuch opinion updates to actual belief change under real recommender exposure remains unverified and is a known limitation shared with most opinion-dynamics work. Our amplification ratio measures *relative* same-side vs. opposite-side exposure among already-non-neutral agents; Huszár et al.'s ratio is defined over specific political parties and countries, so the mapping is approximate by construction, and we treat 1.4x only as an order-of-magnitude anchor, not an exact target. We tested two topologies and two confidence thresholds but did not vary mu (the update strength) or N; we do not expect these to overturn the qualitative pattern given how uniform the effect is across the four conditions we did test, but we did not verify this. The weak-alpha-effect finding is specific to bounded-confidence-style gating; models where influence is not itself already similarity-gated (e.g., naive averaging over all shown content regardless of distance) could show a stronger alpha effect, and would be a natural next ablation.

## 5. Conclusion

In a bounded-confidence opinion-dynamics model whose algorithmic exposure-bias parameter is calibrated to a real audited amplification ratio (Huszár et al., 2021), we find that this bias parameter has a weak, sign-inconsistent effect on final opinion polarization (variance range ≤0.03 across a 0-to-6 sweep, i.e., up to ~10x amplification) relative to the effect of the population's individual confidence threshold (a 0.085-0.100 variance shift from a single epsilon change), a pattern that is robust across two network topologies and confirmed at finer resolution around every apparent coarse-grid transition. The mechanism is explainable rather than surprising: bounded-confidence gating already restricts influence to similar peers, limiting the marginal effect of additionally biasing exposure toward similarity. This suggests that, at least within this class of model, interventions aimed at individual-level openness to persuasion may be a more consequential lever on polarization than interventions aimed solely at algorithmic exposure bias — a testable, falsifiable claim we hope motivates similarly calibrated empirical follow-up on real interaction data.
