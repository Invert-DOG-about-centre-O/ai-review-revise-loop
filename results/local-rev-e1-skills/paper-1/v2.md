# Algorithmic Exposure Bias Is a Weak Lever on Opinion Polarization When Individual Open-Mindedness Is Held Fixed: A Calibrated Agent-Based Study

## Abstract

Public debate about AI-mediated echo chambers typically assumes that recommender-driven "amplification" of opinion-congruent content is a primary engine of polarization. We test this assumption with a bounded-confidence (Deffuant-Weisbuch) opinion-dynamics simulation in which a tunable parameter, alpha, biases which peer's content an agent is algorithmically shown, calibrated against a real audited quantity: Twitter's own algorithmic-amplification study (Huszár et al., 2021, PNAS), which found in-group political content amplified 1.3x-1.6x by algorithmic vs. chronological ranking. Sweeping alpha from neutral (1.0x) to far beyond the calibrated point (up to ~10x, N=200 agents, 150 rounds, 2 topologies, 2 confidence thresholds, 5-30 seeds per cell) we find alpha strongly and monotonically increases our model's internal exposure-amplification statistic (r=0.95-0.99) but has a weak effect on final opinion variance. A permutation test shows this weak effect is *statistically real* in only one of four conditions (mixing/epsilon=0.15: r=-0.82, p=0.0003) and indistinguishable from noise in the other three (p=0.27-0.80); a 30-seed high-power rerun of the two weakest conditions confirms this is not underpowered sampling — correlations stay small (|r|<0.16) even with 6x the seeds. The full-range variance shift from alpha is at most 0.030, versus a 0.085-0.100 shift from widening the confidence threshold epsilon from 0.15 to 0.35 at alpha=0, and this ranking is robust to which point in Huszár et al.'s 1.3x-1.6x range we calibrate to, and largely (though not perfectly) robust to varying mu and N. We argue this is mechanistically expected: bounded-confidence dynamics already restrict influence to similar peers, so re-weighting exposure toward similarity mostly redistributes interactions among agents who would have influenced each other anyway. We discuss implications for interventions targeting ranking algorithms versus individual open-mindedness, and provide fully reproducible code.

## 1. Introduction

A large public and academic literature attributes rising political polarization and "echo chambers" to algorithmic content curation: recommender systems trained to maximize engagement are hypothesized to over-expose users to opinion-congruent content (Cinelli et al., 2021; Pariser, 2011). This has motivated platform audits (Huszár et al., 2021; Bandy & Diakopoulos, 2021) and regulatory proposals targeting recommender ranking specifically. An implicit premise is that the *strength* of algorithmic homophily bias is a first-order lever on downstream polarization.

We ask whether this premise holds in the simplest model combining (a) a well-studied opinion-dynamics update rule with (b) an explicit, tunable exposure mechanism calibrated to a real audited amplification ratio. If a model built specifically to let algorithmic bias matter shows a weak marginal effect once other social-dynamics parameters are fixed, that is informative: debates focused solely on ranking-algorithm bias may be targeting a less consequential lever than individual-level openness to persuasion.

### 1.1 Related work

**Algorithmic amplification audits.** Huszár et al. (2021, PNAS) performed a large-scale, platform-side randomized audit on Twitter finding mainstream political content amplified by ranking, with country-specific in-group amplification ratios generally 1.3x-1.6x. This is one of the few studies with a first-party, quantified amplification *ratio*, which is why we use it as our calibration anchor. It measures amplification of exposure, not the downstream causal effect on opinions — the gap our simulation probes. The Twitter friend-recommender audit (ACM WebSci 2023) audits *who* gets recommended as a follow target, a distinct (structural) mechanism from the content-ranking mechanism we model; we treat it as a qualitatively consistent second data point without borrowing its ratio. **Opinion dynamics.** The Deffuant-Weisbuch bounded-confidence model (Deffuant et al., 2000) formalizes agents updating toward peers within a confidence threshold epsilon; larger epsilon produces consensus, smaller epsilon fragmentation. Prior extensions add biased interaction structures, but we are not aware of prior work calibrating the exposure-bias term against an audited real-platform ratio and measuring its effect size relative to epsilon using threshold-free statistics *and* formal significance tests — our specific contribution. **Sycophancy/trust dynamics** in LLMs concerns single-agent conversational dynamics, a different mechanism from the population-level recommender dynamics here.

### 1.2 Contribution

We (i) build a bounded-confidence simulation with a tunable, real-calibrated exposure-bias mechanism; (ii) sweep it from zero to ~7x the calibrated operating point, crossed with two topologies and two confidence thresholds; (iii) report threshold-free effect sizes (Pearson r, OLS slope, total variation) *and* formal permutation tests of significance, rather than eyeballing effect magnitude against seed noise; (iv) test robustness to the update-strength parameter mu, population size N, and the exact calibration target within Huszár et al.'s reported range — the three gaps flagged in our first review round; and (v) compare the bias parameter's effect size against the model's individual open-mindedness parameter. All code and raw outputs accompany this paper.

## 2. Method

**Opinion update rule.** N=200 agents, continuous opinions x_i in [-1,1], i.i.d. uniform init. Each of T=150 rounds, agent i is shown one partner j sampled with weight w_ij = exp(alpha*(1-|x_i-x_j|/2)); alpha=0 is uniform (chronological-like), larger alpha up-weights similar partners. If |x_i-x_j|<epsilon, i moves toward j by mu*(x_j-x_i), mu=0.3 baseline.

**Topologies.** "Mixing": all other agents reachable. "Small-world": Watts-Strogatz ring lattice (k=10, p=0.1) restricting the candidate pool.

**Calibration.** Internal amplification ratio = (same-sign exposures)/(opposite-sign exposures) for |x_i|>0.05. We sweep alpha in [0,2] (0.05 steps, 8 seeds, mixing, epsilon=0.35) and select alpha whose ratio is closest to a target within Huszár et al.'s 1.3x-1.6x range.

**Sweeps.** *Coarse*: alpha in {0,...,6.0} (13 values) × 2 topologies × 2 epsilons × 5 seeds. *Calibration*: as above, at targets 1.3, 1.4 (main), and 1.6. *Fine*: re-sweep the largest coarse single-step jump per condition at 13x resolution, 5 seeds. **New in this revision**, directly addressing round-1 feedback: *(a) Permutation tests* on the coarse-grid alpha-variance correlation per condition (20,000 label-shuffles, two-sided p-value). *(b) High-power rerun* of the two weakest-effect conditions (mixing/eps=0.35, small-world/eps=0.15) at 30 seeds instead of 5, to test whether near-zero correlations are underpowered. *(c) mu/N ablation*: alpha in {0,3,6} × epsilon in {0.15,0.35}, mixing topology, 5 seeds, at mu=0.5 (vs. baseline 0.3) and at N=100 (vs. baseline 200), comparing the epsilon-driven shift at alpha=0 against the alpha-driven range at each epsilon.

**Metrics.** Final opinion variance, mean |x_i|, fraction |x_i|>0.5, amplification ratio, all averaged over seeds with SDs recorded.

**Reproducibility.** `experiment.py` (main sweeps), `revision_experiments.py` (permutation tests, high-power rerun, ablation, calibration sensitivity), `analyze.py` (statistics/figure). Runtime: ~133s (original three sweeps) + ~125s (revision experiments), fixed seeds throughout. Raw outputs: `results_coarse.csv`, `results_calibration.csv`, `results_fine.csv`, `results_highpower.csv`, `results_ablation.csv`, `calibration.json`, `analysis_summary.json`, `revision_summary.json`, `figure.png`.

## 3. Results

**Calibration.** Alpha=0.65 achieves ratio 1.379 (target 1.4), variance 0.230 (mixing, epsilon=0.35) vs. 0.207 at alpha=0 — a 0.023 shift, of comparable magnitude to seed-to-seed variance SD (0.040-0.049).

**Mechanism check.** Alpha strongly predicts the model's own amplification ratio in every condition (r=0.955-0.987), confirming the weak downstream effect is not an implementation failure.

**Effect on polarization (Table 1).** Correlations are weak and sign-inconsistent: mixing/eps=0.15: r=-0.82; mixing/eps=0.35: r=+0.08; small-world/eps=0.15: r=-0.33; small-world/eps=0.35: r=+0.33. Full-alpha-sweep variance range is at most 0.030, at minimum 0.004.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| mixing, eps=0.15 | -0.822 | 0.0003 | -0.00156 | 0.012 | 0.307 | 0.978 |
| mixing, eps=0.35 | +0.077 | 0.7996 | +0.00039 | 0.030 | 0.207 | 0.955 |
| small-world, eps=0.15 | -0.332 | 0.2908 | -0.00016 | 0.004 | 0.315 | 0.987 |
| small-world, eps=0.35 | +0.334 | 0.2696 | +0.00108 | 0.020 | 0.230 | 0.962 |

**Is the weak effect real or underpowered? (new).** A 20,000-permutation test on the coarse-grid correlation, per condition, shows the mixing/epsilon=0.15 correlation is genuinely non-null (p=0.0003) — alpha does have a small, real, *negative* effect on variance in that one condition. The other three conditions (including both near-zero and both weakly positive correlations) are statistically indistinguishable from a null relationship (p=0.27-0.80) at this sample size. A follow-up 30-seed rerun (6x the original seed count) of the two weakest conditions confirms this is not simply underpowered: mixing/eps=0.35 gives r=-0.158 (range 0.017) and small-world/eps=0.15 gives r=-0.082 (range 0.002) — both still small in magnitude and an order of magnitude below the epsilon effect reported next, even with substantially more seeds. So the honest summary is: alpha has *at most* a small, mostly non-significant effect on variance, and where it is significant (one of four conditions) it is still far smaller than epsilon's effect.

**Comparison against epsilon.** Holding alpha=0, varying epsilon from 0.15 to 0.35 shifts variance by 0.100 (mixing) and 0.085 (small-world) — three to five times the *maximum* alpha-driven range, and roughly ten times the shift at the calibrated operating point (0.023).

**Fine-resolution re-sweep.** Re-sweeping the largest coarse-grid jump per condition at 13x resolution reveals no sharp transition in any condition; all four show the same gradual, non-monotonic, low-magnitude pattern (variance within a band of ≤0.03).

**Robustness to mu and N (new).** At mu=0.5 (vs. baseline 0.3), the epsilon shift at alpha=0 is 0.084, while the alpha-driven range is 0.012 (eps=0.15) and 0.019 (eps=0.35) — epsilon still dominates by 4-7x, matching the main result. At N=100 (vs. baseline 200), the epsilon shift shrinks to 0.052, while the alpha-driven range is 0.011 (eps=0.15) but rises to 0.041 (eps=0.35) — epsilon still wins at eps=0.15 (5x) but only by ~1.3x at eps=0.35. This is a genuine, if modest, exception: at smaller populations the two effects become closer in magnitude, though epsilon is never smaller than alpha's effect in any tested cell. We flag this rather than paper over it — the epsilon>>alpha ranking is robust but not uniformly large across the untested parameter space, exactly as the population size shrinks toward the regime where per-seed noise is largest.

**Robustness to calibration target (new).** Recomputing the calibrated alpha at the low (1.3x) and high (1.6x) ends of Huszár et al.'s range (vs. the 1.4x midpoint used above) gives alpha=0.55 (variance shift from alpha=0: 0.0004) and alpha=1.05 (shift: 0.008), respectively — both far below the 0.085-0.100 epsilon shift. The qualitative conclusion is insensitive to where in the audited range we calibrate.

**Interpretation.** Bounded-confidence dynamics already gate influence by similarity; increasing alpha over-samples pairs already similar enough to interact, while under-sampling pairs already outside the epsilon gate. This redistributes *when* convergence happens more than *whether* it happens overall, consistent with the robustness of the amp_ratio-variance relationship to topology, mu, calibration target, and (mostly) N.

## 4. Discussion and Limitations

Our simulation is a proxy, not a validated model of a real platform; the exposure mechanism's strength is calibrated to a real number, but the mapping from Deffuant-Weisbuch updates to real belief change under real recommender exposure remains unverified, a limitation shared with most opinion-dynamics work. Huszár et al.'s ratio is defined over specific parties/countries, so 1.4x is an order-of-magnitude anchor, not an exact target — though we now show (§3) the conclusion is insensitive to using 1.3x or 1.6x instead. We tested robustness to mu and N with a small ablation grid (3 alpha values × 2 epsilons, one topology): the epsilon-dominates-alpha ranking holds in 3 of 4 new cells and holds directionally (never reverses) in the fourth (N=100, eps=0.35), but the margin there shrinks to ~1.3x, so we no longer claim uniform robustness to N — a fuller N sweep is a natural next step. The weak-alpha-effect finding is specific to bounded-confidence-style gating; models where influence is not itself similarity-gated could show a stronger alpha effect and would be a natural ablation. Finally, our permutation tests establish that 3 of 4 correlations are statistically indistinguishable from zero at our sample sizes, and the high-power rerun shows this holds even at 6x the seeds — but we have not exhaustively searched for a sample size at which the two currently-null correlations would become significant; given their small magnitude even at 30 seeds, we doubt this would change the substantive conclusion, but we have not proven it.

## 5. Conclusion

In a bounded-confidence opinion-dynamics model whose exposure-bias parameter is calibrated to a real audited amplification ratio (Huszár et al., 2021), this bias parameter has a weak effect on final polarization that is statistically significant in only one of four tested conditions and small even there (variance range ≤0.03 across a 0-to-6 sweep), versus a 0.085-0.100 variance shift from a single change to the population's confidence threshold. This ranking survives a formal permutation test, a 6x higher-power seed rerun, calibration to either end of the audited amplification range, and a change to the update-strength parameter mu, and survives directionally (with a shrinking margin) under a smaller population size N=100. This suggests that, within this model class, interventions aimed at individual-level openness to persuasion may be a more consequential lever on polarization than interventions aimed solely at algorithmic exposure bias — a falsifiable claim we hope motivates calibrated empirical follow-up on real interaction data.
