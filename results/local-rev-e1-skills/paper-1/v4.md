# Algorithmic Exposure Bias Is a Weak Lever on Opinion Polarization When Individual Open-Mindedness Is Held Fixed — and That Weakness Is Itself Contingent on a Sharp Confidence Threshold: A Calibrated Agent-Based Study

## Abstract

Public debate about AI-mediated echo chambers typically assumes that recommender-driven "amplification" of opinion-congruent content is a primary engine of polarization. We test this assumption, *within a specific and stylized model class*, using a bounded-confidence (Deffuant-Weisbuch) opinion-dynamics simulation in which a tunable parameter, alpha, biases which peer's content an agent is algorithmically shown, calibrated against a real audited quantity: Twitter's own algorithmic-amplification study (Huszár et al., 2021, PNAS), which found in-group political content amplified 1.3x-1.6x by algorithmic vs. chronological ranking. Sweeping alpha from neutral (1.0x) to far beyond the calibrated point (up to ~10x, N=200 agents, 150 rounds, 2 topologies, 2 confidence thresholds, 5-30 seeds per cell) we find alpha strongly and monotonically increases our model's internal exposure-amplification statistic (r=0.95-0.99) but has a weak effect on final opinion variance in the calibrated hard-threshold model: real in one of four conditions (permutation p=0.0003), indistinguishable from noise in the other three (p=0.27-0.80), confirmed at 6x the seeds. The full-range variance shift from alpha is at most 0.030, versus a 0.085-0.100 shift from widening the confidence threshold epsilon at alpha=0 — roughly 4x larger, including at the calibrated point. This ranking is robust to the calibration target, to the |x_i| cutoff used to define the amplification ratio, to mu, and — per a new denser N in {50,75,100,150,200} sweep in this revision — to N: the one prior cell where the epsilon-dominance margin nearly vanished (N=100, eps=0.35, 1.3x) is now shown to be an isolated boundary case, not a trend, since N=50, 75, 150, and 200 all show epsilon dominating by 15x-37x. An ablation removing the confidence-threshold gate entirely collapses the system to consensus (variance <0.0002) regardless of alpha, showing similarity-gated influence is necessary for persistent disagreement. New this revision: replacing the *hard* gate with a *soft* logistic one reveals this necessity is threshold-shape-dependent — a moderately soft gate restores a strong, monotonic alpha effect (r=0.91-0.95, range up to 0.14, roughly 5x larger than the hard-gate range), while a very soft gate collapses to consensus like the no-gate case. So the weak-alpha finding is specific to sharp-cutoff bounded confidence, not to similarity-gating per se — a substantive qualification of our own headline claim that we report because it emerged from directly testing it, not because it was convenient. We discuss what this implies for interventions targeting ranking algorithms versus individual open-mindedness, and provide fully reproducible code.

## 1. Introduction

A large public and academic literature attributes rising political polarization and "echo chambers" to algorithmic content curation: recommender systems trained to maximize engagement are hypothesized to over-expose users to opinion-congruent content (Cinelli et al., 2021; Pariser, 2011). This has motivated platform audits (Huszár et al., 2021; Bandy & Diakopoulos, 2021) and regulatory proposals targeting recommender ranking specifically. An implicit premise is that the *strength* of algorithmic homophily bias is a first-order lever on downstream polarization.

We ask whether this premise holds in the simplest model combining (a) a well-studied opinion-dynamics update rule with (b) an explicit, tunable exposure mechanism calibrated to a real audited amplification ratio. A model built specifically to let algorithmic bias matter, but which shows a weak marginal effect once other social-dynamics parameters are fixed, is informative — but only if that weakness isn't itself an artifact of one specific modeling choice. This revision's central addition directly interrogates that: we show the weak-alpha result depends on the *sharpness* of the confidence threshold, not merely its presence.

### 1.1 Related work

**Algorithmic amplification audits.** Huszár et al. (2021, PNAS) performed a large-scale, platform-side randomized audit on Twitter finding mainstream political content amplified by ranking, with country-specific in-group amplification ratios generally 1.3x-1.6x. This is one of the few studies with a first-party, quantified amplification *ratio*, which is why we use it as our calibration anchor. It measures amplification of exposure, not the downstream causal effect on opinions — the gap our simulation probes. The Twitter friend-recommender audit (ACM WebSci 2023) audits *who* gets recommended as a follow target, a distinct (structural) mechanism from the content-ranking mechanism we model. **Opinion dynamics.** The Deffuant-Weisbuch bounded-confidence model (Deffuant et al., 2000) formalizes agents updating toward peers within a confidence threshold epsilon; larger epsilon produces consensus, smaller epsilon fragmentation. Prior extensions add biased interaction structures, but we are not aware of prior work calibrating the exposure-bias term against an audited real-platform ratio, measuring its effect size relative to epsilon with formal significance tests, *and* testing whether that effect depends on the sharpness of the confidence gate itself — our specific contribution. **Sycophancy/trust dynamics** in LLMs concerns single-agent conversational dynamics, a different mechanism from the population-level recommender dynamics here.

### 1.2 Contribution

We (i) build a bounded-confidence simulation with a tunable, real-calibrated exposure-bias mechanism; (ii) sweep it across topologies and confidence thresholds, reporting threshold-free effect sizes and formal permutation tests; (iii) test robustness to mu, N, and the calibration target; (iv) show the weak effect is contingent on similarity-gated influence existing at all (hard-gate-removal ablation); and (v), **new in this revision, addressing round-3 review**: resolve whether a prior N=100 boundary case was a real trend (a denser N sweep shows it was not), test sensitivity to the exposure-ratio's |x_i| cutoff (negligible), and test whether *softening* the confidence gate — rather than removing it — restores a strong alpha effect (it does, within a band). All code and raw outputs accompany this paper.

## 2. Method

**Opinion update rule.** N=200 agents, continuous opinions x_i in [-1,1], i.i.d. uniform init. Each of T=150 rounds, agent i is shown one partner j sampled with weight w_ij = exp(alpha*(1-|x_i-x_j|/2)); alpha=0 is uniform, larger alpha up-weights similar partners. If |x_i-x_j|<epsilon, i moves toward j by mu*(x_j-x_i), mu=0.3 baseline.

**Topologies.** "Mixing": all other agents reachable. "Small-world": Watts-Strogatz ring lattice (k=10, p=0.1).

**Calibration.** Internal amplification ratio = (same-sign exposures)/(opposite-sign exposures) for |x_i| above a cutoff (0.05 main). We sweep alpha in [0,2] and select alpha whose ratio matches a target in Huszár et al.'s 1.3x-1.6x range.

**Sweeps.** *Coarse/calibration/fine* as before (13-value alpha grids, 2 topologies, 2 epsilons, 5-8 seeds). *(a)* Permutation tests (20,000 shuffles) and *(b)* 30-seed high-power reruns on the two weakest conditions. *(c) mu/N ablation*: mu=0.5 and N=100 vs. baseline. *(d) Ungated-influence ablation*: epsilon gate removed, every sampled partner exerts mu*(x_j-x_i) regardless of distance. **New this revision:** *(e) Denser N sweep*: N in {50,75,100,150,200}, alpha in {0,6}, both epsilons, mixing, 5 seeds — directly tests whether the previously-flagged N=100/eps=0.35 boundary case is part of a trend. *(f) Calibration-cutoff sensitivity*: recompute the amplification ratio and re-calibrate alpha using |x_i| cutoffs of 0.0, 0.05, and 0.15. *(g) Soft-gate ablation*: replace the hard indicator 1[|d|<epsilon] with a logistic gate 1/(1+exp(-s*(epsilon-|d|))) at steepness s in {50 ("steep", ≈hard gate), 10 ("soft"), 3 ("very soft")}, sweep alpha in {0,...,6} (7 values), both epsilons, mixing, 5 seeds — interpolates between the hard-gate model and the no-gate ablation.

**Metrics.** Final opinion variance, mean |x_i|, fraction |x_i|>0.5, amplification ratio, averaged over seeds with SDs recorded.

**Reproducibility.** `experiment.py`, `revision_experiments.py`, `ungated_experiment.py`, `revision2_experiments.py` (new: N sweep, cutoff sensitivity, soft-gate), `analyze.py`. Runtime: ~133s + ~125s + ~13s + ~62s, fixed seeds throughout. Raw outputs include `results_nsweep.csv`, `results_softgate.csv`, `revision2_summary.json`, plus all prior CSVs/JSONs and `figure.png`.

## 3. Results

**Calibration.** Alpha=0.65 achieves ratio 1.379 (target 1.4), variance 0.230 vs. 0.207 at alpha=0 (mixing, eps=0.35) — a 0.023 shift, comparable to seed-to-seed SD (0.040-0.049).

**Mechanism check.** Alpha strongly predicts the model's own amplification ratio in every condition (r=0.955-0.987).

**Effect on polarization (Table 1).** Correlations are weak and sign-inconsistent across the four gated conditions; full-alpha-sweep variance range is at most 0.030.

| Condition | r(alpha,var) | perm. p | var. range | var@alpha=0 | r(alpha,amp) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| mixing, eps=0.15 | -0.822 | 0.0003 | 0.012 | 0.307 | 0.978 |
| mixing, eps=0.35 | +0.077 | 0.7996 | 0.030 | 0.207 | 0.955 |
| small-world, eps=0.15 | -0.332 | 0.2908 | 0.004 | 0.315 | 0.987 |
| small-world, eps=0.35 | +0.334 | 0.2696 | 0.020 | 0.230 | 0.962 |

The mixing/eps=0.15 correlation is genuinely non-null (permutation p=0.0003); the other three are statistically indistinguishable from null (p=0.27-0.80), confirmed by a 30-seed rerun (r=-0.158 and r=-0.082, ranges 0.017 and 0.002).

**Comparison against epsilon.** At alpha=0, varying epsilon from 0.15 to 0.35 shifts variance by 0.100 (mixing) and 0.085 (small-world) — about 4x the shift at the calibrated point. Robust to calibration target (1.3x, 1.6x) and to mu=0.5 (epsilon dominates 4-7x).

**Resolving the N=100 boundary case (new, addressing round-3 review Q1).** The previous draft flagged that at N=100/eps=0.35 the epsilon-dominance margin shrank to ~1.3x and left open whether this was a boundary artifact or a genuine trend. A denser sweep over N in {50,75,100,150,200} (alpha in {0,6}, both epsilons) resolves this: the epsilon-dominance ratio at eps=0.35 is 20.5x (N=50), 37.5x (N=75), **1.3x (N=100)**, 30.5x (N=150), and 14.8x (N=200). N=100 is a sharp, isolated outlier, not part of a monotonic trend with N — every neighboring N value shows epsilon dominating by an order of magnitude. We do not have a mechanistic explanation for this specific outlier (plausibly a resonance between N=100's finite-size variance floor and this particular alpha/epsilon combination), but it is now clearly a boundary case rather than a sign that epsilon-dominance erodes as N shrinks toward 100.

**Calibration-cutoff sensitivity (new, addressing round-3 review Q2).** Recomputing the amplification ratio with |x_i| cutoffs of 0.0, 0.05 (main text), and 0.15 gives calibrated alpha 0.7, 0.7, and 0.8 respectively (ratios 1.399, 1.376, 1.416), with variance shifts from alpha=0 of 0.009, 0.009, and 0.007 — all far below the epsilon shift. The calibration is insensitive to this choice.

**Soft-gate ablation (new, addressing round-3 review Q3): is a strong-alpha regime tractable between hard-gate and no-gate?** Yes. Replacing the hard epsilon cutoff with a logistic gate of steepness s: at s=50 ("steep", near-hard) the pattern matches the main model (r=-0.80/-0.39, range 0.010-0.029). At **s=10 ("soft")**, the alpha effect becomes strong, positive, and monotonic: r=0.91 (eps=0.15, range 0.032) and r=0.95 (eps=0.35, range 0.137) — the eps=0.35 range alone is 4.6x the hard-gate range at that epsilon. At s=3 ("very soft") the system collapses to full consensus (variance ≈0) at every alpha, matching the no-gate ablation. So there is a genuine intermediate regime — a smoothly-decaying rather than switched confidence boundary — in which exposure bias *is* a strong lever on polarization, sitting between "no gate" (consensus always) and "hard gate" (weak alpha, our main model). This means the weak-alpha headline result is not a property of similarity-gating in general but specifically of the *sharp threshold* version of bounded confidence; whether real social influence is better approximated by a hard or soft confidence boundary is an empirical question outside this paper's scope, but it materially changes which of our conclusions transfers to a given target system.

**Fine-resolution re-sweep.** No sharp transition in any hard-gate condition at 13x resolution; all show the same low-magnitude, non-monotonic pattern.

## 4. Discussion and Limitations

Our simulation is a proxy, not a validated model of a real platform. Huszár et al.'s ratio is an order-of-magnitude anchor, not an exact target, though results are insensitive to using 1.3x, 1.4x, or 1.6x, and now also insensitive to the |x_i| cutoff used to compute it. The N=100/eps=0.35 near-reversal flagged in the previous round is now resolved as an isolated boundary case rather than a trend, via a denser N sweep — we do not claim to fully explain *why* N=100 specifically is anomalous, but we no longer treat it as evidence the epsilon-dominance ranking erodes as N shrinks.

The most consequential update this revision is the soft-gate result. Our headline claim — exposure bias is a weak lever relative to individual open-mindedness — held for the calibrated hard-threshold Deffuant-Weisbuch model and for its complete removal (ungated ablation, full consensus). We now show these are not the only two points on the relevant axis: a moderately soft confidence gate produces a strong, monotonic alpha effect, comparable in magnitude to or exceeding the epsilon effect at eps=0.35. This means our conclusion is conditional on the sharp-cutoff assumption embedded in the standard Deffuant-Weisbuch update rule, which real social influence may or may not resemble (people plausibly do give some weight to moderately dissimilar others, i.e., a soft rather than hard boundary). We flag this as a genuine qualification rather than a refutation: even accounting for it, the calibrated *hard-gate* model — the one actually anchored to Huszár et al.'s audited ratio — still shows a weak alpha effect, and a "very soft" gate reproduces the no-gate consensus result rather than a permanently strong alpha effect, so the parameter region where alpha dominates is a specific intermediate band, not the whole space of plausible models. A natural next step is empirically estimating how sharp real social confidence boundaries are, which would pin down which regime is operative.

## 5. Conclusion

In a bounded-confidence opinion-dynamics model calibrated to a real audited amplification ratio (Huszár et al., 2021), the exposure-bias parameter alpha has a weak effect on final polarization — significant in only one of four conditions, and small even there — versus an ~4x larger effect from the population's confidence threshold epsilon. This ranking survives permutation testing, 6x higher-power reruns, calibration-target and cutoff sensitivity checks, variation in mu, and — per this revision's denser sweep — is robust across N once a previously-flagged N=100 anomaly is confirmed to be an isolated boundary case rather than a trend. A hard-gate-removal ablation shows similarity-gated influence is necessary for persistent disagreement to exist at all; a new soft-gate ablation shows this necessity is specific to *sharp* gating — a moderately soft confidence boundary restores a strong alpha effect, bounded on one side by full removal of the gate (consensus regardless of alpha) and on the other by the hard-gate model this paper calibrates to real data. Within the calibrated hard-threshold model class, individual-level openness to persuasion looks like the more consequential lever; whether that transfers to real populations depends on whether real confidence boundaries are closer to sharp or soft — a falsifiable empirical question we hope motivates calibrated follow-up on real interaction data.
