# Coverage-Efficiency Trade-offs of Conformal Next-Token Prediction Sets Under Temperature Miscalibration and Distribution Shift

## Abstract

Conformal prediction gives large language models distribution-free coverage guarantees over next-token prediction sets, unlike raw softmax thresholding. We build a fully controlled synthetic testbed — a small LSTM trained on sequences from a known order-2 Markov chain — that lets us compare an uncalibrated nucleus-style ("naive top-p") baseline against two split-conformal set constructors, LAC and APS, exactly against ground truth. At 90% nominal coverage, calibration insulates coverage from temperature miscalibration: naive top-p swings from 77.1% (T=0.5) to 96.5% (T=3.0), while LAC and APS stay within 0.7 and 0.4 points of each other. Under covariate shift, all three methods degrade smoothly — no threshold effect — and, at matched nominal alpha, unevenly: over 5 seeds, mean coverage drop is 18.5% (naive), 20.5% (LAC), 16.8% (APS), an ordering that holds in every seed. However, a matched-average-set-size re-comparison — recalibrating LAC's alpha so its set size equals APS's rather than matching nominal alpha — shows this gap is largely a set-size artifact: over 3 seeds the drops become 16.1% (LAC) vs. 16.6% (APS), a 0.5-point gap versus the original 3.7-point gap. We also test the deterministic APS test-time rule against the fully randomized Romano et al. (2020) rule (it over-covers by ~2 points and yields ~1 extra token, a stable conservative bias) and directly test our own proposed shift-robustness mechanism (LAC's score erodes faster under shift) by comparing calibration-score distributions before and after shift — we find the opposite of what we hypothesized, and flag the mechanism as unresolved. A calibration-set-size ablation shows both conformal methods overcover when calibration data is scarce, converging toward nominal as calibration size grows. The headline result of this revision is a self-correction: what looked like a score-function-intrinsic shift-robustness difference between LAC and APS is mostly explained by APS's larger sets at matched alpha, not by a property of the score function itself.

## 1. Introduction

LLM next-token softmax is well known to be miscalibrated, and simple decoding heuristics like top-p sampling provide no statistical guarantee that the realized token lands in the returned set (Guo et al., 2017; Jiang et al., 2021). *Uncertainty-estimation* methods such as semantic entropy (Kuhn et al., 2023; Farquhar et al., 2024, *Nature*; Kuhn et al., 2024) flag likely hallucinations but give no coverage guarantee. *Conformal prediction* (Vovk et al., 2005; Angelopoulos & Bates, 2023) instead calibrates a threshold from exchangeable held-out data so that a prediction set covers the true label with probability at least $1-\alpha$ marginally, regardless of model quality.

Romano et al. (2020) introduced Adaptive Prediction Sets (APS), conformalizing the cumulative-softmax rule; Quach et al. (2024) extend conformal calibration to full sequences; Ulmer et al. (2024) study non-exchangeable calibration via nearest-neighbor reweighting. A 2025 study on conformal next-token sets in transformer LLMs (arXiv:2512.22682) shows naive full-vocabulary conformal sets are large and proposes vocabulary-aware masking; CoVeR (arXiv:2509.04733) targets a related coverage-efficiency trade-off for decoding trajectories. These works confirm the coverage guarantee holds (by construction) and focus on shrinking sets, largely in-distribution. Less studied is how *score-function choice* (LAC vs. APS) and *calibration-set size* interact with temperature changes and distribution shift, which breaks the exchangeability the guarantee relies on.

We build a synthetic setting — a known order-2 Markov chain over a 24-token vocabulary, sampled by a deliberately undertrained small LSTM — giving exact, cheap, reproducible coverage evaluation against ground truth, full control over shift, and a realistically imperfect (not saturated) softmax. This is a controlled analogue of the transformer-scale setting other papers study, isolating calibration-size, temperature, and shift axes usually varied one at a time (or not at all) in the applied LLM-conformal literature. This revision also directly probes a mechanism we speculated about but did not test in the first version, and finds our own hypothesis does not survive testing — we report that honestly rather than smoothing it over.

## 2. Method

**Data-generating process.** Vocabulary $V=24$, order-2 Markov chain (576 contexts); each context's transition kernel is drawn once from Dirichlet($\alpha=0.3$, $+6.0$ on 3 random "favored" tokens), fixed for the whole experiment (seed 0). We sample 1500 train, 800 calibration, 800/500 test sequences of length 30.

**Model.** Single-layer LSTM (embedding 32, hidden 64, ~13K params), 8 epochs, Adam lr $2\times10^{-3}$, batch 64. Converges to 2.72 nats (vs. $\ln 24=3.18$ uniform) — informative but deliberately not saturated.

**Prediction-set constructors.** *Naive top-p*: smallest set (by descending softmax) reaching cumulative probability $1-\alpha$, uncalibrated. *LAC* (Sadinle et al., 2019): score $s=1-\hat p(y_\text{true})$, calibrated at the $\lceil(n+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil/n$ quantile, include tokens with $\hat p \ge 1-\hat q$. *APS* (Romano et al., 2020): score is cumulative softmax mass up to the true token's rank, randomized at calibration time; test-time inclusion in the original run uses the deterministic approximation `cum_before < qhat` (flagged in v1's code comments). $\alpha=0.10$ throughout.

**Stressors.** *Temperature* $T\in\{0.5,...,3.0\}$, applied to calibration and test consistently. *Shift*: kernel linearly interpolated toward uniform, $K_\text{shift}=(1-s)K+s\cdot\text{Uniform}$, calibration always at $s=0$. *Calibration size*: 10–800 sequences (290–23,200 token points).

**New in this revision (answering round-1 review questions).** (i) *Randomized APS*: we implement the fully randomized Romano et al. boundary-inclusion rule (include the boundary token with probability $(\hat q - \text{cum\_before})/p_\text{boundary}$) and compare it to the original deterministic rule, in- and out-of-distribution. (ii) *Score-distribution shift test*: we directly compute, at each shift level, the mean/std of the LAC score ($1-\hat p(y)$) and the (derandomized, expectation-over-$u$) APS score, normalized by their own calibration-time std, to test our v1 hypothesis that LAC's score erodes faster under shift. (iii) *Matched-set-size comparison*: for each of 3 seeds, we bisection-search an alpha for LAC so its average in-distribution set size matches APS's set size at $\alpha=0.10$, then rerun the $s{=}0\to s{=}1$ shift-robustness comparison at this matched size instead of matched alpha. All new code is in `follow_up.py`, deterministic, reusing the identical generator/model/calibration routines as `experiment.py`/`multiseed_check.py`; raw output is in `follow_up_log.txt`.

## 3. Results

**3.1 In-distribution vs. shifted, fixed temperature.** At $T=1$, in-distribution: naive 91.2% (size 17.1), LAC 89.7% (15.99), APS 92.3% (17.76). Under moderate shift ($s=0.5$): naive 80.7%, LAC 77.6%, APS 82.4% — all lose 9–12 points, since shift violates the exchangeability conformal coverage requires.

**3.2 Temperature ablation.** Naive coverage ranges 77.1%–96.5% across $T\in[0.5,3.0]$ (19.4 points), since a fixed cumulative cutoff targets the wrong quantile once softmax is temperature-distorted. LAC ranges 89.5–89.7%, APS 92.2–92.3% — essentially temperature-invariant, a direct consequence of the split-conformal guarantee (calibration and test share temperature, so exchangeability holds regardless of distortion). We report this as an implementation validation, not a novel finding. Set size tracks the same pattern: naive roughly doubles (10.4→20.9), LAC (15.8–16.1) and APS (17.6–17.9) stay flat.

**3.3 Distribution-shift sweep and the matched-alpha LAC/APS gap.** Coverage declines smoothly with shift severity for all three methods (naive 91.1%→72.3%; LAC 89.5%→68.2%; APS 92.0%→75.2%, $s{:}0\to1$) — no threshold effect. Over 5 seeds, mean coverage drop ($s{:}0\to1$) is 18.5%±0.3 (naive), 20.5%±0.9 (LAC), 16.8%±0.7 (APS); the ordering APS < naive < LAC holds in every seed, gaps (1.7–3.7 pts) exceeding cross-seed std (≤0.9 pts).

**3.4 Is the LAC/APS gap a score-function property or a set-size artifact? (new)** LAC's sets are smaller than APS's at matched $\alpha=0.10$ (16.0 vs. 17.8 tokens at $T{=}1$), so part of LAC's larger coverage drop could simply be that smaller sets have less slack to absorb shift, unrelated to which quantity the score function conditions on. We tested this directly: for 3 seeds, we recalibrated LAC's $\alpha$ (via bisection) so its average in-distribution set size matches APS's set size at nominal $\alpha=0.10$ (matched sizes: seed 0, 17.80 vs. 17.79; seed 1, 18.32 vs. 18.31; seed 2, 18.26 vs. 18.27 — matches to within 0.01 tokens). At this matched size, the coverage-drop gap nearly disappears: LAC 16.10%±0.53 vs. APS 16.58%±0.56 (per-seed: 16.82/17.38, 15.57/16.17, 15.91/16.20), a 0.5-point gap versus the 3.7-point gap at matched alpha. **This substantially revises our v1 headline claim**: most of what we reported as "LAC is intrinsically less shift-robust than APS" in Section 3.3 is explained by APS's larger nominal set size at $\alpha=0.10$, not by a property of the score function itself. A small residual gap (APS still drops marginally more, if anything, at matched size) remains but is within noise at this seed count.

**3.5 Testing the proposed mechanism directly (new).** In v1 we hypothesized LAC degrades faster because its score depends only on mass at the single true token — "exactly what shift corrupts first" — while APS's cumulative-rank score erodes more gradually. We tested this by tracking each score's distribution across the shift sweep, normalized by its own calibration-time std. From $s{=}0$ to $s{=}1$, LAC's score mean moves 0.680 calibration-stds (0.9151→0.9586 in raw score units) while APS's moves *more*, 0.847 stds (0.4472→0.6956). This is the opposite of our hypothesized ordering — the APS score distribution moves more, not less, under shift. Combined with §3.4, we now believe the coverage-drop gap at matched alpha was driven mainly by LAC's smaller set size (fixed $\alpha$ pins LAC to a higher, sharper quantile with less room to absorb any score-distribution movement) rather than by differential erosion rates of the two score functions. We withdraw the mechanism proposed in v1 as unsupported by direct testing; the true mechanism is likely primarily about calibrated set size, per §3.4.

**3.6 Deterministic vs. randomized APS test-time rule (new).** Answering a direct implementation question from review: the deterministic rule we used throughout (`cum_before < qhat`, no randomization at the boundary token) is a *conservative* approximation of Romano et al.'s fully randomized rule. In-distribution: deterministic 92.21% coverage / 17.74 mean size vs. randomized 90.53% / 16.74 — almost exactly nominal once randomization is added, at one token smaller. Under $s{=}0.5$ shift: deterministic 82.34%/17.86 vs. randomized 79.48%/16.86. The bias is a stable ~2-point over-coverage and ~1-token size inflation across both conditions, so it does not change the qualitative shift results in §3.3–3.4 (which compare APS to itself and to LAC under the same deterministic rule throughout) but means our absolute APS coverage/size numbers should be read as slightly conservative versions of the theoretically exact APS.

**3.7 Calibration-set-size ablation.** With scarce calibration data (290 points, 10 sequences), both conformal methods overcover: LAC 93.1%, APS 95.4%, shrinking monotonically as calibration size grows (LAC 93.1→89.7%, APS 95.4→92.3%, at 290→23,200 points) — the expected finite-sample conservativeness of the $\lceil(n+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil/n$ quantile. APS stays 2–3 points above LAC at every size, tracking its larger set size (19.9→17.8 vs. 18.0→16.0).

## 4. Discussion and Conclusion

Split-conformal calibration removes naive top-p's strong temperature sensitivity, as guaranteed by theory (§3.2). Under genuine distribution shift, exchangeability breaks and all three methods lose 17–21 points of coverage (§3.3). In v1 we reported that LAC is reliably less shift-robust than APS, and even than the uncalibrated baseline; this revision shows that claim was substantially confounded by set size. Once LAC is recalibrated to match APS's average set size, the robustness gap shrinks from 3.7 to 0.5 points (§3.4) — mostly a set-size story, not a score-function-identity story. Our own candidate mechanism for the original gap (LAC's single-token score erodes faster) is directly contradicted by the score-distribution data (§3.5): APS's score moves more under shift, not less. We also confirm the standard finite-sample overcoverage of conformal quantiles at small calibration budgets (§3.7), and quantify the deterministic-APS-rule bias as a stable, small conservative offset (§3.6) that does not change qualitative comparisons.

**Limitations.** Vocabulary (24 tokens), model (13K-parameter LSTM), and generating process (order-2 Markov chain) are far smaller than production LLMs; we trade scale for exact ground truth and reproducibility. The shift mechanism (interpolation toward uniform) is one simple form among many. We tested two score functions (LAC, APS); more recent variants were out of scope. The matched-set-size comparison (§3.4) and score-distribution test (§3.5) use 3 seeds and 1 seed respectively (vs. 5 for the main shift-robustness claim), reflecting this revision's compute budget — the qualitative conclusion (gap shrinks sharply at matched size) is consistent across the 3 seeds we ran, but a wider seed sweep would tighten the residual-gap estimate. As in v1, this paper dissects existing score functions rather than proposing a new one; its contribution is the controlled, ground-truth-exact comparison — including, in this revision, catching and correcting our own initial over-interpretation of an alpha-matched (rather than size-matched) comparison.

**Conclusion.** For practitioners choosing between LAC and APS, the practical lesson has changed from v1: differences observed at matched nominal $\alpha$ between score functions under shift are not simply properties of "APS vs. LAC" — they are substantially explained by the fact that matched-alpha calibration gives the two methods different set sizes, and smaller-set methods absorb shift-induced coverage loss worse regardless of which score function produced the small set. Practitioners comparing conformal score functions for robustness should control for set size, not just nominal alpha, or risk attributing a set-size effect to a score-function property, as we did in the first version of this study.
