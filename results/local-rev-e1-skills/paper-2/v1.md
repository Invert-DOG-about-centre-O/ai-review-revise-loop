# Coverage-Efficiency Trade-offs of Conformal Next-Token Prediction Sets Under Temperature Miscalibration and Distribution Shift

## Abstract

Conformal prediction has recently been proposed as a way to give large language models distribution-free coverage guarantees over next-token prediction sets, in contrast to raw softmax thresholding, which has no such guarantee. We build a fully controlled synthetic testbed — a small LSTM trained on sequences from a known order-2 Markov chain — that lets us compare an uncalibrated nucleus-style ("naive top-p") baseline against two split-conformal set constructors, LAC (thresholding on the true class's softmax score) and APS (adaptive prediction sets), exactly against ground truth. At the nominal target of 90% coverage, calibration successfully insulates prediction-set coverage from temperature miscalibration: naive top-p coverage swings from 77.1% at T=0.5 to 96.5% at T=3.0, while LAC and APS stay within 0.7 and 0.4 percentage points of each other across the same range. Under covariate shift (interpolating the transition kernel toward uniform), all three methods degrade smoothly with shift severity — no threshold effect — but not uniformly: over 5 seeds, mean coverage drop from no-shift to full-shift is 18.5% for naive, 20.5% for LAC, and 16.8% for APS, i.e., LAC is consistently *less* shift-robust than the uncalibrated baseline, while APS is consistently *more* robust, by margins that exceed cross-seed noise (std ≤ 0.9 points). A calibration-set-size ablation (10-800 calibration sequences) shows both conformal methods overcover when calibration data is scarce (up to +5.7 points at n=290 calibration tokens) and converge toward nominal as calibration size grows. Our results confirm the textbook guarantee that conformal calibration is temperature-invariant at coverage time, but show this guarantee does not translate into uniform shift-robustness gains across conformal score functions — the choice between LAC and APS matters more under shift than in-distribution, a nuance obscured when these methods are evaluated only in-distribution.

## 1. Introduction

Large language models emit a probability distribution over the next token, but that distribution is well known to be miscalibrated: raw softmax confidence does not reliably predict correctness, and simple decoding heuristics like top-p (nucleus) sampling with a fixed cumulative-probability cutoff provide no statistical guarantee that the realized token is contained in the returned set (Guo et al., 2017; Jiang et al., 2021). Two broad families of fixes have emerged. The first estimates *uncertainty* — e.g., semantic entropy, which clusters sampled generations by meaning and measures entropy over meaning-clusters rather than token strings, and its cheaper "probe" variant that regresses semantic entropy from hidden states of a single forward pass (Kuhn et al., 2023; Farquhar et al., 2024, *Nature*; Kuhn et al., 2024). These methods flag when a model is likely to hallucinate but do not themselves produce a set with a coverage guarantee. The second family adapts *conformal prediction* (Vovk et al., 2005; Angelopoulos & Bates, 2023), a distribution-free calibration technique, to language generation: given exchangeable calibration data, conformal methods choose a threshold so that a prediction set covers the true label with probability at least $1-\alpha$ marginally, regardless of model quality.

Recent work applies conformal ideas directly to next-token prediction. Romano et al. (2020) introduced Adaptive Prediction Sets (APS), which conformalize the cumulative-softmax rule and generalize nucleus sampling to use a calibrated, rather than fixed, cutoff. Quach et al. (2024) extend conformal calibration to full generated sequences with stopping rules ("Conformal Language Modeling"). Ulmer et al. (2024) study conformal prediction under non-exchangeable calibration data via nearest-neighbor reweighting, directly addressing distribution shift. A 2025 study on conformal next-token prediction sets in transformer LLMs (arXiv:2512.22682) documents that naive conformal calibration on full-vocabulary logits produces sets of hundreds of tokens, and proposes vocabulary-aware masking (VACP) to shrink sets to single digits while preserving coverage; CoVeR (arXiv:2509.04733) targets a related coverage-efficiency trade-off for decoding trajectories. These papers consistently report the *coverage* guarantee holding (by construction of split conformal prediction) but focus on making sets smaller (*efficiency*), largely in-distribution. Less studied — and our focus — is how the *choice of conformal score function* (LAC vs. APS) and *calibration-set size* interact with two deployment stressors: (a) temperature/sampling-parameter changes at inference, and (b) distribution shift between calibration and deployment corpora, which breaks the exchangeability assumption underlying the coverage guarantee.

Real LLM vocabularies and corpora make it impossible to know the *true* next-token distribution, so prior empirical studies can only measure coverage relative to the one observed continuation, not to ground truth. We instead build a synthetic setting — sequences from a known, fixed order-2 Markov chain over a 24-token vocabulary — and train a deliberately undertrained small LSTM on it. This gives (i) an imperfect, realistically miscalibrated softmax (the LSTM does not recover the true chain exactly), (ii) full control over distribution shift (the true kernel can be analytically interpolated toward uniform), and (iii) exact, cheap, reproducible evaluation of coverage and set size. This is a controlled analogue of the transformer-scale setting other papers study, not a replacement for it; our contribution is a clean, exactly-reproducible dissection of *when* conformal calibration helps and *how much*, isolating calibration-set-size, temperature, and shift axes that are usually varied one at a time or not at all in the applied LLM-conformal literature.

## 2. Method

**Data-generating process.** We define a vocabulary of size $V=24$ and an order-2 ($k=2$) Markov chain: for every context of the last two tokens (576 contexts), the next-token distribution is drawn once from a Dirichlet($\alpha=0.3$ for all tokens, $+6.0$ added to 3 randomly chosen "favored" tokens per context) to produce peaked-but-not-deterministic transition kernels, fixed for the whole experiment (seed 0). We sample 1500 training, 800 calibration, and 800/500 test sequences of length 30 from this chain.

**Model.** A single-layer LSTM (embedding dim 32, hidden dim 64, ~13K parameters) is trained for 8 epochs (Adam, lr $2\times10^{-3}$, batch 64) with next-token cross-entropy. Training converges to a loss of 2.72 nats (vs. $\ln 24 = 3.18$ for a uniform model), i.e., the model is informative but *not* close to the entropy of the true generator — this is intentional, giving realistically imperfect probability estimates rather than a saturated fit, and training/inference for the full pipeline (see below) takes well under one minute on CPU.

**Prediction-set constructors.** (1) *Naive top-p*: the smallest set of tokens (sorted by descending softmax probability) whose cumulative probability reaches $1-\alpha$, computed directly from the model's own softmax — no calibration. (2) *LAC* (least ambiguous set-valued classifier; Sadinle et al., 2019): nonconformity score $s = 1 - \hat p(y_\text{true})$; calibrate a threshold $\hat q$ as the $\lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil/n$ empirical quantile of calibration scores, then include all tokens with $\hat p \ge 1-\hat q$ at test time. (3) *APS* (Romano et al., 2020): nonconformity score is the cumulative softmax mass up to and including the true token's rank, randomized at the boundary; calibrated the same way, then applied at test time. We use $\alpha = 0.10$ (nominal 90% coverage) throughout. All three methods are evaluated by exact set membership against the token actually realized by the underlying (possibly shifted) Markov chain, aggregated over all sequence positions.

**Stressors.** *Temperature*: logits divided by $T \in \{0.5, 0.8, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 3.0\}$ before softmax, applied consistently to calibration and test data for a given $T$ (the LAC/APS calibration thresholds are recomputed for each $T$; the naive method needs no calibration by construction). *Distribution shift*: the true transition kernel is linearly interpolated toward the uniform distribution, $K_\text{shift} = (1-s)K + s \cdot \text{Uniform}$, for $s \in \{0, 0.1, ..., 0.5, 0.7, 1.0\}$; calibration is always performed on unshifted ($s=0$) data, and test sequences are drawn from the shifted kernel, breaking calibration/test exchangeability by construction. *Calibration-set size*: 10 to 800 calibration sequences (290 to 23,200 token-level calibration points).

All code (`experiment.py`, `multiseed_check.py`) is deterministic (fixed seeds throughout) and reproduces the numbers below exactly; raw output is saved in `run_log.txt` / `results.json`.

## 3. Results

**3.1 In-distribution vs. shifted, fixed temperature.** At $T=1$, in-distribution: naive coverage 91.2% (set size 17.1), LAC 89.7% (15.99), APS 92.3% (17.76) — all near the 90% target, since the (undertrained but not adversarial) model's softmax happens to be reasonably well-matched to nominal coverage even uncalibrated. Under moderate shift ($s=0.5$): naive drops to 80.7%, LAC to 77.6%, APS to 82.4% — all three lose 9-12 points, confirming that shifting the calibration/test relationship degrades every method, including the conformal ones, since conformal coverage requires exchangeability that shift violates.

**3.2 Temperature ablation (Table 1, in-distribution).** Naive top-p coverage is highly sensitive to temperature: 77.1% (T=0.5) → 88.4% (T=0.8) → 91.2% (T=1.0) → 94.3% (T=1.5) → 95.4% (T=2.0) → 96.5% (T=3.0), a 19.4-point range, because a fixed cumulative-probability cutoff targets the wrong quantile once the softmax is temperature-distorted. Both conformal methods are essentially temperature-invariant: LAC ranges 89.5%-89.7% and APS ranges 92.2%-92.3% across the same 6 temperatures — a direct, expected consequence of the split-conformal marginal-coverage guarantee, since calibration and test data share the same temperature and remain exchangeable regardless of how distorted the softmax is (Angelopoulos & Bates, 2023). We report this as a validation of our implementation, not a novel finding. Set size tracks the efficiency side: naive set size roughly doubles from 10.4 (T=0.5) to 20.9 (T=3.0), while LAC (15.8-16.1) and APS (17.6-17.9) stay nearly flat — conformal calibration absorbs temperature distortion into the threshold rather than the set size.

**3.3 Distribution-shift sweep (Table 2, 8-point grid, $T=1$).** Coverage declines smoothly and monotonically with shift severity for all three methods (naive: 91.1% → 72.3%; LAC: 89.5% → 68.2%; APS: 92.0% → 75.2%, from $s=0$ to $s=1$). We used this finer 8-point grid specifically to rule out a discontinuity or sharp threshold, and found none — the decline is gradual, consistent with a slowly-growing exchangeability violation rather than a phase transition. Set sizes are comparatively insensitive to shift (naive 17.16→17.33, LAC 16.03→16.38, APS 17.78→17.96), so shift mainly costs coverage, not size.

At face value, LAC has both the *worst* coverage and the *largest* coverage drop under shift — counter to the intuition that conformal calibration should make things more robust. To rule out a single-seed artifact, we reran the full pipeline (fresh data draw, LSTM, calibration) for 5 independent seeds and measured the coverage drop from $s=0$ to $s=1$ (Table 3): naive 18.5% ± 0.3 pts, LAC 20.5% ± 0.9 pts, APS 16.8% ± 0.7 pts. The ordering APS < naive < LAC holds in every one of the 5 seeds, and the gaps between methods (1.7-3.7 points) exceed the cross-seed standard deviations (≤0.9 points), so this is not sampling noise. A plausible (but unverified) mechanism: LAC's score, $1-\hat p(y)$, is calibrated almost entirely on the *mass on the single true token*, exactly what shift corrupts first (peaked contexts lose their peak first), whereas APS's cumulative-rank score is calibrated against the *shape* of the whole sorted distribution, which erodes more gradually. We flag this as a hypothesis, not an established finding.

**3.4 Calibration-set-size ablation (Table 4, in-distribution, $T=1$).** With little calibration data (290 token-level points, 10 sequences), both conformal methods overcover relative to the 90% target: LAC 93.1%, APS 95.4%. Overcoverage shrinks monotonically as calibration size grows (LAC: 93.1→92.1→90.8→90.5→89.9→89.7%; APS: 95.4→94.4→93.3→92.9→92.6→92.3%, at 290/725/1450/2900/5800/23200 points), consistent with the finite-sample conservativeness of the $\lceil(n+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil/n$ conformal quantile, which is provably $\ge 1-\alpha$ but converges to $1-\alpha$ only as $n\to\infty$. APS stays 2-3 points above LAC at every calibration size, tracking its consistently larger set size (19.9→17.8 vs. 18.0→16.0).

## 4. Discussion and Conclusion

In a fully controlled synthetic setting with a known ground-truth next-token distribution, we confirm that split-conformal calibration (LAC, APS) removes the strong sensitivity of naive top-p prediction sets to temperature/sampling-parameter changes — as guaranteed by conformal theory, since calibration and test distributions remain exchangeable across a temperature change applied uniformly to both. However, this invariance does not extend to genuine distribution shift, where exchangeability is violated by construction: all three methods lose 17-21 points of coverage under full shift, and — the one result here that is not a direct consequence of the conformal guarantee and required a 5-seed robustness check to trust — the *specific* conformal score function matters: LAC is reliably the *least* shift-robust of the three methods we tested, while APS is the *most* robust, a distinction invisible if these methods are only compared in-distribution (as most reported LLM-conformal coverage numbers currently are). We also confirm the standard finite-sample overcoverage of conformal quantiles at small calibration budgets (up to +5.7 points at 290 calibration points here), which matters in practice since production LLM deployments often calibrate on limited, curated held-out sets.

**Limitations.** Our vocabulary (24 tokens), model (13K-parameter LSTM), and generating process (order-2 Markov chain) are far smaller than a production LLM and its natural-language distribution; we intentionally traded scale for exact ground truth and full reproducibility. The shift mechanism (interpolation toward uniform) is one specific, simple form of distribution shift among many; other shift types (e.g., new vocabulary, longer-range dependency changes) may show different score-function orderings. We tested only two conformal score functions (LAC, APS); more recent variants (e.g., vocabulary-aware masking, non-exchangeable reweighting) that directly target our stressors were out of scope for this compute budget and are natural next steps. Our finding on the LAC/APS shift-robustness gap is empirical and correlational; we offer a candidate mechanism but do not verify it directly.

**Conclusion.** For practitioners choosing between conformal score functions for LLM next-token prediction sets, our results suggest that in-distribution coverage/efficiency comparisons alone are insufficient: score functions that look interchangeable in-distribution (LAC and APS were both within 3 points of nominal coverage and of each other pre-shift) can diverge by several points once realistic distribution shift is introduced, and the direction of that divergence (APS more robust, LAC less robust than even the uncalibrated baseline) is not obvious from the conformal guarantee itself, which only promises validity under exchangeability — not the *rate* at which validity degrades once exchangeability breaks.
