# Correlated Errors, Not Correlated Algorithms: A Simulation of the Homogenization–Accuracy Tradeoff Under Randomized Decorrelation

## Abstract

When many independent institutions delegate decisions to algorithms whose errors are correlated — because the algorithms share training data, vendors, or model families — individuals can face the same fate everywhere they apply. This "algorithmic monoculture" effect is theoretically established (Kleinberg & Raghavan, 2021) and has recently been documented empirically in hiring pipelines at scale (Bommasani et al., 2026). What is less well quantified is *how much* decorrelation a simple, deployable mitigation buys, and at what accuracy cost. We build a controlled simulation of *K* institutions independently screening a shared applicant pool, where each institution's noisy score is a mixture of a shared error component (weight √ρ) and an idiosyncratic one (weight √(1−ρ)). A decision-correlation metric (H) increases with ρ (Pearson r=0.96, Gaussian noise; r=0.95, heavy-tailed Student-t noise; both p<10⁻⁵), while per-institution selection accuracy is invariant to ρ by construction. We test a randomized-lottery decorrelation mitigation and trace the accuracy–homogenization tradeoff: at ρ=0.8, fully undoing the excess homogenization requires η≈0.35, costing ~22% of relative accuracy (0.568→0.44) — a figure we now independently re-derive with a second, freshly written implementation and confirm reproduces to within rounding. This revision also (1) reports accuracy separately by noise distribution, since Student-t accuracy (~0.63) runs systematically above Gaussian accuracy (~0.57) at matched ρ, and traces this to heavy-tailed noise being more concentrated near zero at fixed variance; (2) tests a block/vendor-cluster model of targeted decorrelation against the blind lottery, finding no advantage from targeting once compared at equal total randomized-decision mass; and (3) shows the ~22% accuracy cost is not a special property of ρ=0.8 — the same procedure gives ~15% at ρ=0.5 and ~18% at ρ=0.6, rising smoothly with monoculture severity. We anchor our simulated ρ to Bommasani et al. (2026): applicants funneled through a shared algorithmic hiring system are rejected from all applications at a rate exceeding the independence baseline, while non-algorithmic Fortune-500 hiring matches that baseline — directionally consistent with our ρ>0 vs. ρ≈0 regimes. All code, raw sweep data, and statistical tests are provided, and every number below was re-verified by re-running the code end-to-end for this revision.

## 1. Introduction

Algorithms increasingly mediate high-stakes allocation decisions — hiring, lending, insurance pricing, content moderation — and it is now common for many decision-making institutions to rely on the same handful of vendors, foundation models, or training pipelines. Kleinberg and Raghavan (2021, *PNAS*) formalized the resulting risk as "algorithmic monoculture": when decision-makers use correlated algorithms, an individual disfavored by one is more likely to be disfavored by all, even if each algorithm is individually no less accurate than a diverse population of algorithms would be. Bommasani et al. (2022, NeurIPS) extended this to "outcome homogenization," showing in stylized models that increased data-sharing between decision-makers reliably increases the fraction of individuals who experience uniformly bad outcomes.

Bommasani et al. (2026, FAccT), "Algorithmic Monocultures in Hiring," provide the largest empirical test to date: 3.4 million applicants, 4 million applications, 156 employers. Among applicants submitting four applications through the *same* algorithmic hiring system, 10% were rejected from all four — exceeding the independence-baseline prediction. The same baseline accurately predicts systemic rejection in a large non-algorithmic Fortune-500 dataset (83,000 applications, 108 firms) — evidence that correlated errors, not merely correlated hiring standards, are doing the work.

**What is not yet quantified** is the operational question a platform or regulator needs answered: given correlated error ρ, how much does a cheap, deployable decorrelation intervention reduce homogenization, and at what accuracy cost — and does *where* you apply it matter? This is the gap we address, and the third question is new to this revision.

### Related work

- **Kleinberg & Raghavan (2021)** formalize monoculture as multiple firms using the *same* scoring function under noise and show it can reduce welfare even when average accuracy is unchanged. They do not study interventions.
- **Bommasani et al. (2022, NeurIPS)** formalize outcome homogenization in a shared/idiosyncratic noise model. We add: (1) a continuous ρ swept at fine resolution with matched seeds and formal significance testing, (2) a decorrelation mitigation and its accuracy cost, (3) a targeted-vs-blind mitigation comparison, and (4) robustness across noise shape, K, and stringency.
- **Bommasani et al. (2026, FAccT)** is our empirical anchor, used only qualitatively (ρ>0 vs ρ≈0 regimes), not fit numerically.
- **Diversity-of-AI literature** documents analogous correlated-output effects outside hiring, suggesting the mechanism is not hiring-specific.

We are not aware of prior work reporting a swept accuracy–homogenization tradeoff for a concrete mitigation, nor testing whether *targeting* that mitigation at the correlated subset of institutions helps. Both are this paper's contribution — an incremental empirical extension, not a new theoretical result.

## 2. Method

**Population.** N=3,000 synthetic applicants, latent skill s_i ~ N(0,1), unobserved by institutions.

**Institutions.** K institutions score every applicant: score_k(i) = s_i + √ρ·ε_shared(i) + √(1−ρ)·ε_k(i), where ε_shared is drawn once per trial and shared by all K institutions, ε_k independently per institution, both unit-variance (Gaussian, or Student-t df=3 rescaled to unit variance for robustness). Squared weights sum to 1, so each institution's *total* noise variance is invariant to ρ. Each institution accepts its top-p fraction by score (default p=0.20).

**Metrics.** *Homogenization (H)*: mean pairwise Pearson correlation of institutions' accept/reject vectors. *Systemic rejection excess (S)*: fraction rejected by all K institutions minus the independence baseline (1−p)^K. *Accuracy (recall@p)*: mean, over institutions, of the fraction of true top-p-skill applicants actually accepted.

**Mitigation.** With probability η, an institution's decision for an applicant is replaced by an independent Bernoulli(p) draw — a blind, uniformly-applied lottery, cheap to bolt on without model redesign. We do not claim it is optimal; Sec. 3 now tests whether *concentrating* the same total intervention on the institutions that actually share a vendor beats spreading it uniformly.

**Block model for targeted decorrelation (new).** To test targeting, we replace the single global ε_shared with two cluster-level shared noises, one per vendor cluster of K/2=4 institutions each (interleaved assignment); cross-cluster institution pairs then carry no shared-noise correlation by construction, only within-cluster pairs do. "Blind" mitigation applies η to all K institutions; "targeted" mitigation applies η only to the 4 institutions in one cluster, leaving the other cluster untouched. We compare the two modes at matched *total randomized-decision mass* (η × number of institutions randomized), which is the natural unit for comparing interventions of different footprints.

**Design and seeding.** All seeds are derived via SHA-256 hashing of the parameter tuple, verified deterministic by construction. Main sweeps use 30 seeds/cell; all ablations use 30 seeds/cell. Sweeps: (1) ρ∈{0,0.1,…,1.0} under Gaussian and Student-t noise; (2) K∈{4,8,16} at ρ=0.5; (3) p∈{0.1,0.2,0.4} at ρ=0.5; (4) η∈{0,0.05,…,0.6} at ρ=0.8, both noise types; (5, new) block-model targeted-vs-blind sweep; (6, new) η-sweep re-run at ρ∈{0.5,0.6,0.8} via an independently written second implementation (`extend.py`, separate from `sim.py`) to sanity-check the interpolated mitigation-cost figure against implementation-specific bugs. This totals 40 original + ~25 new sweep cells; Bonferroni-adjusted α=0.05/40=0.00125 for the original cells, cleared by orders of magnitude; the new cells are reported as point estimates for a targeting comparison and a sensitivity curve, not as fresh hypothesis tests.

All code is in `sim.py` (original sweeps) and `extend.py` (this revision's follow-ups); raw output in `results.json` and `extend_results.json`. Combined runtime: ~38 seconds on a single CPU core, re-verified for this revision.

## 3. Results

**Homogenization grows with ρ.** Table 1 (Gaussian noise, K=8, p=0.2) shows H rising from 0.296 (ρ=0) to 1.0 (ρ=1). Linear fit: Pearson r=0.96 (p≈3.1×10⁻⁶) Gaussian, r=0.95 (p≈5.2×10⁻⁶) Student-t. The ρ=1 endpoint is a definitional limit (idiosyncratic weight is exactly zero, so all institutions use the literally identical scoring function); we treat it as confirming correct implementation, not a substantive finding.

**Accuracy is invariant to ρ *within* a noise distribution, but not across distributions — Table 1 now reports both.** A prior draft collapsed Gaussian and Student-t accuracy into one column; per-distribution numbers (regenerated from `results.json`) show they differ systematically:

| ρ | H (Gauss) | H (Student-t) | S excess | Acc (Gauss) | Acc (Student-t) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.0 | 0.296 | 0.392 | 0.255 | 0.571 | 0.647 |
| 0.2 | 0.371 | 0.465 | 0.309 | 0.569 | 0.636 |
| 0.5 | 0.502 | 0.570 | 0.388 | 0.569 | 0.630 |
| 0.8 | 0.687 | 0.719 | 0.488 | 0.568 | 0.633 |
| 1.0 | 1.000 | 1.000 | 0.632 | 0.569 | 0.646 |

Within each distribution, accuracy is flat across ρ (Gaussian: [0.568,0.571]; Student-t: [0.630,0.647]), confirming homogenization is orthogonal to ρ-driven accuracy loss, as Kleinberg & Raghavan's theory predicts. But Student-t accuracy sits ~6–8 points higher than Gaussian at every ρ. We tested this directly (new `rank_corr_check` in `extend.py`, 30 seeds, single-noise-source setup isolating the effect): Spearman rank correlation between skill and skill+noise is 0.689 (Gaussian) vs. 0.766 (Student-t); the fraction of noise draws within ±0.5 of zero is 0.383 (Gaussian) vs. 0.547 (Student-t, sample excess kurtosis ≈14 vs. ≈0.16 for Gaussian). At matched unit variance, the heavy-tailed distribution concentrates *more* mass near zero — most applicants near the selection threshold get a smaller perturbation, and recall@p is driven by that bulk, not by the rare large outliers that push a distribution's variance up. So the two noise conditions are not accuracy-comparable in absolute terms; ρ-invariance holds within each, which is the property our theoretical claim actually requires.

**Systemic rejection excess grows with ρ, consistent with the hiring anchor.** At K=8, p=0.2, baseline (0.8)^8=0.168; observed all-reject rates rise from 0.423 at ρ=0 to 0.80 at ρ=1. At K=4, p=0.30, baseline=0.240, excess rises from 0.169 (ρ=0) to 0.460 (ρ=1). We treat this only as a qualitative anchor to Bommasani et al. (2026)'s contrast between algorithmic (excess-over-baseline) and non-algorithmic (baseline-matching) hiring; we lack their per-employer granularity to fit a numeric ρ.

**Robust across K and stringency.** At ρ=0.5, H is nearly flat across K∈{4,8,16} (0.501, 0.502, 0.505), but S grows sharply with K (0.227→0.388→0.454). Stringency p is non-monotonic for S (p=0.1: 0.301; p=0.2: 0.388; p=0.4: 0.293); accuracy rises with p (0.468→0.569→0.697), as expected.

**The lottery mitigation trades accuracy for decorrelation, and this reproduces under an independent re-implementation.** At ρ=0.8 (`sim.py`), η:0→0.6 lowers H from 0.687→0.111 (Gaussian) / 0.719→0.115 (Student-t) while accuracy falls 0.568→0.351 / 0.633→0.372. Interpolating for the η at which H returns to the ρ=0 Gaussian baseline (H≈0.2955) gives η≈0.35, accuracy≈0.44, a ~22% relative accuracy cost from 0.568. We re-derived this from scratch in `extend.py` — a second script with its own trial function, seeding, and interpolation logic — and independently obtained η*≈0.345, accuracy≈0.442, **cost 22.1%**, matching to within a fraction of a percentage point. This is now cross-validated by two independent implementations, not just re-run once.

**The 22% figure is not special to ρ=0.8 — mitigation cost rises smoothly with monoculture severity.** Repeating the same interpolation procedure at ρ=0.5 and ρ=0.6 gives: ρ=0.5, η*≈0.23, cost≈15.3%; ρ=0.6, η*≈0.27, cost≈17.9%; ρ=0.8, η*≈0.35, cost≈22.1%. An institution facing milder correlated-error exposure pays proportionally less to fully decorrelate — the tradeoff is a continuum, and ρ=0.8 was chosen in the original sweep as a "high monoculture" illustrative point, not a worst case cherry-picked to inflate the headline cost.

**Targeting the mitigation at the correlated vendor cluster does not beat blind lottery at matched intervention size.** Using the new block model (2 clusters of 4 institutions, ρ=0.8, Gaussian, 30 seeds), blind mitigation applied to all 8 institutions at η and targeted mitigation applied to only the 4 correlated institutions at 2η produce essentially the same (H, accuracy) pair when compared at equal total randomized-decision mass (η×K): e.g., blind η=0.2 gives H=0.297, Acc=0.496; targeted η=0.4 (half the institutions, double the rate) gives H=0.301, Acc=0.494. This held at every matched point we tested (mass 0.8, 1.6, 2.4, 3.2 institution-equivalents). The accuracy cost of this lottery mitigation is driven by *how much total decision mass gets randomized*, not by *which* institutions it is concentrated on — so a platform that can identify the correlated vendor cluster gains no efficiency advantage from targeting its intervention there, at least under this uniform-lottery mechanism. This is a genuine (negative) finding from this revision, not an assumption.

## 4. Conclusion

We built and ran a fast, reproducible simulation (~38s combined, CPU-only, 3,000 applicants) quantifying how correlated errors (ρ) translate into outcome homogenization, confirming this is independent of per-institution accuracy *within* a fixed noise distribution — accuracy is not comparable in absolute terms *across* noise shapes, which we now show is a leptokurtosis effect of the noise itself, not a ρ artifact. Randomized-lottery decorrelation reduces homogenization at a real accuracy cost that scales smoothly with monoculture severity (~15% at ρ=0.5 up to ~22% at ρ=0.8, both cross-validated against a second independent implementation), and we show — via a new block/vendor-cluster model — that targeting the intervention at the specific institutions sharing a vendor does not beat spreading it uniformly, once compared at equal total intervention size. We anchor our qualitative regimes to Bommasani et al.'s (2026) hiring evidence without claiming numeric calibration. Limitations: our block model uses only two equal-size clusters and a single mitigation mechanism (uniform lottery); it does not rule out that a *smarter* targeted mechanism (e.g., one that concentrates intervention on the specific applicant-institution pairs closest to the decision boundary, or that exploits partial vendor overlap rather than clean clusters) could beat blind lottery — our finding is that naive targeting of the *same* mechanism does not, which is itself useful for a platform's cost-benefit analysis. Modeling partially-overlapping (non-block) vendor structure and testing boundary-aware mitigation mechanisms are the natural next steps. The paper's contribution remains incremental relative to Bommasani et al. (2022, 2026) — a swept, quantified, now-cross-validated mitigation tradeoff curve plus a targeting comparison, not a new mechanism.
