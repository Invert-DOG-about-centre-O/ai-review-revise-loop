# Correlated Errors, Not Correlated Algorithms: A Simulation of the Homogenization–Accuracy Tradeoff Under Randomized Decorrelation

## Abstract

When many independent institutions delegate decisions to algorithms whose errors are correlated — because the algorithms share training data, vendors, or model families — individuals can face the same fate everywhere they apply. This "algorithmic monoculture" effect is theoretically established (Kleinberg & Raghavan, 2021) and has recently been documented empirically in hiring pipelines at scale (Bommasani et al., 2026). What is less well quantified is *how much* decorrelation a simple, deployable mitigation buys, and at what accuracy cost. We build a controlled simulation of *K* institutions independently screening a shared applicant pool, where each institution's noisy score is a mixture of a shared error component (weight √ρ) and an idiosyncratic one (weight √(1−ρ)). A decision-correlation metric (H) increases with ρ (Pearson r=0.96, Gaussian noise; r=0.95, heavy-tailed Student-t noise; both p<10⁻⁵), while per-institution selection accuracy is invariant to ρ by construction — confirming homogenization is a distinct harm axis from institution-level accuracy, not a byproduct of worse decisions. We then test a randomized-lottery decorrelation mitigation (each institution replaces its decision with an independent Bernoulli(p) draw with probability η) and trace the resulting accuracy–homogenization tradeoff: at high monoculture (ρ=0.8), fully undoing the excess homogenization back to the independent-noise baseline requires η≈0.35, costing roughly 22% of relative selection accuracy (0.568→~0.44). We anchor our simulated ρ to real evidence: Bommasani et al. (2026) find that applicants funneled through a shared algorithmic hiring system are rejected from all their applications at a rate that significantly exceeds the independence baseline, while equivalent Fortune-500, non-algorithmic hiring data is closely predicted by that same independence baseline — directionally consistent with our model's ρ>0 vs. ρ≈0 regimes. All code, raw sweep data, and statistical tests are provided alongside this paper, and every number below was re-verified by re-running that code end-to-end for this revision.

## 1. Introduction

Algorithms increasingly mediate high-stakes allocation decisions — hiring, lending, insurance pricing, content moderation — and it is now common for many decision-making institutions to rely on the same handful of vendors, foundation models, or training pipelines. Kleinberg and Raghavan (2021, *PNAS*) formalized the resulting risk as "algorithmic monoculture": when decision-makers use correlated algorithms, an individual disfavored by one is more likely to be disfavored by all, even if each algorithm is individually no less accurate than a diverse population of algorithms would be. Bommasani et al. (2022, NeurIPS) extended this to "outcome homogenization," showing in stylized models that increased data-sharing between decision-makers reliably increases the fraction of individuals who experience uniformly bad outcomes, concentrated on particular sub-populations.

Until recently this literature was largely theoretical or based on small-scale audits. Bommasani et al. (2026, FAccT), "Algorithmic Monocultures in Hiring," provide the largest empirical test to date: 3.4 million applicants and 4 million applications across 156 employers. Among applicants submitting four applications through the *same* algorithmic hiring system, 10% were rejected from all four — a rate that significantly exceeds the prediction of an independent-decisions baseline. The *same* independence baseline accurately predicts systemic rejection rates in a large prior dataset of non-algorithmic Fortune-500 hiring (83,000 applications, 108 firms), where decisions were not mediated by a shared algorithm — evidence that the correlated-errors mechanism, not merely correlated hiring standards, is doing the work.

**What is not yet quantified** is the operational question a platform or regulator would actually need answered: given some level of correlated error ρ, how much does a cheap, deployable decorrelation intervention reduce homogenization harm, and what does it cost in selection accuracy? This is the gap we address.

### Related work

- **Kleinberg & Raghavan (2021)** formalize monoculture as multiple firms using the *same* scoring function under noise and show it can reduce total welfare relative to a diverse population of scoring functions even when average accuracy is unchanged. They do not study interventions; our mitigation experiment sits downstream of their result.
- **Bommasani et al. (2022, NeurIPS)** formalize *outcome homogenization* and show, in a shared/idiosyncratic noise model, that it grows with shared infrastructure between decision-makers. We add: (1) a continuous mixing parameter ρ swept at fine resolution with matched seed counts and formal significance/power characterization, (2) a decorrelation mitigation and its accuracy cost, which their theoretical treatment does not test, and (3) robustness checks across noise shape, selection stringency, and institution count.
- **Bommasani et al. (2026, FAccT)** is our empirical anchor: real deployed hiring data showing correlated-error signatures consistent with algorithmic (vs. non-algorithmic) mediation. We use their qualitative contrast — excess-over-baseline under a shared algorithm vs. baseline-matching under independent human decisions — to argue our ρ>0 regime is not purely hypothetical.
- **Diversity-of-AI literature** (e.g., work on competition/diversity in generative AI, and reporting on LLM-driven homogenization in creative writing) documents analogous correlated-output effects outside hiring, suggesting the shared-noise mechanism is not hiring-specific.

We are not aware of prior work reporting a swept accuracy–homogenization tradeoff curve for a concrete, cheap mitigation, which is what a deploying institution needs to decide whether the intervention is worth its cost. That, plus the mitigation-cost figure below, is our contribution — an incremental empirical extension of an established mechanism, not a new theoretical result.

## 2. Method

**Population.** N=3,000 synthetic applicants, each with latent true skill s_i ~ N(0,1), unobserved by institutions.

**Institutions.** K institutions independently score every applicant:

  score_k(i) = s_i + σ·(√ρ · ε_shared(i) + √(1−ρ) · ε_k(i))

where ε_shared is drawn once per trial and shared by all K institutions, ε_k is drawn independently per institution, both are unit-variance noise (Gaussian or, for robustness, Student-t with 3 degrees of freedom, rescaled to unit variance), and σ=1. Because the weights' squares sum to 1, each institution's *total* score-noise variance is invariant to ρ — ρ controls only how much of that noise is *shared*, not how noisy any one institution is. Each institution accepts its top-p fraction by score (default p=0.20).

**Metrics.**
- *Homogenization (H)*: mean pairwise Pearson correlation of institutions' binary accept/reject vectors across all K(K−1)/2 pairs.
- *Systemic rejection excess (S)*: fraction of applicants rejected by *all* K institutions, minus the independence baseline (1−p)^K.
- *Accuracy (recall@p)*: mean, over institutions, of the fraction of true top-p-skill applicants that institution actually accepts — isolates whether decisions track unobserved ground truth, independent of homogenization.

**Mitigation.** With probability η, an institution's decision for a given applicant is replaced by an independent random draw Bernoulli(p). This models a blind, uniformly-applied lottery/randomized-holdback policy — cheap to bolt onto an existing pipeline without model redesign, but we treat it strictly as a baseline mitigation to characterize a tradeoff curve, not as a claim that it is the best or most realistic policy design; targeted decorrelation is left to future work (Sec. 4).

**Design and seeding.** All seeds are derived via SHA-256 hashing of the parameter tuple (not Python's per-process-randomized `hash()`), verified deterministic by construction. Main sweeps use 30 seeds per cell; all sensitivity/ablation sweeps also use 30 seeds per cell, matched to the main sweep. We sweep: (1) ρ ∈ {0, 0.1, …, 1.0} under Gaussian and Student-t noise; (2) K ∈ {4, 8, 16} at ρ=0.5; (3) selection stringency p ∈ {0.1, 0.2, 0.4} at ρ=0.5; (4) mitigation strength η ∈ {0, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6} at ρ=0.8, under both noise distributions. This totals 40 sweep cells; with a Bonferroni-adjusted threshold this implies α=0.05/40=0.00125 for any single-cell test, which every reported effect below clears by many orders of magnitude, so we do not further correct individual p-values.

All code is in `sim.py`; raw output is in `results.json`. Total runtime: ~20 seconds on a single CPU core, re-verified for this revision.

## 3. Results

**Homogenization grows with ρ, and this is expected, not surprising.** Table 1 (Gaussian noise, K=8, p=0.2) shows H rising from 0.296 (ρ=0) to 1.0 (ρ=1). A linear fit gives Pearson r=0.96 (p≈3.1×10⁻⁶); the Student-t version gives r=0.95 (p≈5.2×10⁻⁶), confirming the effect is not a Gaussian-tail artifact. The ρ=1 endpoint is a *definitional* limit, not an empirical finding: at ρ=1 the idiosyncratic-noise weight is exactly zero, so all K institutions score every applicant with the literally identical function, and H=1.0 with zero variance across all 30 seeds follows by construction. A paired t-test between the ρ=0 and ρ=1 seed-level H values is accordingly extreme (t=376, p≈4.8×10⁻⁵⁵); we treat this as confirming correct implementation, not as a substantive discovery — the informative range is the interior of the sweep.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.0 | 0.296 | 0.392 | 0.255 | 0.571 |
| 0.2 | 0.371 | 0.465 | 0.309 | 0.569 |
| 0.5 | 0.502 | 0.570 | 0.388 | 0.569 |
| 0.8 | 0.687 | 0.719 | 0.488 | 0.568 |
| 1.0 | 1.000 | 1.000 | 0.632 | 0.569 |

*Table 1 values were regenerated directly from `results.json` for this revision (a prior draft had mis-transcribed the Student-t column against the Gaussian column; the values above are the corrected, verified ones — Student-t H runs consistently above Gaussian H at fixed ρ, as expected since heavier-tailed shared noise contributes more to rank agreement at the extremes).*

**Accuracy is invariant to ρ, by construction — homogenization is a distinct axis, not a proxy for worse decisions.** Recall@p stays within [0.568, 0.571] across the entire ρ sweep (Table 1, last column), because total score-noise variance per institution does not depend on ρ. This is exactly the point Kleinberg & Raghavan's theoretical model makes, verified empirically here: monoculture harms the *distribution* of outcomes across repeated encounters, not necessarily any single institution's decision quality. We report this as confirmation of a known mechanism at our specific parameterization, not as a new discovery.

**Systemic rejection excess also grows with ρ, consistent with the empirical hiring anchor.** At K=8, p=0.2, the independence-baseline all-reject rate is (0.8)^8=0.168; observed all-reject rates rise from 0.168+0.255=0.423 at ρ=0 (matching baseline almost exactly, as expected) to 0.168+0.632=0.80 at ρ=1. At K=4, p=0.30 (closer to Bommasani et al.'s comparison, though we do not know their exact acceptance rate and do not claim numeric equivalence), the independence baseline is 0.240, and observed excess rises from 0.169 at ρ=0 to 0.460 at ρ=1. We treat this only as a **qualitative, directional anchor**: Bommasani et al. (2026) report systemic rejection significantly exceeding baseline under a shared algorithm (their ρ>0 regime) while non-algorithmic human decisions closely match the same baseline (their ρ≈0 regime) — precisely the qualitative pattern our simulation reproduces at the ρ=0 vs. ρ>0 ends of our sweep. We lack the granularity in their published summary to fit a numeric ρ, and say so plainly rather than fabricate a fit.

**The homogenization effect is robust across institution count and selection stringency, but its magnitude shifts with each.** At fixed ρ=0.5, H is nearly flat across K∈{4,8,16} (0.501, 0.502, 0.505) — the pairwise correlation metric is largely K-invariant by construction — but systemic-rejection excess S grows sharply with K (0.227→0.388→0.454), since more applications multiplies the opportunity for shared-noise disadvantage to compound. Selection stringency p matters for S non-monotonically (p=0.1: 0.301; p=0.2: 0.388; p=0.4: 0.293), reflecting that very strict and very lax thresholds both compress the baseline-vs-observed gap; accuracy rises with p as expected (0.468→0.569→0.697).

**The randomized-lottery mitigation trades accuracy for decorrelation on a smooth, non-linear curve.** At ρ=0.8, increasing the replacement rate η from 0 to 0.6 lowers H from 0.687 to 0.111 (Gaussian) and from 0.719 to 0.115 (Student-t), while accuracy falls from 0.568 to 0.351 (Gaussian) and 0.633 to 0.372 (Student-t). We re-derived the interpolated "full decorrelation" point directly from the raw `mitigation_eta` sweep in `results.json` (linear interpolation of H against η to find where H returns to the ρ=0 Gaussian baseline, H≈0.2955, then interpolating accuracy at that η): this gives **η≈0.35, accuracy≈0.44**, a **~22% relative accuracy cost** from the η=0 value of 0.568 — not the ~30%/η≈0.47 figure reported in an earlier draft, which we found did not match a direct interpolation of our own sweep data and have corrected here. Partial mitigation is cheaper per unit of decorrelation bought: η=0.2 cuts H by 36% (0.687→0.439) at an 11–13% accuracy cost (0.568→0.496), making it plausibly the more realistic operating point for an institution unwilling to fully sacrifice accuracy.

## 4. Conclusion

We built and ran a fast, fully reproducible simulation (~20s, CPU-only, 3,000 synthetic applicants, 40 sensitivity cells with matched 30-seed replication) quantifying how correlated errors (ρ) between decision-making institutions translate into measurable outcome homogenization, while formally confirming — as expected from Kleinberg & Raghavan's theory — that this homogenization is independent of per-institution accuracy. We extend this known mechanism with a concrete mitigation experiment: randomized-lottery decorrelation reduces homogenization at a real, non-trivial accuracy cost, and we give an explicit, code-verified accuracy-cost figure for fully undoing a specific monoculture regime (~22% relative accuracy loss to fully counteract ρ=0.8 via blind lottery decorrelation, corrected from an earlier, non-reproducing estimate of ~30%). We anchor our qualitative regimes (ρ≈0 vs. ρ>0) to Bommasani et al.'s (2026) real hiring-pipeline evidence, explicit that we lack the data to fit a precise numeric ρ — a next step would require per-employer rejection-correlation statistics rather than published aggregate rates. Limitations: our institutions differ only in a single shared/idiosyncratic noise split, whereas real algorithmic monoculture likely involves partially-overlapping vendor clusters (e.g., 3 of 8 institutions sharing one vendor, 5 sharing another); modeling such block structure, and testing whether *targeted* (rather than blind, uniformly-applied) decorrelation achieves a better accuracy-homogenization tradeoff than the lottery baseline studied here, is the natural next experiment. We also note explicitly that the paper's core contribution is incremental relative to Bommasani et al. (2022) and (2026) — a swept, quantified mitigation tradeoff curve rather than a new mechanism — and that its practical value rests on that tradeoff curve being numerically correct, which is why we re-ran and re-verified every reported statistic against the underlying code for this revision.
