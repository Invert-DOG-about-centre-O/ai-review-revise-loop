# Correlated Errors, Not Correlated Algorithms: A Simulation of the Homogenization–Accuracy Tradeoff Under Randomized Decorrelation

## Abstract

When many independent institutions delegate decisions to algorithms whose errors are correlated — because the algorithms share training data, vendors, or model families — individuals can face the same fate everywhere they apply. This "algorithmic monoculture" effect is theoretically established (Kleinberg & Raghavan, 2021) and has recently been documented empirically in hiring pipelines at scale (Bommasani et al., 2026). We build a controlled simulation of *K* institutions independently screening a shared applicant pool, where each institution's noisy score is a mixture of a shared error component (weight √ρ) and an idiosyncratic one (weight √(1−ρ)). A decision-correlation metric (H) increases with ρ (Pearson r=0.96 Gaussian, r=0.95 Student-t noise, both p<10⁻⁵), while per-institution accuracy is invariant to ρ by construction. A randomized-lottery mitigation fully undoes the excess homogenization at ρ=0.8 for η≈0.35, at a ~22% relative accuracy cost (0.568→0.44 recall@p); this cost rises smoothly with ρ (~15% at ρ=0.5, ~18% at ρ=0.6) and is cross-validated by a second, independently written implementation. This revision closes three questions from review. First, we test whether the accuracy-cost figure is an artifact of the recall@p metric: repeating the interpolation under a fixed population-quantile threshold gives nearly identical costs (15.4/17.7/22.6%), but under a continuous point-biserial correlation with true skill the costs are systematically higher (23.6/27.3/35.0%) — the headline "15–22%" is robust to threshold-based accuracy definitions but understates the cost under a correlation-based one. Second, we test whether the paper's negative targeting result (concentrating the same total lottery mass on the correlated vendor cluster does not beat spreading it uniformly) is specific to the *uniform* Bernoulli lottery mechanism: under a threshold-proximity-biased redraw (only applicants near each institution's cutoff are re-randomized) targeting and blind mitigation are *not* equivalent at matched mass — targeting decorrelates faster per unit mass but at higher accuracy cost per unit mass — so the earlier equivalence is a property of the uniform mechanism, not a general one. Third, we clarify which of our two outcome metrics — H (pairwise decision correlation, K-invariant) or S (systemic all-reject excess, sharply K-increasing) — a regulator should target, since they diverge: S is the applicant-facing harm measure and is what a mandate should be defined against. All code, raw sweep data, and statistical tests are provided.

## 1. Introduction

Algorithms increasingly mediate high-stakes allocation decisions — hiring, lending, insurance pricing, content moderation — and many decision-making institutions now rely on the same handful of vendors, foundation models, or training pipelines. Kleinberg and Raghavan (2021, *PNAS*) formalized the resulting risk as "algorithmic monoculture": when decision-makers use correlated algorithms, an individual disfavored by one is more likely to be disfavored by all, even if no algorithm is individually less accurate. Bommasani et al. (2022, NeurIPS) extended this to "outcome homogenization," showing that increased data-sharing between decision-makers reliably increases the fraction of individuals who experience uniformly bad outcomes.

Bommasani et al. (2026, FAccT), "Algorithmic Monocultures in Hiring," provide the largest empirical test to date: 3.4 million applicants, 4 million applications, 156 employers. Among applicants submitting four applications through the *same* algorithmic hiring system, 10% were rejected from all four — exceeding the independence-baseline prediction. The same baseline accurately predicts systemic rejection in a large non-algorithmic Fortune-500 dataset (83,000 applications, 108 firms) — evidence that correlated errors, not merely correlated hiring standards, are doing the work.

**What is not yet quantified** is the operational question a platform or regulator needs answered: given correlated error ρ, how much does a cheap, deployable decorrelation intervention reduce homogenization, at what accuracy cost, does *where* you apply it matter, does *how* you randomize matter, and which outcome metric should a mandate actually target? This revision closes the last three of those.

### Related work

- **Kleinberg & Raghavan (2021)** formalize monoculture under noise and show it can reduce welfare even at unchanged average accuracy. They do not study interventions.
- **Bommasani et al. (2022, NeurIPS)** formalize outcome homogenization in a shared/idiosyncratic noise model. We add a continuous swept ρ, a decorrelation mitigation and its accuracy cost, a targeted-vs-blind and mechanism-vs-mechanism comparison, and a metric-choice sensitivity analysis.
- **Bommasani et al. (2026, FAccT)** is our empirical anchor, used only qualitatively (ρ>0 vs ρ≈0 regimes), not fit numerically.

We are not aware of prior work reporting a swept accuracy–homogenization tradeoff for a concrete mitigation, nor testing whether *targeting* or *redraw mechanism design* changes that tradeoff. This is an incremental empirical extension, not a new theoretical result.

## 2. Method

**Population.** N=3,000 synthetic applicants, latent skill s_i ~ N(0,1), unobserved by institutions.

**Institutions.** K institutions score every applicant: score_k(i) = s_i + √ρ·ε_shared(i) + √(1−ρ)·ε_k(i), both noise sources unit-variance (Gaussian, or Student-t df=3 rescaled to unit variance). Each institution accepts its top-p fraction by score (default p=0.20).

**Metrics.** *Homogenization (H)*: mean pairwise Pearson correlation of institutions' accept/reject vectors. *Systemic rejection excess (S)*: fraction rejected by all K institutions minus the independence baseline (1−p)^K. *Accuracy*: mean, over institutions, of recall@p — fraction of the true top-p-skill applicants accepted — unless otherwise noted (Sec. 3.3 tests two alternatives).

**Mitigation (uniform lottery).** With probability η, an institution's decision for an applicant is replaced by an independent Bernoulli(p) draw.

**Block model for targeted decorrelation.** Two vendor clusters of K/2=4 institutions each share cluster-level noise instead of one global ε_shared; cross-cluster pairs carry no shared-noise correlation. "Blind" applies η to all K institutions; "targeted" applies it only within one cluster, compared at matched *total randomized-decision mass* (η × institutions randomized).

**Boundary-biased redraw mechanism (new).** Instead of a uniform Bernoulli(η) mask over all N applicants, we re-randomize only the *w* applicants whose score is closest to each institution's own acceptance cutoff (a symmetric window around the threshold rank), redrawing their decision via Bernoulli(p). This targets exactly the applicants whose accept/reject status is most sensitive to noise, holding the same "number of decisions redrawn" as the mass unit for comparing blind (window applied to all K institutions) vs. targeted (window doubled, applied only to one cluster).

**Alternative accuracy definitions (new).** Besides recall@p, we compute: (a) *fixed population-quantile* accuracy, using ground truth s_i > Φ⁻¹(1−p) (the population quantile) instead of the empirical top-p of the finite sample; (b) *point-biserial correlation* between each institution's binary accept/reject decision and true skill s_i, a continuous calibration measure rather than a thresholded one.

**Design and seeding.** All seeds are SHA-256-derived from the parameter tuple. Main sweeps use 30 seeds/cell; all ablations and this revision's new sweeps use 30 seeds/cell. This revision adds two new sweeps: (5) a boundary-biased targeted-vs-blind comparison (`extend2.py`), and (6) a mitigation-cost-by-accuracy-metric sensitivity sweep at ρ∈{0.5,0.6,0.8} (`extend2.py`). These are reported as point estimates and sensitivity curves, consistent with the non-hypothesis-testing framing used for the prior revision's new cells.

All code is in `sim.py` (original sweeps), `extend.py` (revision-2 follow-ups), and `extend2.py` (this revision's follow-ups); raw output in `results.json`, `extend_results.json`, `extend2_results.json`. Combined runtime: under a minute on a single CPU core.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Homogenization, accuracy, and the noise-shape effect (unchanged from prior revision)

H rises from 0.296 (ρ=0) to 1.0 (ρ=1) under Gaussian noise (r=0.96, p≈3.1×10⁻⁶) and similarly under Student-t (r=0.95, p≈5.2×10⁻⁶). Within a noise distribution, recall@p is flat across ρ (Gaussian: [0.568,0.571]; Student-t: [0.630,0.647]) — homogenization is orthogonal to ρ-driven accuracy loss, as theory predicts — but Student-t accuracy sits ~6–8 points above Gaussian at every ρ. This traces to heavy-tailed noise being *more* concentrated near zero at matched unit variance (fraction of draws within ±0.5 of zero: 0.383 Gaussian vs. 0.547 Student-t; excess kurtosis ≈0.16 vs. ≈14), so most near-threshold applicants get a smaller perturbation even though rare large outliers exist.

Systemic rejection excess S also grows with ρ (K=8,p=0.2: baseline 0.168, observed 0.423→0.80 as ρ:0→1), consistent with Bommasani et al. (2026)'s algorithmic-vs-non-algorithmic contrast, used only qualitatively.

**Robust across K and stringency.** At ρ=0.5, H is nearly flat across K∈{4,8,16} (0.501, 0.502, 0.505), but **S grows sharply with K** (0.227→0.388→0.454).

### 3.2 Mitigation cost, and whether targeting helps — mechanism matters

At ρ=0.8, the uniform lottery lowers H from 0.687→0.111 while accuracy falls 0.568→0.351; interpolating to the ρ=0 baseline gives η*≈0.35, a ~22% relative accuracy cost — independently re-derived (η*≈0.345, cost 22.1%) by a second implementation (`extend.py`). The cost scales smoothly with monoculture severity: ~15.3% at ρ=0.5, ~17.9% at ρ=0.6, ~22.1% at ρ=0.8.

Under the **uniform** lottery, targeting the correlated vendor cluster does not beat spreading the same total mass across all institutions blindly (e.g., blind η=0.2: H=0.297, Acc=0.496 vs. targeted η=0.4 on half the institutions: H=0.301, Acc=0.494) — this held at every mass we tested.

**This equivalence breaks under a different redraw mechanism.** Using the boundary-biased redraw (block model, ρ=0.8, Gaussian, 30 seeds), blind and targeted are no longer interchangeable at matched mass. At mass=1200 decisions redrawn: blind (window=600 per cluster, both clusters) gives H=0.366, Acc=0.422; targeted (window=1200, one cluster only) gives H=0.227, Acc=0.366 — targeting decorrelates substantially more (H drops further) but also costs more accuracy, at the *same* total redraw budget. At mass=200: blind H=0.457,Acc=0.551 vs. targeted H=0.456,Acc=0.552 (near-identical, small-mass regime). The gap opens as mass grows and closes again only once the targeted window saturates most of one cluster's decisions. So **the paper's earlier "targeting doesn't help" finding is a property of the specific uniform-Bernoulli mechanism, not a general property of targeting** — a platform that both knows its correlated cluster *and* concentrates randomization at the decision boundary faces a genuinely different (steeper) tradeoff curve than blind uniform mitigation, in either direction depending on whether H-reduction or accuracy preservation is prioritized.

### 3.3 Sensitivity of the accuracy-cost figure to the accuracy definition

Repeating the η-interpolation at ρ∈{0.5,0.6,0.8} under three accuracy definitions:

| ρ | recall@p cost | fixed-quantile cost | point-biserial-corr cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.5 | 15.0% | 15.4% | 23.6% |
| 0.6 | 18.1% | 17.7% | 27.3% |
| 0.8 | 22.8% | 22.6% | 35.0% |

The two **threshold-based** metrics (recall@p against the empirical top-p, and against the fixed population quantile) agree almost exactly, confirming the headline 15–22% figure is not an artifact of using empirical rather than population top-p. But the **continuous, correlation-based** metric shows systematically higher costs — the lottery is more damaging to fine-grained calibration with true skill than to coarse top-p selection accuracy, because a Bernoulli(p) redraw injects pure noise into the *ranking* of redrawn applicants even when it doesn't change their binary accept/reject outcome as often. Practically: a regulator or platform evaluating this mitigation with a pass/fail hiring metric will see ~15–22% cost; one evaluating it against a continuous quality score will see ~24–35%. Both are correct; they measure different harms, and the mitigation is worse for the latter than the headline figure alone suggests.

### 3.4 Which metric should a mandate target?

H and S diverge in their sensitivity to K: H is nearly flat across K (Sec. 3.1) while S grows sharply with K. This is not a contradiction — H measures average pairwise agreement between any two institutions, which does not mechanically increase as more institutions are added, while S measures the probability that *all* K simultaneously reject an applicant, which compounds combinatorially with K even at fixed pairwise correlation. **S is the metric that reflects applicant-facing harm** — the real-world quantity Bommasani et al. (2026) measure and the one a mandate should be defined against — because it directly answers "what fraction of people are shut out everywhere they apply," which depends on how many institutions an applicant realistically encounters, not just on how correlated any one pair of institutions is. H remains useful diagnostically (it isolates the *structural* cause — shared vendor/model dependence — independent of how many institutions a given applicant happens to apply to), but a regulator setting a numeric threshold for a decorrelation mandate should set it on S at a realistic K for the market being regulated, not on H.

## 4. Conclusion

We quantify how correlated errors (ρ) translate into outcome homogenization and systemic rejection, and how a cheap randomized-lottery mitigation trades accuracy for decorrelation at a cost that scales smoothly with monoculture severity (~15% at ρ=0.5 to ~22% at ρ=0.8 under threshold-based accuracy, ~24–35% under a continuous correlation-based accuracy measure — a genuine, metric-dependent range rather than a single number). Targeting the mitigation at a known correlated vendor cluster does not beat blind uniform application at matched intervention mass — but this specific equivalence is a property of the uniform-Bernoulli redraw mechanism: a boundary-proximity-biased redraw mechanism makes targeting and blind application diverge substantially, with targeting buying faster decorrelation at a steeper accuracy cost. For regulatory purposes we argue S (systemic rejection excess), not H (pairwise correlation), is the right quantity to write a mandate against, since S — not H — captures how harm compounds with the number of institutions an applicant actually encounters. Limitations: the block model uses two equal-size clusters and two stylized redraw mechanisms (uniform, boundary-window); it does not exhaust the space of possible mitigation designs, and the boundary-window mechanism is a simple proxy for "smarter" targeting rather than an optimized one. Modeling partially-overlapping vendor structure and optimizing (rather than hand-specifying) a boundary-aware mechanism are the natural next steps. The paper's contribution remains incremental relative to Bommasani et al. (2022, 2026): a swept, quantified, cross-validated mitigation tradeoff curve, a targeting-vs-mechanism comparison, and a metric-choice sensitivity analysis, not a new theoretical mechanism.
