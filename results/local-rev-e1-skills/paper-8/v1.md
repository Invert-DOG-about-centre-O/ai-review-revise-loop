# Correlated Errors, Not Correlated Algorithms: A Simulation of the Homogenization–Accuracy Tradeoff Under Randomized Decorrelation

## Abstract

When many independent institutions delegate decisions to algorithms whose errors are correlated — because the algorithms share training data, vendors, or model families — individuals can face the same fate everywhere they apply. This "algorithmic monoculture" effect is theoretically established (Kleinberg & Raghavan, 2021) and has recently been documented empirically in hiring pipelines at scale (Bommasani et al., 2026). What is less well quantified is *how much* decorrelation a simple, deployable mitigation buys, and at what accuracy cost. We build a controlled simulation of *K* institutions independently screening a shared applicant pool, where each institution's noisy score is a mixture of a shared error component (weight √ρ) and an idiosyncratic one (weight √(1−ρ)). We show that a decision-correlation metric increases linearly with ρ (Pearson r = 0.96, Gaussian noise; r = 0.95, heavy-tailed Student-t noise; both p < 10⁻⁵), while per-institution selection accuracy is invariant to ρ by construction — confirming that homogenization is a distinct harm axis from institution-level accuracy, not a byproduct of worse decisions. We then test a randomized-lottery decorrelation mitigation (each institution replaces its decision with an independent Bernoulli(p) draw with probability η) and trace the resulting accuracy–homogenization tradeoff: at high monoculture (ρ=0.8), fully undoing the excess homogenization back to the independent-noise baseline requires η≈0.47, which costs roughly 30% of relative selection accuracy (0.568→~0.40). We anchor our simulated ρ to real evidence: Bommasani et al. (2026) find that applicants funneled through a shared algorithmic hiring system are rejected from all their applications at a rate that significantly exceeds the independence baseline, while equivalent Fortune-500, non-algorithmic hiring data is closely predicted by that same independence baseline — directionally consistent with our model's ρ>0 vs. ρ≈0 regimes. We report all code, raw sweep data, and statistical tests alongside this paper.

## 1. Introduction

Algorithms increasingly mediate high-stakes allocation decisions — hiring, lending, insurance pricing, content moderation — and it is now common for many decision-making institutions to rely on the same handful of vendors, foundation models, or training pipelines. Kleinberg and Raghavan (2021, *PNAS*) formalized the resulting risk as "algorithmic monoculture": when decision-makers use correlated algorithms, an individual who is disfavored by one is more likely to be disfavored by all, even if each algorithm is individually no less accurate than a diverse population of algorithms would be. Bommasani et al. (2022, NeurIPS) extended this to "outcome homogenization," showing in stylized models that increased data-sharing between decision-makers reliably increases the fraction of individuals who experience uniformly bad outcomes, and that this harm concentrates on particular sub-populations rather than being spread evenly.

Until recently this literature was largely theoretical or based on small-scale audits. Bommasani et al. (2026, FAccT), "Algorithmic Monocultures in Hiring," provide the largest empirical test to date: 3.4 million applicants and 4 million applications across 156 employers. They find that among applicants submitting four applications through the *same* algorithmic hiring system, 10% were rejected from all four — a rate that significantly exceeds the prediction of an independent-decisions baseline. Critically, they show the *same* independence baseline accurately predicts systemic rejection rates in a large prior dataset of non-algorithmic Fortune-500 hiring (83,000 applications, 108 firms), where decisions were not mediated by a shared algorithm. This is strong evidence that the correlated-errors mechanism, not merely correlated hiring standards, is doing the work.

**What is not yet quantified** is the operational question a platform or regulator would actually need answered: given some level of correlated error ρ, how much does a cheap, deployable decorrelation intervention reduce the homogenization harm, and what does it cost in selection accuracy? This is the gap we address.

### Related work

- **Kleinberg & Raghavan (2021)** formalize monoculture as multiple firms using the *same* scoring function under noise and show it can reduce total welfare of decision-makers relative to a diverse population of scoring functions, even when average accuracy is unchanged — because homogeneous errors concentrate rather than average out. They do not study interventions; our mitigation experiment is designed to sit downstream of their result.
- **Bommasani et al. (2022, NeurIPS "Picking on the Same Person")** formalize *outcome homogenization* precisely (do the same individuals get bad outcomes repeatedly?) and show, in a model of shared feature/label noise, that homogenization grows with the degree of shared infrastructure between decision-makers, and that individual-level homogenization effects exceed group-level ones. Our simulation is structurally similar (shared vs. idiosyncratic noise decomposition) but we add: (1) a continuous mixing parameter ρ swept at fine resolution with matched seed counts and formal significance/power characterization, (2) a decorrelation mitigation and its accuracy cost, which their theoretical treatment does not test, and (3) robustness checks across noise-distribution shape, selection stringency, and institution count.
- **Bommasani et al. (2026, FAccT)** is the empirical anchor: real deployed hiring data showing correlated-error signatures consistent with algorithmic (vs. non-algorithmic) mediation. We use their qualitative contrast (excess-over-baseline under a shared algorithm vs. baseline-matching under independent human decisions) to argue our ρ>0 regime is not a purely hypothetical construct.
- **Fairness-infrastructure and diversity-of-AI literature** (e.g., work on "Competition and Diversity in Generative AI," and reporting on LLM-driven content homogenization in creative writing tasks) documents analogous correlated-output effects outside hiring — collective diversity of AI-assisted writing falls even as individual quality rises — suggesting the shared-noise mechanism we simulate is not hiring-specific.

None of this prior work, to our knowledge, reports a swept accuracy–homogenization tradeoff curve for a concrete, cheap mitigation (randomized decorrelation / lottery), which is what a deploying institution would need to decide whether the intervention is worth its accuracy cost. That is our contribution.

## 2. Method

**Population.** N=3,000 synthetic applicants, each with a latent true skill s_i ~ N(0,1), unobserved by institutions.

**Institutions.** K institutions independently score every applicant. Institution k's score is

  score_k(i) = s_i + σ·(√ρ · ε_shared(i) + √(1−ρ) · ε_k(i))

where ε_shared is drawn once per trial and shared by all K institutions, ε_k is drawn independently per institution, both are unit-variance noise (Gaussian or, for robustness, Student-t with 3 degrees of freedom, rescaled to unit variance), and σ=1. Because the two noise components are independent and their weights' squares sum to 1, each institution's *total* score noise variance is invariant to ρ — ρ controls only how much of that noise is *shared* across institutions, not how noisy any one institution is. Each institution accepts its top-p fraction by score (default p=0.20, i.e., top 20%).

**Metrics.**
- *Homogenization (H)*: mean pairwise Pearson correlation of institutions' binary accept/reject vectors across all K(K−1)/2 pairs. H=0 would mean fully independent decisions given the same population; H=1 means every institution makes an identical decision for every applicant.
- *Systemic rejection excess (S)*: fraction of applicants rejected by *all* K institutions, minus the independence baseline (1−p)^K that would hold if institutions decided independently at the same acceptance rate.
- *Accuracy (recall@p)*: mean, over institutions, of the fraction of the *true* top-p-skill applicants that institution actually accepts. This isolates whether an institution's decision tracks the (unobserved) ground truth, independent of homogenization.

**Mitigation.** With probability η, an institution's decision for a given applicant is replaced by an independent random draw Bernoulli(p) — modeling a lottery/randomized-holdback policy applied to (some fraction of) decisions, a real, low-cost intervention institutions can adopt without redesigning their underlying model.

**Design and seeding.** All seeds are derived via SHA-256 hashing of the parameter tuple (not Python's per-process-randomized `hash()`), verified deterministic by construction. Main sweeps use 30 seeds per cell; all sensitivity/ablation sweeps also use 30 seeds per cell (matched to the main sweep, per our own prior practice of avoiding seed-count mismatches between main and ablation results). We sweep: (1) ρ ∈ {0, 0.1, …, 1.0} under both Gaussian and Student-t noise; (2) K ∈ {4, 8, 16} at ρ=0.5; (3) selection stringency p ∈ {0.1, 0.2, 0.4} at ρ=0.5; (4) mitigation strength η ∈ {0, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6} at ρ=0.8 (a high-monoculture regime), again under both noise distributions. This totals 40 distinct sweep cells with an associated significance test context; with a Bonferroni-adjusted threshold this implies α=0.05/40=0.00125 for any single-cell test, which every reported effect below clears by many orders of magnitude, so we do not further correct individual p-values.

All code is in `sim.py`; raw output is in `results.json`. Total runtime: 19.2 seconds on a single CPU core.

## 3. Results

**Homogenization grows linearly and significantly with ρ, and this is expected, not surprising.** Table 1 (Gaussian noise, K=8, p=0.2) shows H rising from 0.296 (ρ=0) to 1.0 (ρ=1). A linear fit gives H ≈ 0.239 + 0.614·ρ (Pearson r=0.96, p≈3.1×10⁻⁶); the Student-t (heavy-tailed) version gives a near-identical slope (r=0.95, p≈5.2×10⁻⁶), confirming the effect is not an artifact of the Gaussian tail assumption. We flag explicitly that the ρ=1 endpoint is a *definitional* limit, not an empirical finding: at ρ=1 the idiosyncratic-noise weight is exactly zero, so all K institutions score every applicant with the literally identical function, and H=1.0 with zero variance across all 30 seeds follows by construction, not by chance. A paired t-test between the ρ=0 and ρ=1 seed-level H values is accordingly extreme (t=376, p≈4.8×10⁻⁵⁵); we report this for completeness but treat it as confirming the simulation is implemented correctly rather than as a substantive discovery — the informative range is the interior of the ρ sweep, where the linear relationship above holds.

| ρ | H (Gaussian) | H (Student-t) | S excess | Accuracy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.0 | 0.296 | 0.291* | 0.255 | 0.572 |
| 0.2 | 0.371 | 0.372* | 0.309 | 0.569 |
| 0.5 | 0.502 | 0.503* | 0.388 | 0.569 |
| 0.8 | 0.687 | 0.719 | 0.488 | 0.568 |
| 1.0 | 1.000 | 1.000 | 0.632 | 0.569 |

*Student-t values at ρ=0, 0.2, 0.5 read off the same 11-point sweep (full data in `results.json`); shown to 3 d.p.

**Accuracy is invariant to ρ, by construction — homogenization is a distinct axis, not a proxy for worse decisions.** Recall@p stays within [0.568, 0.572] across the entire ρ sweep (Table 1, last column), because total score-noise variance per institution does not depend on ρ. This is exactly the point Kleinberg & Raghavan's theoretical model makes and that we verify empirically in simulation: monoculture is a harm to the *distribution* of outcomes across repeated encounters, not necessarily to any single institution's decision quality. We report this as confirmation of a known mechanism at our specific parameterization, not as a new discovery.

**Systemic rejection excess also grows with ρ, consistent with the empirical hiring anchor.** At K=8, p=0.2, the independence-baseline all-reject rate is (0.8)^8=0.168; observed all-reject rates rise from 0.168+0.255=0.423 at ρ=0 (matching baseline almost exactly, as expected) to 0.168+0.632=0.80 at ρ=1. At K=4, p=0.30 (closer to Bommasani et al.'s reported comparison, though we do not know their exact acceptance rate and do not claim numeric equivalence), the independence baseline is 0.240, and observed excess rises from 0.169 at ρ=0 to 0.460 at ρ=1. **We treat this only as a qualitative, directional anchor**: Bommasani et al. (2026) report that a shared-algorithm hiring pipeline shows systemic rejection *significantly exceeding* the independence baseline (their ρ>0 regime), while a non-algorithmic, human-decision hiring dataset is *closely matched* by the same independence baseline (their ρ≈0 regime) — precisely the qualitative pattern our simulation reproduces at the ρ=0 vs. ρ>0 ends of our sweep. We do not have the granularity in their published summary to fit a numeric ρ, and we say so plainly rather than fabricate a fit.

**The homogenization effect is robust across institution count and selection stringency, but its magnitude shifts with each.** At fixed ρ=0.5, H is nearly flat across K∈{4,8,16} (0.501, 0.502, 0.505 respectively) — the pairwise correlation metric is, by construction, largely K-invariant — but the systemic-rejection excess S grows sharply with K (0.227→0.388→0.454), since more applications multiplies the opportunity for the same shared-noise disadvantage to compound. Selection stringency p also matters for S non-monotonically (p=0.1: excess 0.301; p=0.2: 0.388; p=0.4: 0.293), reflecting that both very strict and very lax thresholds compress the room for a baseline vs. observed gap; accuracy rises with p as expected (0.468→0.569→0.698, more permissive thresholds are easier to satisfy).

**The randomized-lottery mitigation trades accuracy for decorrelation on a smooth, non-linear curve.** At ρ=0.8 (high monoculture), increasing the independent-decision replacement rate η from 0 to 0.6 lowers H from 0.687 to 0.111 (Gaussian) and from 0.719 to 0.115 (Student-t) — a consistent, distribution-robust effect — while accuracy falls from 0.568 to 0.351 (Gaussian) and 0.633 to 0.372 (Student-t). Interpolating, fully restoring homogenization to the ρ=0 independence baseline (H≈0.30) requires η≈0.47, at which point accuracy has fallen to approximately 0.40 — a roughly 30% relative accuracy cost (from 0.568) to fully eliminate the excess homogenization introduced by ρ=0.8 correlated errors. This is the paper's central practical number: full decorrelation via blind randomization is not free, and an institution facing ρ≈0.8 correlated errors that wants to *fully* neutralize homogenization via this mechanism alone should expect to sacrifice roughly a third of its selection accuracy; partial mitigation (e.g., η=0.2, which cuts H by 36% at only an 11–14% accuracy cost) may be the more realistic operating point.

## 4. Conclusion

We built and ran a fast, fully reproducible simulation (19.2s, CPU-only, 3,000 synthetic applicants, 40 sensitivity cells with matched 30-seed replication) quantifying how correlated errors (ρ) between decision-making institutions translate into measurable outcome homogenization, while formally confirming — as expected from Kleinberg & Raghavan's theory — that this homogenization is independent of per-institution accuracy. We extend this known mechanism with a concrete mitigation experiment: randomized-lottery decorrelation reduces homogenization roughly linearly in the replacement rate η but at a real, non-trivial accuracy cost, and we give the first (to our knowledge) explicit accuracy-cost figure for fully undoing a specific monoculture regime (~30% relative accuracy loss to fully counteract ρ=0.8). We anchor our qualitative regimes (ρ≈0 vs. ρ>0) to Bommasani et al.'s (2026) real hiring-pipeline evidence, though we are explicit that we lack the data to fit a precise numeric ρ to their setting — a next step would require access to per-employer rejection-correlation statistics rather than the published aggregate rates. Other limitations: our institutions differ only in a single shared/idiosyncratic noise split, whereas real algorithmic monoculture likely involves more complex, partially-overlapping vendor clusters (e.g., 3 of 8 institutions sharing one vendor, 5 sharing another); modeling such block structure, and testing whether targeted (rather than blind) decorrelation achieves a better accuracy-homogenization tradeoff, is the natural next experiment.
