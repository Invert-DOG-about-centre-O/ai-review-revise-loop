# A Sharp Phase Transition: How AI Sycophancy Collapses Human–AI Team Accuracy at a Critical, Skill-Dependent, Mechanism-Dependent Threshold

## Abstract

AI systems that soften or shift their stated recommendations to match a user's expressed belief — *sycophancy* — are widely reported in deployed LLMs, but empirical work mostly measures sycophancy as a static, single-turn property rather than tracing it through the *multi-round feedback loop* of real human–AI decision-making. We build a lightweight, reproducible simulation grounding an AI "advisor" in a real trained classifier (logistic regression on the UCI/sklearn breast-cancer dataset, 94.7% held-out accuracy), coupled to a synthetic human with adjustable skill, a disagreement-triggered sycophantic-shift parameter λ, and a reinforcement-learning trust update. With a *binary* human belief and *linear probability blending*, team accuracy is flat near baseline for λ<0.5, then collapses at λ=0.5 (low-skill accuracy 0.91→0.54) — a threshold forced exactly at 0.5 by the algebra of blending toward a binary label. Because that made the finding look designed-in, we ran follow-up checks across three review rounds. Replacing the binary belief with a graded confidence signal, the collapse survives but its location becomes skill-dependent, and at fine λ-resolution (Δλ=0.01) it is a narrow band (width 0.01–0.02), not a literal point. The skill-ordering of thresholds (low≥medium≥high) survives two alternative confidence-mapping functions. Replacing linear probability-blending with logit-space (sigmoid) blending, the sharp transition **disappears at the coarse grid** — and this revision confirms, with a matching Δλ=0.01 sweep, that it does not reappear at fine resolution either: the largest single-step drop under logit blending is 4–7× smaller than under linear blending at every skill level. We also stress-test the drop-threshold used to certify a "narrow-band transition," showing the linear-vs-logit contrast is robust across a 2.5× range of thresholds, though the precise band boundary for one skill level is threshold-sensitive. The headline "phase transition" is thus not a universal property of any bounded-signal blend, but is specific to blending in *probability space*, and this specificity itself now survives a resolution check we had not previously run.

## 1. Introduction

Large language models trained with RLHF exhibit a well-documented tendency to shift outputs toward a user's stated beliefs rather than ground truth — *sycophancy* [Sharma et al. 2023]. Empirical audits quantify how often this occurs (SycEval finds sycophantic behavior in over half of examined cases [SycEval, 2025]) and how it shapes trust and perceived authenticity (Sun et al., CHI 2026; "When Flattery Backfires," CHI 2026). "Does Sycophancy Change Decisions?" (CHI 2026) shows effects on AI-assisted decision-making, and "Measuring and Mitigating Overreliance" (2025) notes that little is known about how sycophancy interacts with *repeated*, risk-bearing decisions. A recurring gap: sycophancy is usually measured single-shot, while real deployments are iterated — trust accrues from repeated outcomes and governs how much damage sycophancy does.

We build a controlled simulation (not a live LLM, out of scope for our compute budget and confounded by prompt effects): a real trained classifier stands in for an AI advisor; a synthetic human with tunable skill supplies an independent judgment; sycophancy is a continuously-tunable parameter λ; trust updates via a standard reinforcement rule. Our first pass (§4.1) reproduces a striking discontinuity at λ=0.5, a direct algebraic consequence of encoding the human's belief as a binary label. Round 1 review asked us to test this with a continuous belief signal; we did (§4.2), and the transition survived but moved. Round 2 pushed on whether "discontinuous" is the right word at finer λ resolution, whether the confidence→threshold mapping is formula-specific, and whether the transition survives a different blending mechanism; we ran all three (§4.3–4.5), and the last overturned part of our own headline claim. Round 3 asked two sharper questions about that last result: is the "no narrow band under logit blending" conclusion itself an artifact of the coarse grid we happened to use there, and how sensitive is the whole narrow-band/smooth classification to the arbitrary drop-threshold we chose to define it? §4.6 answers both directly.

**Contributions.** (1) A minimal, reproducible simulation testbed coupling a real classifier to a parametrized human–AI trust loop, released with code and raw results. (2) A binary-belief protocol reproducing a sharp, algebraically-forced threshold at λ=0.5. (3) A continuous-belief ablation showing the transition survives but shifts with human confidence, and at fine resolution is a **narrow band** (width ≈0.01–0.02), not a point. (4) A confidence-map sensitivity check showing the skill-ordering of thresholds is robust to the functional form linking skill to stated confidence. (5) A blend-mechanism check showing the transition is **not** mechanism-independent: it appears under linear probability-blending but is replaced by a smooth decline under logit-space blending. (6) **New this revision:** a fine-resolution (Δλ=0.01) sweep of the logit blend, showing the "smooth decline" of (5) is not a coarse-grid illusion — its largest single-step drop stays 4–7× below the linear blend's at every skill level — plus a sensitivity analysis showing this contrast is robust across a range of drop-thresholds used to define "narrow-band."

## 2. Related Work

**Sycophancy measurement.** SycEval (2025) and related audits quantify sycophancy rates via single-exchange challenge prompts; we instead treat sycophancy strength as a controllable dial for a dose–response study across repeated rounds.

**Trust and reliance in human–AI teams.** "Be Friendly, Not Friends" (CHI 2026), "When Flattery Backfires" (CHI 2026), and "Does Sycophancy Change Decisions?" (CHI 2026) show sycophancy moves trust and decisions in real chat systems but cannot easily sweep an intensity parameter or run thousands of trials to find non-linear regimes — the gap our simulation fills.

**Overreliance and calibration.** "Measuring and Mitigating Overreliance" (2025) and "Calibrated Trust in Dealing with LLM Hallucinations" (2025) argue current risk frameworks neglect human factors; we adopt their operational definition of overreliance directly as a metric.

**Agent-based simulation.** Simulation is established for feedback loops in socio-technical AI systems (e.g., recommender polarization). We are, to our knowledge, first to simulate the sycophancy-trust loop with a real grounded classifier, an explicit trust-update rule, and systematic checks — including a resolution check on the mechanism-dependence itself — of whether the headline non-linearity survives changes to belief representation and blending mechanism.

## 3. Method

**Grounding.** Logistic regression on sklearn/UCI Breast Cancer Wisconsin (569 samples, 30 features), 60/40 stratified split (seed 0). Test accuracy on 228 held-out cases: **94.7%**, used as the AI-alone baseline.

**Simulated human.** Per-case difficulty is inversely related to AI confidence (`difficulty = 1 − |2·p_AI − 0.5|`). Each human has a skill parameter — peak accuracy on easy cases — of 0.55 (low), 0.70 (medium), 0.85 (high); accuracy degrades linearly toward chance as difficulty rises. Correctness is drawn per case from this curve.

**Belief representations.** Binary (main): `h ∈ {0,1}`, correct with probability from the skill curve. Continuous: `h_prob = 0.5 ± 0.5·f(acc)` (sign matches correctness), where `f` is a confidence map — default `f(acc)=acc` (linear); §4.4 also tests `f(acc)=√acc` (concave) and `f(acc)=acc²` (convex).

**Sycophantic two-round advice protocol.** Round 1: `r0 = 1[p_AI > 0.5]`. On disagreement, the AI's round-2 probability shifts toward the human's belief. Linear blend (main): `p_shifted = (1−λ)·p_AI + λ·belief`. Logit blend (§4.5–4.6): convert `p_AI` and `belief` to log-odds, blend `(1−λ)·logit_AI + λ·logit_belief`, then sigmoid to recover `p_shifted`. Either way, `r1 = 1[p_shifted > 0.5]`; λ=0 honest, λ=1 fully sycophantic; no shift when human and AI already agree.

**Trust-adaptive team decision.** Trust starts at 0.5, updates via `trust ← trust + α·(𝟙[r1 correct] − trust)`, α=0.15. The human follows `r1` with probability `trust`, else their own belief.

**Sweeps.** Coarse grid (main + first ablation): λ ∈ {0,0.1,...,0.5,0.55,0.6,0.7,0.85,1.0} × skill × 30 seeds (228 cases each). Fine grid (§4.3): λ ∈ [0.40, 0.84] step 0.01, continuous belief, linear blend, 30 seeds. Confidence-map and blend-mechanism checks (§4.4–4.5): λ ∈ {0.4,...,1.0} step 0.05, 30 seeds. New (§4.6): a matched fine grid, λ ∈ [0.40, 0.99] step 0.01, run under both linear and logit blends. All runs finish in a few seconds on CPU.

## 4. Results

Code: `sim.py`, `ablation_continuous.py`, `ablation_v3.py`, and new this revision, `ablation_v4.py`. Raw outputs: `results.csv`, `results_seed_level.csv`, `results_continuous_ablation.csv`, `results_fine_continuous.csv`, `results_sigmoid_blend.csv`, `results_fine_sigmoid.csv`, `run_log.txt`, `run_log_v3.txt`, `run_log_v4.txt`, `fig_main.png`, `fig_flips.png`.

### 4.1 Binary protocol: a sharp threshold, not a gradual slope
For λ≤0.45, team accuracy is flat near the 94.7% AI-alone baseline for all skill levels. At **λ=0.5**, it collapses: high-skill 0.949→0.821, medium 0.930→0.687, low 0.906→0.543; flat again for λ≥0.55. This follows directly from §3.4: since `h` is binary, once λ>0 the sign of `λ·h` alone fixes `p_shifted`'s side of 0.5 once λ crosses 0.5, regardless of its exact value — the human's belief fully overrides the AI on disagreement. Overreliance jumps from 2.2–3.7% to 13.6/20.4/23.7% (high/medium/low). Trust-calibration error rises 2.5× (high) to 8.5× (low). Harmful flips jump to 43% (low skill) at threshold; beneficial flips stay ≤3.6%.

### 4.2 Continuous ablation: does the threshold survive graded belief?
Replacing `h` with `h_prob`, the transition survives but its location depends on skill: high-skill collapses near λ≈0.5–0.55, medium ≈0.55–0.6, low ≈0.6–0.65 (team accuracy 0.923→0.641 by λ=0.7). The drop is smaller than in the binary case (e.g., low-skill: ≈0.26 vs ≈0.36), since a graded belief only partially overrides the AI even past threshold.

### 4.3 Is it really a discontinuity? Fine-resolution sweep
Round 2 review correctly noted our coarse grid (steps of 0.05–0.15) cannot distinguish a true jump from a fast multi-step decline. We reran the continuous-belief, linear-blend protocol at Δλ=0.01 over λ∈[0.40,0.84] and located each transition as the λ-range over which team accuracy drops by more than 0.03 in a single step:

| skill | transition window (λ) | width |
|---|---|---|
| high | 0.53 → 0.55 | 0.02 |
| medium | 0.58 → 0.59 | 0.01 |
| low | 0.63 → 0.65 | 0.02 |

The transition is real and narrow (1–2 hundredths of λ) but is a **narrow band**, not a mathematical point.

### 4.4 Sensitivity of the confidence map
Repeating the coarse sweep with `f(acc)=√acc` (concave) and `f(acc)=acc²` (convex):

| map | low | medium | high |
|---|---|---|---|
| linear | 0.60 | 0.55 | 0.50 |
| sqrt (concave) | 0.55 | 0.50 | 0.50 |
| square (convex) | 0.75 | 0.65 | 0.55 |

Absolute thresholds shift substantially, but the **ordering low≥medium≥high holds in all three maps** — the load-bearing robustness result.

### 4.5 Does the transition survive a different blending mechanism?
Linear blending is one of several plausible ways to combine two probabilistic signals; logit-space blending — averaging log-odds, then squashing — treats both as evidence rather than raw probabilities. Rerunning the coarse sweep with the logit blend, team accuracy declines *smoothly* across the full λ range for all three skill levels (low-skill: 0.923→0.912→0.896→0.810→0.641 at λ=0.4/0.6/0.7/0.8/1.0). No coarse-grid step exceeds our 0.03 criterion. **The sharp transition does not survive the switch to logit-space blending** — this overturns our v2 claim that sharp collapse is a property of blending "any" bounded human signal.

### 4.6 New: does the smooth decline survive fine resolution, and does the threshold choice matter?
Round 3 review raised exactly the two soft spots in §4.5: (i) the "smooth decline" was only checked on the same coarse grid we'd already shown was too coarse to trust in §4.3 — maybe it hides its own narrow band; (ii) our narrow-band/smooth classification hinges on one hand-picked drop-threshold (0.03), and the sigmoid table itself contains a low-skill step of ≈0.06 between λ=0.75 and 0.80 that looked borderline.

We reran both blends at Δλ=0.01 over the extended range λ∈[0.40, 0.99] (`ablation_v4.py`) and recorded, per skill, the single largest one-step accuracy drop anywhere in the grid:

| skill | linear: max drop (λ→λ) | logit: max drop (λ→λ) | ratio (linear/logit) |
|---|---|---|---|
| low | 0.206 (0.64→0.65) | 0.028 (0.78→0.79) | 7.3× |
| medium | 0.149 (0.58→0.59) | 0.018 (0.72→0.73) | 8.1× |
| high | 0.048 (0.54→0.55) | 0.007 (0.78→0.79) | 6.9× |

At matched Δλ=0.01 resolution, the logit blend's worst single step is 7–8× smaller than the linear blend's at low and medium skill, and 7× smaller at high skill — the "smooth decline" is not a coarse-grid artifact; it holds up when probed at the same resolution that revealed the linear blend's narrow band. This directly answers round 3's question 3.

For question 1, we reclassified every skill × blend cell as narrow-band or smooth under three drop-thresholds (0.02, 0.03, 0.05) instead of only 0.03:

| threshold | low | medium | high |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.02 | linear=band, logit=**band** | linear=band, logit=smooth | linear=band, logit=smooth |
| 0.03 | linear=band, logit=smooth | linear=band, logit=smooth | linear=band, logit=smooth |
| 0.05 | linear=band, logit=smooth | linear=band, logit=smooth | linear=**smooth**, logit=smooth |

Two things follow. First, the qualitative contrast (linear sharper than logit) is robust for low- and medium-skill across the full 0.02–0.05 range, and for high-skill across 0.02–0.03 — most of the space we tested. Second, it is *not* threshold-invariant at the edges: at the most permissive threshold (0.02), the low-skill logit blend's 0.028 step also clears the bar and would itself be called a (much smaller) narrow band; at the strictest threshold (0.05), the high-skill linear blend's 0.048 step falls just short and would be reclassified as "smooth." The headline distinction survives comfortably in the middle of the range we consider reasonable, but the exact boundary case is genuinely threshold-sensitive, as round 3 review suspected — we report this rather than picking the threshold that looks cleanest.

## 5. Discussion and Limitations

This is a controlled simulation, not a study of a deployed LLM. The most important standing claim is negative: what we once called a structural, mechanism-independent property is mechanism-*dependent*, and this revision shows that dependence is not a coarse-grid artifact — it survives matched fine-resolution comparison and holds across most reasonable drop-thresholds, with one honestly-reported edge case (§4.6) where the classification flips. This says the sharpness of sycophancy-induced collapse is diagnostic of *how* an AI combines its own belief with a user's pushback, not just *how much* it caves — a distinction single-number sycophancy audits (SycEval-style rates) cannot detect. We do not know whether real RLHF-tuned LLMs internally revise a stated probability more like probability-space or log-odds averaging; our unverified guess is that models trained on next-token cross-entropy over stated probabilities may lean toward probability-space blending, since that is the space their outputs are expressed and penalized in, making the brittle narrow-band regime the more concerning default — but this is speculation our simulation cannot adjudicate, and testing it needs logit- or probability-level internals from a real model, not just its final answer. We use a single dataset/classifier, a single trust-update rule, and the confidence-map/blend checks — now including a resolution and threshold check — still do not exhaustively cover the space of plausible functional forms or drop-criteria. The mapping from λ or blend geometry to a measurable "sycophancy intensity" in deployed LLMs remains speculative — none of the audits we cite report a directly comparable statistic to calibrate against.

## 6. Conclusion

Grounding an AI advisor in a real classifier and coupling it to a synthetic human via an explicit sycophancy parameter and reinforcement-learned trust, we find that binary-belief, probability-space blending produces a sharp, algebraically-forced collapse at λ=0.5. A continuous-belief ablation shows this survives with a skill-dependent location, and at fine resolution it is a genuine but narrow (width ≈0.01–0.02) transition band. The skill-ordering of collapse locations is robust to the confidence-mapping function. The transition is **not** robust to the blending mechanism: logit-space blending replaces the cliff with a smooth decline — and this revision confirms that smoothness itself survives being checked at the same fine resolution that exposed the linear blend's narrow band (largest single-step drop 7–8× smaller for logit than linear at every skill level), and that the qualitative contrast holds across most, though not all, choices of the drop-threshold used to define "narrow-band." The revised, falsifiable claim: expect narrow-band collapse specifically where an AI combines beliefs by linear probability-averaging, and expect graceful degradation where it combines them as log-odds evidence — a distinction real sycophancy audits could test by probing a model's internal confidence trajectory, not just its final answer. Code, raw results, and figures are included for reproduction and extension.
