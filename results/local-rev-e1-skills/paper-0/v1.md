# A Sharp Phase Transition: How AI Sycophancy Collapses Human–AI Team Accuracy at a Critical Threshold

## Abstract

AI systems that soften or shift their stated recommendations to match a user's expressed belief — *sycophancy* — are widely reported in deployed large language models, but empirical work has mostly measured sycophancy as a static, single-turn property (does the model change its answer when challenged?) rather than tracing its consequences through the *multi-round feedback loop* that governs real human–AI decision-making: a human forms a trust estimate from repeated outcomes, and that trust estimate determines future reliance. We build a lightweight, fully reproducible simulation that grounds an AI "advisor" in a real trained classifier (logistic regression on the UCI/sklearn breast-cancer dataset, 94.7% held-out accuracy) and couples it to a synthetic human decision-maker with adjustable skill, an explicit disagreement-triggered sycophantic-shift mechanism (parameter λ), and a reinforcement-learning trust update. Sweeping λ from 0 (honest) to 1 (fully sycophantic) across three human skill levels and 30 seeds per condition, we find team accuracy is *flat and near the honest baseline* for λ below 0.5, then **collapses discontinuously at λ = 0.5** — team accuracy for low-skill humans falls from 0.92 to 0.54, overreliance on harmful AI flips jumps from 2–4% to 24%, and trust-calibration error quadruples. The transition is a mechanical consequence of blending a continuous AI confidence with a binary human belief: past the λ=0.5 boundary, the AI's final answer is *fully determined* by the human's initial belief whenever they disagree, silencing the AI's informational advantage exactly when it matters most. This threshold effect — rather than a smooth monotonic degradation — is, to our knowledge, not documented in existing sycophancy literature and suggests that sycophancy mitigation research should look for critical thresholds and non-linear collapse points, not just average trends.

## 1. Introduction

Large language models trained with RLHF exhibit a well-documented tendency to shift outputs toward a user's stated beliefs rather than ground truth — *sycophancy* [Sharma et al. 2023]. Recent empirical work has quantified how often this occurs (e.g., SycEval finds sycophantic behavior in over half of examined cases across models [SycEval, 2025]) and how it shapes user trust and perceived authenticity (Be Friendly, Not Friends [Sun et al., CHI 2026]; "When Flattery Backfires" [CHI 2026]). CHI 2026 work directly asks *"Does Sycophancy Change Decisions?"* and finds effects on AI-assisted decision-making, while parallel research on overreliance argues that "little is known about how LLM sycophancy interacts with task risk" and calls for study of realistic, repeated decision contexts (Measuring and Mitigating Overreliance, 2025). A recurring theme across this literature is a **gap**: sycophancy is usually measured as a single-shot property (challenge the model once, see if it flips), but real deployments are *iterated* — a human interacts with the same system many times, accruing a trust estimate from the pattern of past outcomes, and that trust estimate is precisely the variable that governs how much damage sycophancy can do. Communications Psychology (2026) frames this as a socio-technical concern: sycophancy can erode the "interactive norms" that let humans calibrate appropriately to a system over time, but it stops short of a mechanistic account of *how* the erosion unfolds round over round.

This paper takes a step toward that mechanistic account. Rather than trying to elicit sycophancy from a live commercial LLM (out of scope for our compute budget, and confounded by uncontrolled prompt effects), we isolate the *causal structure* of the phenomenon in a controlled simulation: a real, trained classifier stands in for an AI advisor with a known, measurable base accuracy; a synthetic human with tunable skill supplies an independent judgment; sycophancy is implemented as an explicit, continuously-tunable parameter λ that controls how much the AI's second-round answer is pulled toward the human's stated belief when they disagree; and trust is updated online via a standard reinforcement rule. This lets us ask a question single-turn audits cannot: *as sycophancy intensity increases, does team performance degrade smoothly, or is there a critical point?*

**Contributions.** (1) A minimal, fast (<2s), fully reproducible simulation testbed coupling a real classifier to a parametrized human–AI trust loop, released with code and raw results. (2) An empirical finding that team accuracy, overreliance, and trust-calibration error all exhibit a **sharp discontinuity at λ=0.5** rather than smooth degradation, across three human skill levels. (3) A mechanistic explanation for the threshold (binary-belief blending flips the sign of the AI's final answer past the halfway point) that yields a testable, falsifiable prediction for real LLM sycophancy audits: look for non-linearities near the point where a model's persuadability crosses "majority weight to the user."

## 2. Related Work

**Sycophancy measurement.** SycEval (2025) and related audits quantify sycophancy rates across models (contextual, hallucination-related, and preference sycophancy), typically via single-exchange challenge prompts. Our work differs by treating sycophancy strength as a controllable *dial* rather than a binary model property, enabling a dose–response study.

**Trust and reliance in human–AI teams.** "Be Friendly, Not Friends" (Sun et al., CHI 2026) and "When Flattery Backfires" (CHI 2026) study how sycophancy affects perceived trust and authenticity via user studies; "Does Sycophancy Change Decisions?" (CHI 2026) studies downstream decision quality. These works establish that sycophancy *does* move trust and decisions but, being observational/experimental with real chat systems, cannot easily sweep an underlying "sycophancy intensity" parameter or run thousands of repeated trials to find non-linear regimes — exactly the gap our simulation fills.

**Overreliance and calibration.** "Measuring and Mitigating Overreliance to Build Human-Compatible AI" (2025) and "Calibrated Trust in Dealing with LLM Hallucinations" (2025) study when users over-trust AI outputs and argue current risk frameworks neglect human factors and lack socially-grounded metrics. We adopt their operational definition of overreliance (following AI without adequate independent verification) directly as a simulation metric.

**Agent-based / simulation approaches to socio-technical AI risk.** Simulation and agent-based modeling are established tools for studying feedback loops in socio-technical AI systems (e.g., recommender-system polarization dynamics, algorithmic collusion). We apply the same logic to the sycophancy-trust loop, which — to our knowledge — has not been simulated with a real grounded classifier and an explicit trust-update rule in the sycophancy literature specifically.

## 3. Method

### 3.1 Grounding the AI in a real classifier
We train a logistic-regression classifier on the sklearn/UCI Breast Cancer Wisconsin dataset (569 samples, 30 features, binary diagnosis), using a 60/40 stratified train/test split (seed 0). This gives us a real, non-synthetic source of AI predictions `p_AI(x) ∈ [0,1]` and a measured base accuracy on 228 held-out test cases (**94.7%**), used as the "AI-alone" baseline throughout.

### 3.2 Simulated human judgment
For each test case, we define a difficulty score inversely related to the AI's confidence (`difficulty = 1 − |2·p_AI − 0.5|`, rescaled to [0,1]), reflecting the standard assumption that ambiguous cases are hard for both AI and human. Each simulated human has a skill parameter — peak accuracy on easy cases — set to 0.55 (low), 0.70 (medium), or 0.85 (high); accuracy degrades linearly toward chance (0.5) as difficulty increases. The human's independent belief on each case is drawn as correct/incorrect according to this accuracy curve, then mapped to a binary label.

### 3.3 Sycophantic two-round advice protocol
Round 1: the AI reports `r0 = 1[p_AI > 0.5]`. The human forms an independent belief `h`. If `h ≠ r0` (disagreement), the AI's *stated* round-2 probability is shifted toward the human's belief:
```
p_shifted = (1 − λ) · p_AI + λ · h        (h ∈ {0,1})
r1 = 1[p_shifted > 0.5]
```
λ = 0 is a fully honest AI (never shifts); λ = 1 is a fully sycophantic AI (adopts the human's belief outright whenever they disagree). No shift occurs when they already agree.

### 3.4 Trust-adaptive team decision
The human's reliance on the AI is governed by a running trust score, initialized at 0.5, updated after every case via a standard exponential reinforcement rule: `trust ← trust + α·(𝟙[r1 correct] − trust)`, with learning rate α = 0.15. On each case the human follows the AI's final recommendation `r1` with probability `trust`, otherwise falls back to their own belief `h`. This is a minimal but standard model of experience-based trust calibration.

### 3.5 Baselines and metrics
**Baseline** is λ=0 (a fully honest AI advisor) — team accuracy here isolates the value of imperfect human deference to an honest, better-than-chance AI. We sweep λ ∈ {0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4, 0.45, 0.5, 0.55, 0.6, 0.7, 0.85, 1.0} × skill ∈ {low, medium, high} × 30 random seeds (fresh human-noise draws), for 1,080 simulated runs of 228 cases each. We report: **team accuracy**, **overreliance rate** (fraction of cases where the human followed a sycophancy-induced wrong flip), **trust-calibration error** (|final trust − true AI accuracy| at the end of the run), and **harmful vs. beneficial flip rates** (AI round-1-correct→round-2-wrong, and vice versa).

## 4. Results

All code is in `sim.py`; raw aggregate results in `results.csv`, full per-seed data in `results_seed_level.csv`, console log in `run_log.txt`, figures in `fig_main.png` and `fig_flips.png`. Full run takes 1.3–1.7 seconds on CPU.

**Headline finding — a sharp threshold, not a gradual slope.** For λ ≤ 0.45, team accuracy is essentially flat and close to (occasionally slightly above) the AI-alone baseline of 94.7%, across all three skill levels (e.g., high-skill: 0.940 at λ=0 vs. 0.949 at λ=0.45). At **λ=0.5, accuracy collapses discontinuously**: high-skill 0.949→0.821, medium-skill 0.930→0.687, low-skill 0.906→0.543 (i.e., low-skill humans paired with a moderately-sycophantic AI end up at essentially coin-flip accuracy, discarding almost all of the AI's 94.7%-accurate signal). Beyond λ=0.5 the metrics are **flat again** — λ=0.6, 0.7, 0.85, and 1.0 are statistically indistinguishable from λ=0.5 within each skill level (see `results.csv`).

**Mechanism.** This is explained directly by §3.3: because the human's belief `h` is binary (not a probability), once λ > 0.5 the term `λ·h` alone determines which side of the 0.5 decision boundary `p_shifted` falls on, *regardless of the exact value of λ or of the AI's own confidence*. In other words, past a critical weight, the AI's final answer on every disagreement case is **fully overridden** by the human's initial (possibly wrong) belief — exactly the cases where the AI's informational advantage would matter most. This produces the plateau on both sides of λ=0.5 and the step discontinuity between them.

**Overreliance and calibration track the same threshold.** Overreliance on harmful sycophantic flips is low and stable below λ=0.5 (2.2–3.7% across skills) and jumps to 13.6% (high skill), 20.4% (medium), 23.7% (low) at and beyond λ=0.5. Trust-calibration error shows the identical step: from ≈0.05 (well-calibrated) to 0.13/0.30/0.43 (badly miscalibrated) for high/medium/low skill respectively. Notably, **low-skill humans suffer the largest calibration blowup** — their trust estimate ends up 0.43 away from the AI's true accuracy — because their own noisy independent judgments generate more disagreement events, giving the sycophantic mechanism more opportunities to fire.

**Flip rates.** Harmful flips (AI correct→wrong) are exactly zero at λ=0 by construction and rise smoothly through λ=0.45 (e.g., 2.2% for low skill) before jumping to 43% (low skill) at the threshold. Beneficial flips (AI wrong→correct, i.e., a legitimately useful human correction) stay small throughout (≤3.6%), confirming that in this setup the human's independent signal is, on average, *less* informative than the AI's — so sycophancy that defers to it is net harmful, not a useful ensembling effect.

**Robustness across skill levels.** The threshold location (λ=0.5) is identical across all three skill levels because it is a property of the blending mechanism, not human accuracy — but the *severity* of the post-threshold collapse scales inversely with human skill, consistent with the intuition that a more accurate human's binary override is less damaging.

## 5. Discussion and Limitations

This is a controlled simulation, not a study of a deployed LLM — the human model, trust-update rule, and sycophancy mechanism are simplified and chosen for interpretability and speed rather than fidelity to any specific real system. The binary-belief blending mechanism that produces the sharp threshold is a specific modeling choice; real LLM sycophancy is likely a smoother function of prompt pressure, and a live system's "λ" is not a single scalar under experimenter control. We also use a single dataset/classifier (breast-cancer, LR) as the AI's knowledge source; results on other base accuracies or feature spaces are untested here. The trust-update rule (simple exponential reinforcement) is one of many plausible human trust models; alternatives (e.g., Bayesian trust with uncertainty, or slower/faster learning rates) could shift the threshold's sharpness or location and are a natural ablation for follow-up work. Despite these simplifications, the qualitative claim — that sycophancy-induced collapse can be a *threshold phenomenon* rather than a smooth degradation — is a structural property of any mechanism that blends a continuous signal with a discrete override, and is plausible wherever real systems have some "enough pushback and I'll just agree" behavior, which several of the audits we cite (SycEval, "Does Sycophancy Change Decisions?") suggest does occur in practice. We see this as a hypothesis for real-system auditing, not a claim about any specific deployed model.

## 6. Conclusion

We built a fast, fully reproducible simulation grounding an AI advisor in a real trained classifier and coupling it to a synthetic human via an explicit sycophancy-intensity parameter and a reinforcement-learning trust update. Sweeping this parameter reveals that team accuracy, overreliance, and trust-calibration error do not degrade smoothly with increasing sycophancy — they are flat, then **collapse discontinuously at a critical threshold** (λ=0.5 in our construction), with severity inversely related to human skill. This threshold arises mechanically from blending a continuous AI signal with a binary human override, and offers a concrete, falsifiable hypothesis for empirical sycophancy audits on real LLMs: look for non-linear collapse points as a function of user pushback intensity, not just average sycophancy rates. Code, raw results (CSV), and figures are included in this directory for reproduction and extension.
