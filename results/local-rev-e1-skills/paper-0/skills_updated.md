# Skills / lessons for writing better first drafts

- Before framing a result as a "finding," check whether it follows directly from the algebra/construction of your own method. If it does, either (a) don't present it as a standalone empirical discovery, or (b) proactively run the cheap ablation that tests whether it's an artifact of a specific modeling choice (e.g., a discretization, a functional-form pick) — do this in the first draft, not only after a reviewer asks. Reviewers reliably catch "designed-in" results and penalize submitting them without a robustness check that was clearly affordable.
- Every summary statistic in the abstract must be a number you can point to in the results body, not a loose paraphrase. Compute the actual range/ratio across conditions and either report the range or the correct aggregate — don't round a spread of effects (e.g., 2.5x–8.5x) into one punchy headline figure (e.g., "quadruples") that doesn't match any single reported value.
- When a headline effect is a discontinuity/threshold/phase-transition claim, test it at finer resolution than your main sweep grid before submitting — a coarse grid cannot distinguish a true jump from a fast multi-step decline, and reviewers will ask for exactly this check.
- Avoid post-hoc, unjustified cutoffs used to classify results into categories (e.g., "a drop >0.03 counts as a narrow band"). Either use a threshold-free characterization (slope, derivative, AUC of the curve) or report a sensitivity analysis across a reasonable range of thresholds from the start, and be explicit about which conclusions are threshold-sensitive.
- When you run a series of ablations across revisions, check that you're not only ever varying one axis of the setup (e.g., only the human/behavioral-model side) while leaving other obviously-relevant axes (e.g., the base data-generating classifier/dataset, the update-rule hyperparameters) completely untested through every round. Reviewers will eventually flag the untested axis specifically, even after many rounds of otherwise-thorough ablations elsewhere.
- If your central phenomenon has a known general mathematical explanation (e.g., "blending in a bounded/probability space is sharper than blending in an unbounded/log-odds space before squashing" is a known property of interpolation geometry), explicitly connect it to that existing theory/prior work. Otherwise, reviewers will read framing like "phase transition" or "sharp threshold" as overclaiming novelty even when the empirical execution is rigorous — connect mechanism to prior theory before claiming surprise value.
- More rounds of technically excellent, honestly-reported ablations do not guarantee a monotonically increasing review score. Once the easy methodological critiques (missing ablations, unverified thresholds) are exhausted, reviewers scrutinize the core contribution's novelty/significance more sharply — a later draft that only narrows and hedges the claim, without deepening engagement with related work or connecting to existing theory, can score lower than an earlier, less-defended draft. Budget revision effort toward broadening framing and literature grounding, not only toward answering the last reviewer's specific ablation request.
- Related-work sections that give one-sentence characterizations of each cited paper get flagged as shallow, and this critique tends to recur across multiple review rounds unaddressed if not fixed early — engage substantively with at least the most central 2-3 citations (what they measured, what they didn't test, how your parameters relate to any quantity they report) rather than treating related work as a list to check off.
- If your method is a simulation/proxy standing in for a real-world system (e.g., a toy trust-update rule standing in for real LLM sycophancy), try to find at least one empirical anchor point from cited literature to calibrate your key parameter against, even approximately. Leaving the mapping from simulation parameter to real-world quantity entirely speculative through every revision is a recurring, unresolved weakness reviewers keep raising.
- Ship deterministic, self-contained, fast-running code alongside every numeric claim in the paper. Full exact reproducibility (a reviewer can rerun your script and match your numbers to 3+ decimal places) was consistently rewarded across every review round in this paper's process — it's cheap to provide and materially increases reviewer trust.
