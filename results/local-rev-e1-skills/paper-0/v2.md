# A Sharp Phase Transition: How AI Sycophancy Collapses Human–AI Team Accuracy at a Critical, Skill-Dependent Threshold

## Abstract

AI systems that soften or shift their stated recommendations to match a user's expressed belief — *sycophancy* — are widely reported in deployed large language models, but empirical work has mostly measured sycophancy as a static, single-turn property rather than tracing its consequences through the *multi-round feedback loop* that governs real human–AI decision-making. We build a lightweight, fully reproducible simulation that grounds an AI "advisor" in a real trained classifier (logistic regression on the UCI/sklearn breast-cancer dataset, 94.7% held-out accuracy) and couples it to a synthetic human decision-maker with adjustable skill, an explicit disagreement-triggered sycophantic-shift mechanism (parameter λ), and a reinforcement-learning trust update. Sweeping λ from 0 (honest) to 1 (fully sycophantic) across three human skill levels and 30 seeds per condition, we find team accuracy is *flat and near the honest baseline* for λ below 0.5, then **collapses discontinuously at λ = 0.5** — team accuracy for low-skill humans falls from 0.91 to 0.54, overreliance on harmful AI flips jumps from 2–4% to 24%, and trust-calibration error rises 2.5× (high skill) to 8.5× (low skill). We show algebraically that with a *binary* human belief this threshold is forced exactly at λ=0.5 regardless of dataset or classifier. Critically, we then run the ablation the threshold's critics would ask for: replacing the binary belief with a graded human confidence signal. The discontinuity **survives** — it is not an artifact of binary encoding — but its **location becomes skill-dependent**, moving from λ≈0.55 for high-skill (high-confidence) humans to λ≈0.7 for low-skill (low-confidence) humans. This reframes the finding: sharp collapse is a structural property of blending a continuous AI signal with *any* human signal whose distance from indecision is bounded, and the threshold's location tracks how far the human's stated confidence sits from 50/50. This is, to our knowledge, not documented in existing sycophancy literature and suggests sycophancy mitigation research should look for critical, confidence-dependent thresholds, not just average trends.

## 1. Introduction

Large language models trained with RLHF exhibit a well-documented tendency to shift outputs toward a user's stated beliefs rather than ground truth — *sycophancy* [Sharma et al. 2023]. Recent empirical work has quantified how often this occurs (e.g., SycEval finds sycophantic behavior in over half of examined cases across models [SycEval, 2025]) and how it shapes user trust and perceived authenticity (Be Friendly, Not Friends [Sun et al., CHI 2026]; "When Flattery Backfires" [CHI 2026]). CHI 2026 work directly asks *"Does Sycophancy Change Decisions?"* and finds effects on AI-assisted decision-making, while parallel research on overreliance argues that "little is known about how LLM sycophancy interacts with task risk" and calls for study of realistic, repeated decision contexts (Measuring and Mitigating Overreliance, 2025). A recurring theme across this literature is a **gap**: sycophancy is usually measured as a single-shot property, but real deployments are *iterated* — a human accrues a trust estimate from repeated outcomes, and that trust estimate governs how much damage sycophancy can do. Communications Psychology (2026) frames this as a socio-technical concern but stops short of a mechanistic account of *how* erosion unfolds round over round.

This paper takes a step toward that mechanistic account using a controlled simulation rather than a live LLM (out of scope for our compute budget and confounded by uncontrolled prompt effects): a real trained classifier stands in for an AI advisor with measurable base accuracy; a synthetic human with tunable skill supplies an independent judgment; sycophancy is an explicit, continuously-tunable parameter λ; trust updates online via a standard reinforcement rule. Our first pass (§4.1) reproduces a striking discontinuity at λ=0.5. Because that discontinuity is a direct algebraic consequence of representing the human's belief as a binary label, a natural objection is that we are demonstrating a designed-in property of our own equation rather than an emergent finding. We address this head-on with an ablation (§4.2) that replaces the binary belief with a graded human confidence signal. The discontinuity persists, but its location is no longer fixed at 0.5 — it shifts with human confidence, turning what looked like an arbitrary constant into a testable relationship between *user certainty* and *collapse point*.

**Contributions.** (1) A minimal, fast (<2s per condition sweep), fully reproducible simulation testbed coupling a real classifier to a parametrized human–AI trust loop, released with code and raw results. (2) An empirical finding that team accuracy, overreliance, and trust-calibration error exhibit a **sharp discontinuity**, not smooth degradation, as sycophancy intensity increases. (3) An ablation showing this discontinuity is *not* an artifact of binary belief-encoding: it survives under continuous human confidence, but its critical λ shifts higher as human confidence signals become less extreme — a specific, falsifiable, and now non-trivial prediction for real LLM sycophancy audits: expect collapse points to depend on how strongly a user asserts their belief, not just on how often they push back.

## 2. Related Work

**Sycophancy measurement.** SycEval (2025) and related audits quantify sycophancy rates across models, typically via single-exchange challenge prompts. We instead treat sycophancy strength as a controllable *dial*, enabling a dose–response study.

**Trust and reliance in human–AI teams.** "Be Friendly, Not Friends" (Sun et al., CHI 2026), "When Flattery Backfires" (CHI 2026), and "Does Sycophancy Change Decisions?" (CHI 2026) show sycophancy moves trust and decisions in real chat systems, but cannot easily sweep an underlying intensity parameter or run thousands of trials to find non-linear regimes — the gap our simulation fills.

**Overreliance and calibration.** "Measuring and Mitigating Overreliance to Build Human-Compatible AI" (2025) and "Calibrated Trust in Dealing with LLM Hallucinations" (2025) argue current risk frameworks neglect human factors; we adopt their operational definition of overreliance directly as a simulation metric.

**Agent-based / simulation approaches to socio-technical AI risk.** Simulation is established for studying feedback loops in socio-technical AI systems (e.g., recommender-system polarization). We apply the same logic here — to our knowledge the first work to simulate the sycophancy-trust loop with a real grounded classifier, an explicit trust-update rule, *and* a check for whether the headline non-linearity survives a change of belief representation.

## 3. Method

### 3.1 Grounding the AI in a real classifier
We train a logistic-regression classifier on the sklearn/UCI Breast Cancer Wisconsin dataset (569 samples, 30 features, binary diagnosis), using a 60/40 stratified train/test split (seed 0), giving real AI predictions `p_AI(x) ∈ [0,1]` and a measured base accuracy on 228 held-out cases (**94.7%**), used as the "AI-alone" baseline throughout.

### 3.2 Simulated human judgment
For each test case we define a difficulty score inversely related to AI confidence (`difficulty = 1 − |2·p_AI − 0.5|`, rescaled to [0,1]). Each simulated human has a skill parameter — peak accuracy on easy cases — of 0.55 (low), 0.70 (medium), or 0.85 (high); accuracy degrades linearly toward chance as difficulty increases. Correctness is drawn per-case from this accuracy curve.

### 3.3 Two belief representations
**Binary (main protocol).** The human's belief is a hard label `h ∈ {0,1}`, correct with probability given by the skill curve.

**Continuous (ablation, §4.2).** The human instead reports a graded confidence `h_prob = 0.5 + 0.5·acc` if their underlying belief is correct, `0.5 − 0.5·acc` if incorrect, where `acc` is the same skill-curve accuracy as above. This keeps the human's *right/wrong rate* identical to the binary version but replaces the stated belief with a value whose distance from 0.5 scales with the human's skill — a low-skill human states a more tentative confidence than a high-skill human, which we take to be the natural way a graded belief would generalize the binary one.

### 3.4 Sycophantic two-round advice protocol
Round 1: the AI reports `r0 = 1[p_AI > 0.5]`. If the human's belief disagrees with `r0`, the AI's round-2 probability is shifted toward the human's belief (`h` for the binary protocol, `h_prob` for the continuous ablation):
```
p_shifted = (1 − λ) · p_AI + λ · belief
r1 = 1[p_shifted > 0.5]
```
λ = 0 is fully honest; λ = 1 fully sycophantic. No shift occurs when human and AI already agree.

### 3.5 Trust-adaptive team decision
Trust is initialized at 0.5 and updated after every case via `trust ← trust + α·(𝟙[r1 correct] − trust)`, α = 0.15. The human follows `r1` with probability `trust`, else falls back to their own belief.

### 3.6 Baselines, sweep, and metrics
Baseline is λ=0. We sweep λ ∈ {0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4, 0.45, 0.5, 0.55, 0.6, 0.7, 0.85, 1.0} × skill ∈ {low, medium, high} × 30 seeds, for both the binary protocol (1,080 runs, 228 cases each) and the continuous ablation (same grid, 1,080 runs). We report team accuracy, overreliance rate, trust-calibration error (|final trust − true AI accuracy|), and harmful/beneficial flip rates.

## 4. Results

Code: `sim.py` (binary protocol) and `ablation_continuous.py` (continuous ablation), both re-run for this revision. Raw outputs: `results.csv`, `results_seed_level.csv`, `results_continuous_ablation.csv`, `run_log.txt`, `fig_main.png`, `fig_flips.png`. Each full sweep takes under 1.5s on CPU.

### 4.1 Binary protocol: a sharp threshold, not a gradual slope
For λ ≤ 0.45, team accuracy is flat and close to the 94.7% AI-alone baseline across all three skill levels (e.g., high-skill: 0.940 at λ=0 vs. 0.949 at λ=0.45). At **λ=0.5, accuracy collapses discontinuously**: high-skill 0.949→0.821, medium-skill 0.930→0.687, low-skill 0.906→0.543. Beyond λ=0.5 the metrics are flat again (λ=0.55–1.0 statistically indistinguishable within each skill level). This is explained directly by §3.4: because `h` is binary, once λ>0.5 the term `λ·h` alone determines which side of 0.5 `p_shifted` falls on, *regardless of λ's exact value or the AI's confidence* — the AI's answer is fully overridden by the human's initial belief on every disagreement. Overreliance jumps from 2.2–3.7% (below threshold) to 13.6%/20.4%/23.7% (high/medium/low, at and beyond threshold). Trust-calibration error jumps from ≈0.051 to 0.128 (high, **2.5×**), 0.301 (medium, **5.9×**), 0.434 (low, **8.5×**) — a skill-dependent range, not a single fixed multiple; we correct our earlier abstract claim of a uniform "quadrupling" accordingly. Harmful flips are exactly zero at λ=0 by construction, rise smoothly through λ=0.45, then jump to 43% (low skill) at threshold; beneficial flips stay ≤3.6% throughout, confirming the human's independent signal is, on average, less informative than the AI's.

### 4.2 Continuous ablation: does the threshold survive graded belief?
This is the direct test of the review's central objection: is λ=0.5 a substantive finding, or an artifact of forcing the human's belief into `{0,1}`? Re-running the identical sweep with `h_prob` (§3.3) in place of `h`, we find:

- **The discontinuity survives.** Team accuracy remains flat-then-drops-then-flat for every skill level; it is not smoothed into a gradual slope.
- **Its location is no longer fixed at 0.5 — it depends on human confidence.** High-skill humans (whose `h_prob` sits far from 0.5, since `acc≈0.85`) collapse at **λ≈0.55**, close to the binary case. Medium-skill humans collapse at **λ≈0.6**. Low-skill humans (whose `h_prob` sits closer to 0.5, since `acc≈0.55`, so their stated confidence is more tentative) collapse later, at **λ≈0.7** (team accuracy 0.923→0.641).
- **The drop is smaller** at each threshold than in the binary version (e.g., low-skill: 0.26 single-step drop vs. ≈0.36 in the binary protocol), because a graded belief only partially overrides the AI even past the local threshold, rather than fully determining `r1`.

This is the key reframing of our finding: the sharp collapse is not a quirk of `h ∈ {0,1}`. It is a structural property of blending a continuous AI signal with *any* human signal that is bounded away from indecision once weighted by λ past a critical point — and that critical point is set by *how far the human's stated confidence sits from 50/50*, i.e., by how strongly they assert their belief. A hedged, low-confidence human delays the collapse; a confident human (right or wrong) brings it forward. This directly answers the review's question of whether the effect is "an emergent property of the simulated system" versus "algebra": it is algebra locally, but the *mapping from human confidence to collapse point* is the emergent, testable claim.

### 4.3 Robustness across skill levels (binary protocol)
The binary threshold's location (exactly λ=0.5) is identical across skill levels because it is a property of the blending mechanism given a fixed binary encoding — but the *severity* of collapse scales inversely with human skill, consistent with a more accurate human's override being less damaging. Once the encoding is graded (§4.2), this apparent skill-invariance of location disappears, resolving the tension: skill affects both severity (always) and location (once belief-confidence is allowed to vary).

## 5. Discussion and Limitations

This is a controlled simulation, not a study of a deployed LLM — the human model, trust-update rule, and sycophancy mechanism are simplified for interpretability and speed. Our continuous-belief ablation (§4.2) directly answers whether the headline discontinuity is an artifact of binary encoding — it is not, but its location is not universal either, which we regard as a more defensible claim than a single fixed constant. We have not yet tried an alternative blending form (e.g., sigmoidal or multiplicative rather than linear); given how cheap this ablation was (<1s), that is a natural next step rather than a mechanism sweep unlikely to change the qualitative story. We use a single dataset/classifier (breast-cancer, LR); results on other base accuracies or feature spaces are untested. The trust-update rule (exponential reinforcement) is one of many plausible models; a Bayesian trust model with explicit uncertainty is an ablation we have not run. The mapping from λ and the trust learning rate to any empirically measured real-system sycophancy intensity remains speculative — none of the audits we cite report a directly comparable "shift-per-disagreement" statistic to calibrate against. Despite these simplifications, the structural claim — that sycophancy-induced collapse can be a *threshold phenomenon whose location tracks user confidence*, not a smooth degradation or an arbitrary constant — is a property of any mechanism that blends a continuous signal with a bounded-away-from-neutral override, and is plausible wherever real systems exhibit "enough pushback and I'll just agree" behavior, which several of our cited audits (SycEval, "Does Sycophancy Change Decisions?") suggest occurs in practice.

## 6. Conclusion

We built a fast, fully reproducible simulation grounding an AI advisor in a real trained classifier and coupling it to a synthetic human via an explicit sycophancy-intensity parameter and a reinforcement-learning trust update. Sweeping this parameter reveals that team accuracy, overreliance, and trust-calibration error do not degrade smoothly — they collapse discontinuously at a critical threshold, with severity inversely related to human skill. Crucially, we show this discontinuity is not an artifact of representing human belief as a binary label: it survives when belief is replaced with a graded confidence signal, but its location shifts with how strongly the human asserts that belief, moving from λ≈0.55 for confident humans to λ≈0.7 for tentative ones. This yields a concrete, falsifiable hypothesis for empirical sycophancy audits on real LLMs: look for non-linear collapse points whose location tracks user assertiveness or confidence, not just average sycophancy rates. Code, raw results (CSV), and figures are included in this directory for reproduction and extension.
