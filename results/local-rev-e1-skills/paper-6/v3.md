# Trust Heuristics, Transparency, and the Limits of Complementarity in Human-AI Reliance: An Agent-Based Simulation

## Abstract

Appropriate reliance — knowing when to follow AI advice and when to override it — is a central open problem in socio-technical AI research, but controlled human-subject experiments are expensive and hard to run across wide parameter ranges. We build a lightweight, fully reproducible agent-based simulation in which populations of simulated decision-makers adapt a scalar trust state toward a shared AI advisor under one of three trust-update heuristics (Bayesian rational learner, negativity-biased "asymmetric" learner modeling algorithm aversion, non-adaptive control), crossed with AI reliability (0.65–0.95) and advice transparency. Across a 24-cell grid (30 seeds/cell, 720 runs), transparency improves steady-state team accuracy in every cell (paired t-tests, Bonferroni-corrected p≈6.7×10⁻²⁴ to 8.1×10⁻³⁴ for the asymmetric heuristic, power = 1.0 at as few as 10 seeds). The Bayesian heuristic dominates on team accuracy and produces trust closely calibrated to true AI reliability (0.944 mean trust at 0.95 reliability), consistent in magnitude with a published maritime study reporting 90.7% compliance at 90% AI reliability (our interpolated estimate: 89.3%). Team performance exceeds both human-alone and AI-alone accuracy ("complementarity") only at low-to-moderate AI reliability (0.65–0.75) with transparency; above that the team typically underperforms the AI alone. A sensitivity ablation shows the Bayesian heuristic's flip point (0.75–0.85) is insensitive to confidence-signal shape even under a near-uninformative signal, while the asymmetric heuristic's flip point depends on the *absolute* magnitudes of both learning rates, not just their ratio — a new follow-up sweep (Section 4.7) rules out the simpler ratio-only hypothesis directly. A secondary ablation on human population heterogeneity, re-examined here with formal paired tests per cell, shows a small but statistically real effect at the lowest reliability tested (0.65) that vanishes at reliability ≥0.75 — a more precise, partially-revised version of what we previously reported as a clean null. While preparing this revision we also found and fixed a reproducibility bug (Section 2.5): seed derivation relied on Python's randomized string `hash()`, unstable across processes; all results here are regenerated with a deterministic replacement and independently verified byte-identical across reruns.

## 1. Introduction

Deploying AI advisors alongside human decision-makers raises a socio-technical question that is more behavioral than algorithmic: given AI advice of some reliability, will the human population converge to *appropriate* reliance, or will it systematically over-rely (automation bias) or under-rely (algorithm aversion)? A large empirical literature studies this with human subjects in specific domains, but individual studies are necessarily narrow in the parameter space they can sweep. We take a complementary approach: a cheap, fully reproducible agent-based simulation that sweeps AI reliability, transparency, and trust-update heuristic jointly, and stress-tests how robust the resulting regime boundaries are to arbitrary modeling choices.

### 1.1 Related work

A maritime predictive-maintenance study (*Cognition, Technology & Work*, 2026) had domain experts make 60 sequential accept/reject decisions against a fixed-90%-reliability AI advisor, reporting population-average compliance of 90.7%; we use this as a numeric anchor (Section 4.4). "From Trust to Appropriate Reliance" (arXiv:2604.23896) argues compliance should be decomposed conditional on AI correctness, the decomposition we adopt as our primary metrics. "For Those About to Rely" (Springer, 2026) finds most published studies test AI reliability at one fixed point and rarely cross transparency with more than one behavioral model — the gap our joint sweep fills, at the cost of simulated rather than real behavior. "Toward Resilient Human-AI Collaboration" (arXiv:2608.05614) and a systematic review (*MDPI Sustainability* 18(11):5313) both note team-accuracy gains over the better individual performer are inconsistent across domains; our complementarity results (Section 4.2) reproduce this inconsistency and pin down the flip point, and our sensitivity work (Sections 4.6–4.7) asks how much that boundary depends on arbitrary modeling choices. Our asymmetric heuristic is a computational proxy for algorithm aversion (Dietvorst, Simmons, & Massey, 2015), not a new behavioral claim of our own. We are not aware of prior work jointly sweeping reliability, transparency, and a rational-vs-biased heuristic contrast in one reproducible harness, with parameter-sensitivity checks on the resulting regime boundaries.

## 2. Method

**Task and population.** Each run has `N=200` independent agents making `T=300` sequential binary decisions. In round *t*, agent *i* faces ground truth `y ~ Bernoulli(0.5)`, observes a private signal correct with probability `p_h=0.65`, and receives AI advice correct with probability `p_ai` (0.65–0.95). The agent complies with probability equal to its trust state (opaque) or a confidence-adjusted probability (transparent), else follows its own signal.

**Transparency.** The AI emits a confidence score drawn from `Beta(4β,β)` when correct that round and `Beta(β,4β)` when incorrect (β=1.5 by default) — calibrated-but-noisy. Compliance probability is `sigmoid(logit(trust) + 2·(confidence − 0.5))`.

**Trust-update heuristics.** After each decision the agent observes (one-round delay) whether the AI was correct, and updates trust via: **Bayesian** (Beta(a,b) pseudo-counts, prior a=b=2); **Asymmetric** (`trust += η_up(1−trust)` after correct, `trust −= η_down·trust` after incorrect, default η_up=0.08, η_down=0.24); **Fixed** (never updates from 0.5).

**Design.** Main grid: reliability × transparency × heuristic, 30 seeds/cell (720 runs), steady-state metrics from rounds 150–300. Heterogeneity ablation: reliability × {homogeneous, Beta-distributed human ability, mean 0.65 sd 0.12} × heuristic ∈ {bayesian, asymmetric}, transparent only (480 runs). Sensitivity ablation (Section 4.6): η_down ∈ {0.16, 0.24, 0.32} and β ∈ {1.0, 1.5, 2.5} at reliabilities 0.75/0.85, 20 seeds/cell (240 runs). New follow-up ablation (Section 4.7, this revision): (i) five (η_up, η_down) pairs isolating ratio from absolute scale; (ii) β extended down to 0.4 (near-uninformative); (iii) formal paired tests, per cell, of the heterogeneity ablation. All three at 20 seeds/cell (`followup.py`, 260 runs, 5.5s).

**Reproducibility fix.** The original seed derivation combined a fixed global seed with `hash((p_ai, transparent, heuristic, s))`, but Python randomizes string hashing per process by default, so results were not actually reproducible across processes — confirmed directly via repeated fresh-process `hash()` calls returning different values. We replaced it with a SHA-256-based deterministic hash (`stable_hash`) and verified two independent full pipeline runs produce byte-identical output CSVs. Implemented in NumPy (`sim.py`, `sensitivity.py`, `followup.py`), seed 12345. Full grid + ablations + tests: 65.2s single core; sensitivity ablation 3.4s; follow-up ablation 5.5s.

## 3. Baselines and statistical tests

The "fixed" heuristic and human/AI-alone accuracies (same trial stream) serve as baselines. We test: (a) complementarity ≠ 0 per grid cell via one-sample t-test, Bonferroni-corrected across 24 cells; (b) the transparency effect on team accuracy within the asymmetric heuristic via paired t-test across 4 reliability levels, Bonferroni-corrected; (c) a power analysis at p_ai=0.75, seed count ∈ {10,20,40,80,160}; (d) one-sample t-tests for the sensitivity ablation; (e) paired t-tests, per cell, for the heterogeneity ablation (new, Section 4.7).

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Trust heuristic and transparency both matter

Team accuracy increases monotonically with AI reliability for all heuristics; bayesian > asymmetric > fixed holds in every opaque cell (e.g. p_ai=0.85: 0.818 vs 0.781 vs 0.750). Transparency improves team accuracy in **all 24 of 24 grid cells**. For the asymmetric heuristic the effect is significant at every reliability after Bonferroni correction (Δ=+0.057, +0.057, +0.046, +0.019 at p_ai=0.65/0.75/0.85/0.95; p≈6.7×10⁻²⁴ to 8.1×10⁻³⁴), shrinking monotonically as reliability rises. Power analysis: significant in 8/8 replicate batches at every seed count 10–160 — large t-statistics reflect low seed-to-seed variance (200 agents × 150 rounds per seed), not that the real-world effect is this crisp.

### 4.2 Complementarity is regime-dependent, not universal

Complementarity is reliably positive only at low-to-moderate reliability (0.65–0.75) with transparency: at p_ai=0.65, transparent+bayesian = +0.059 (p≈2.8×10⁻³⁹), transparent+fixed = +0.064 (p≈2.1×10⁻⁴³). At high reliability (0.85, 0.95) complementarity is negative for every heuristic except transparent+bayesian, which shrinks from +0.021 at 0.75 to slightly negative (−0.004) by 0.85. At p_ai=0.95 opaque, fixed loses ~15 points relative to AI alone. Mechanistically: once AI accuracy exceeds human accuracy by a wide margin, any human override has positive probability of replacing a correct AI decision with an incorrect one, and no policy that cannot foresee correctness can fully avoid this.

### 4.3 Trust calibration

Bayesian trust tracks true AI reliability closely at every level (mean 0.648/0.747/0.845/0.944 at reliability 0.65/0.75/0.85/0.95, within 0.006 in every case); asymmetric under-trusts substantially and increasingly as reliability drops (0.381–0.385/0.498–0.500/0.653–0.659/0.862–0.865 — ~27 points below true reliability at 0.65 vs. 8 points at 0.95), the expected signature of η_down > η_up.

### 4.4 Numeric anchor to a published human-subject study

The maritime study reports 90.7% compliance at 90% reliability. Our grid does not include p_ai=0.90; Bayesian opaque compliance is 0.844 at 0.85 and 0.942 at 0.95, interpolating to ≈0.893 — about 1.4 points from the published figure. A coarse external check, not a fit: no parameter was tuned to match it.

### 4.5 Population heterogeneity: mostly null, but not uniformly

Section 4.7 supersedes our earlier blanket "no detectable effect" claim with per-cell paired tests; see there for the corrected statement.

### 4.6 Sensitivity of the complementarity regime boundary

At the flip region (reliability 0.75/0.85, 20 seeds/cell): for **Bayesian**, sweeping β ∈ {1.0, 1.5, 2.5} leaves complementarity essentially unchanged (+0.0196 to +0.0205 at 0.75, all p<10⁻¹⁹; −0.0032 to −0.0036 at 0.85, all p<10⁻⁸) — Section 4.7 confirms this holds even far outside this range. For **asymmetric**, sweeping η_down ∈ {0.16, 0.24, 0.32} (η_up fixed at 0.08) shows the flip point is *not* fixed: complementarity at 0.75 goes +0.014 → +0.007 (default) → −0.002 — already negative for strong negativity bias — while at 0.85 it stays negative and grows more negative. The boundary is structural for a well-calibrated learner but shifts earlier the more negativity-biased the population is.

### 4.7 Follow-up: three reviewer questions, run and answered

**(1) Is η_down/η_up ratio, or absolute scale, the relevant quantity?** We ran five (η_up, η_down) pairs at reliability 0.75/0.85, 20 seeds/cell. Holding the ratio fixed at 3 but scaling both rates up or down still moves complementarity at 0.75 (+0.0090 at η_up=0.05/η_down=0.15, down to +0.0034 at η_up=0.12/η_down=0.36) — so ratio alone does not pin down the outcome. Conversely, holding η_down fixed at 0.24 (the main-text default) and varying only η_up flips the *sign* of complementarity at 0.75: +0.0145 at η_up=0.16 (ratio 1.5) vs. −0.0145 at η_up=0.04 (ratio 6). Both rates matter independently, more strongly than either the ratio or η_down alone predicts; the flip point is governed by the full (η_up, η_down) pair, not a single derived scalar. This qualifies Section 4.6's η_down-only framing.

**(2) Does Bayesian insensitivity to confidence-signal shape hold as β→0 (near-uninformative)?** We extended the sweep to β ∈ {0.4, 0.7} in addition to {1.0, 1.5, 2.5}. Complementarity stays essentially flat across the full range: 0.0186–0.0202 at reliability 0.75, −0.0033 to −0.0045 at 0.85 — no trend even at β=0.4, where confidence draws are close to uninformative. The Bayesian flip point's insensitivity to confidence shape is not a narrow-range artifact; it holds well beyond the originally tested interval.

**(3) Is the heterogeneity null uniform across cells, given the ~0.003 gap flagged at p_ai=0.65/asymmetric?** Paired t-tests (hetero − homogeneous complementarity, same seeds) on all 8 cells (4 reliabilities × 2 heuristics) confirm the effect is *not* uniformly null: at reliability 0.65, both asymmetric (Δ=−0.0031, p=6.4×10⁻⁴, Bonferroni p=0.0052) and bayesian (Δ=−0.0032, p=4.6×10⁻³, Bonferroni p=0.037) show small but statistically real reductions in complementarity under heterogeneity. At reliability ≥0.75, all differences are non-significant (|Δ|≤0.0003, Bonferroni p=1.0 throughout). The corrected claim: heterogeneity has **no detectable effect at moderate-to-high AI reliability (≥0.75)**, but a small, statistically significant, practically minor effect (~5% of the complementarity magnitude) emerges at the lowest reliability tested (0.65), plausibly because low-reliability trust dynamics are more sensitive to which agents happen to draw low ability under our design's independent-decision structure.

## 5. Limitations

This is a simulation, not a human-subject study; the heuristics are stylized proxies, and transparency is one calibrated-noisy-confidence design. Section 4.7's follow-up sweeps are still coarse (20 seeds/cell, two reliabilities); a fuller joint (η_up, η_down, β) sweep across all four reliabilities remains future work, though we now have direct evidence (4.7.1) that no single derived scalar (the ratio) collapses the two-parameter asymmetric flip point, so any such sweep needs to vary both rates. Large t-statistics reflect low simulation noise, not necessarily large real-world effect sizes — direction and regime-dependence, not exact magnitude, is the transferable claim. We anchor one number (Section 4.4) to a published study; broader calibration is future work. The reproducibility bug (Section 2.5) is a reminder that "deterministic, seeded" claims in simulation code need explicit verification — rerun in a fresh process, diff outputs — not just a fixed RNG seed.

## 6. Conclusion

A cheap, fully reproducible agent-based simulation reproduces several qualitative patterns from the human-AI collaboration literature — transparency improving reliance outcomes, complementarity being conditional rather than automatic, negativity-biased updating producing systematic under-trust — landing within about a point and a half of a published human-subject compliance number without being tuned to it. The main actionable finding: **complementarity should not be assumed**, and where it disappears depends on how negativity-biased the population is and on the *absolute* magnitude of both its learning rates, not a single ratio; for a well-calibrated (Bayesian) population the flip point is stable across confidence-signal shape even under near-uninformative signals, while for a more algorithm-averse population it can vanish at moderate reliability depending on the specific (η_up, η_down) pair. Population heterogeneity is mostly but not entirely inert — it leaves a small, real fingerprint at the lowest reliability tested. Future work: a fuller joint parameter sweep over both learning rates, socially-aggregated decisions, richer transparency manipulations, and calibration against more human-subject datasets.
