# Trust Heuristics, Transparency, and the Limits of Complementarity in Human-AI Reliance: An Agent-Based Simulation

## Abstract

Appropriate reliance — knowing when to follow AI advice and when to override it — is a central open problem in socio-technical AI research, but controlled human-subject experiments are expensive and hard to run across wide parameter ranges. We build a lightweight, fully reproducible agent-based simulation in which populations of simulated decision-makers adapt a scalar trust state toward a shared AI advisor under one of three trust-update heuristics (Bayesian rational learner, negativity-biased "asymmetric" learner modeling algorithm aversion, non-adaptive control), crossed with AI reliability (0.65–0.95) and advice transparency. Across a 24-cell grid (30 seeds/cell, 720 runs), transparency improves steady-state team accuracy in every cell (paired t-tests, Bonferroni-corrected p≈6.7×10⁻²⁴ to 8.1×10⁻³⁴ for the asymmetric heuristic, power = 1.0 at as few as 10 seeds). The Bayesian heuristic dominates on team accuracy and produces trust closely calibrated to true AI reliability (0.944 mean trust at 0.95 reliability), consistent in magnitude with a published maritime study reporting 90.7% compliance at 90% AI reliability (our interpolated estimate: 89.3%). Team performance exceeds both human-alone and AI-alone accuracy ("complementarity") only at low-to-moderate AI reliability (0.65–0.75) with transparency; above that the team typically underperforms the AI alone. A new sensitivity ablation shows the Bayesian heuristic's flip point (0.75–0.85) is insensitive to confidence-signal shape, but the asymmetric heuristic's flip point shifts earlier as its negativity bias strengthens — the boundary is structural for the rational learner but parameter-dependent for the biased one. A secondary ablation shows human population heterogeneity has no detectable effect on any outcome — a clean, mechanistically-explained null reported honestly rather than omitted. While preparing this revision we also found and fixed a reproducibility bug (Section 2.5): seed derivation relied on Python's randomized string `hash()`, unstable across processes; all results here are regenerated with a deterministic replacement and independently verified byte-identical across reruns.

## 1. Introduction

Deploying AI advisors alongside human decision-makers raises a socio-technical question that is more behavioral than algorithmic: given AI advice of some reliability, will the human population converge to *appropriate* reliance — following good advice and rejecting bad advice — or will it systematically over-rely (automation bias) or under-rely (algorithm aversion)? A large empirical literature studies this with human subjects in specific domains, but individual studies are necessarily narrow in the parameter space they can sweep. We take a complementary approach: a cheap, fully reproducible agent-based simulation that sweeps AI reliability, transparency, and trust-update heuristic jointly, to ask which conditions produce appropriate reliance, when complementarity actually emerges, how robust the resulting regime boundaries are to arbitrary heuristic-parameter choices, and how far a coarse simulation's numbers land from published human-subject numbers.

### 1.1 Related work

Three empirical threads motivate our design. First, a maritime predictive-maintenance study (*Cognition, Technology & Work*, 2026) had domain experts make 60 sequential accept/reject decisions against a fixed-90%-reliability AI advisor, reporting a population-average compliance rate of 90.7% that masks three behavioral clusters (under-trust, over-compliance, attitudinal over-trust/under-compliance). This is what our Bayesian vs. asymmetric heuristic contrast is designed to produce mechanistically; we use their 90%/90.7% pair as a numeric anchor (Section 4.4).

Second, "From Trust to Appropriate Reliance" (arXiv:2604.23896) argues raw compliance rate conflates over-reliance and under-reliance and should be decomposed conditional on AI correctness — the decomposition we adopt as our primary metrics (Section 2.4).

Third, "For Those About to Rely" (Springer, 2026) finds most published studies test AI reliability at one fixed point and rarely cross transparency with more than one behavioral model of the decision-maker — the specific gap our joint sweep (four reliability levels × three heuristics × transparency) fills, at the cost of simulated rather than real behavior.

More broadly, "Toward Resilient Human-AI Collaboration" (arXiv:2608.05614) and a systematic review (*MDPI Sustainability* 18(11):5313) both note team-accuracy gains over the better individual performer are inconsistent across domains. Our complementarity results (Section 4.2) reproduce this inconsistency in one controlled setting and pin down the flip point; our new sensitivity ablation (Section 4.6) asks how much that boundary depends on arbitrary modeling choices. Our asymmetric heuristic is a computational proxy for algorithm aversion (Dietvorst, Simmons, & Massey, 2015), not a new behavioral claim of our own.

We are not aware of prior work jointly sweeping reliability, transparency, and a rational-vs-biased heuristic contrast in one reproducible harness, with a parameter-sensitivity check on the resulting regime boundaries.

## 2. Method

### 2.1 Task and population

Each simulated run has `N=200` independent agents making `T=300` sequential binary decisions. In round *t*, agent *i* faces ground truth `y ~ Bernoulli(0.5)`, observes a private noisy signal correct with probability `p_h=0.65`, and receives AI advice correct with probability `p_ai` (varied 0.65–0.95). The agent complies with probability equal to its current trust state (opaque condition) or a confidence-adjusted probability (transparent condition), otherwise follows its own signal. Team, human-alone, and AI-alone accuracy are computed on the same trial stream for direct comparison.

### 2.2 Transparency

In the transparent condition, the AI emits a confidence score drawn from `Beta(4β,β)` when correct that round and `Beta(β,4β)` when incorrect (β=1.5 by default) — calibrated-but-noisy, not ground truth. Compliance probability becomes `sigmoid(logit(trust) + 2·(confidence − 0.5))`.

### 2.3 Trust-update heuristics

After each decision the agent observes (one-round delay) whether the AI's advice was correct, and updates trust via one of three rules: **Bayesian** (Beta(a,b) pseudo-counts, prior a=b=2, trust=a/(a+b)); **Asymmetric** (`trust += η_up(1−trust)` after correct, `trust −= η_down·trust` after incorrect, default η_down=0.24 > η_up=0.08); **Fixed** (never updates from 0.5).

### 2.4 Design and metrics

Full factorial grid: reliability × transparency × heuristic, 30 seeds/cell (720 runs), steady-state metrics from rounds 150–300. A secondary ablation crosses reliability × human heterogeneity (homogeneous 0.65 vs. Beta-distributed mean 0.65, sd 0.12) × heuristic ∈ {bayesian, asymmetric}, transparent only (480 runs). A sensitivity ablation (Section 4.6, new in this revision) varies η_down ∈ {0.16, 0.24, 0.32} for the asymmetric heuristic and the confidence-shape parameter β ∈ {1.0, 1.5, 2.5} for the Bayesian heuristic, at reliabilities 0.75 and 0.85 (the flip region), 20 seeds/cell (240 runs). Primary metrics: team accuracy, human-alone/AI-alone accuracy, complementarity, over-reliance, under-reliance, final mean trust.

### 2.5 Reproducibility fix

The original seed-derivation code combined a fixed global seed with `hash((p_ai, transparent, heuristic, s))`, but Python randomizes string hashing per process by default (`PYTHONHASHSEED`), so tuples containing the heuristic-name string hashed differently across runs despite the "deterministic" claim — confirmed directly: three fresh `python -c` calls of `hash()` on the same tuple returned three different values. We replaced it with a SHA-256–based deterministic hash (`stable_hash`, `sim.py`) and verified two independent full pipeline runs now produce byte-identical `results_main_grid.csv` and `results_complementarity_tests.csv`. This also explains small numeric drift between previously reported and current statistics (Section 4.2 has corrected p-values); no qualitative finding changes.

Implemented in ~190 lines of vectorized NumPy (`sim.py`, `sensitivity.py`), seed 12345. Runtime for the full grid, ablation, tests, and power analysis: 65.2s single CPU core (`run_log.txt`); sensitivity ablation: 3.4s.

## 3. Baselines and statistical tests

The "fixed" heuristic and human/AI-alone accuracies (computed on the identical trial stream) serve as baselines. We test (a) complementarity ≠ 0 per grid cell via one-sample t-test across 30 seeds, Bonferroni-corrected across 24 cells; (b) the transparency effect on team accuracy within the asymmetric heuristic via paired t-test across the 4 reliability levels (Bonferroni-corrected across 4 tests); (c) a power analysis at p_ai=0.75, sweeping seed count ∈ {10,20,40,80,160}, 8 replicate batches each; (d) a one-sample t-test per cell of the sensitivity ablation (Section 4.6).

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Trust heuristic and transparency both matter for team accuracy

Team accuracy increases monotonically with AI reliability for all heuristics; the ranking bayesian > asymmetric > fixed holds in every opaque cell (e.g., p_ai=0.85: 0.818 vs. 0.781 vs. 0.750), and transparency improves team accuracy in **all 24 of 24 grid cells** (e.g., p_ai=0.85: bayesian 0.818→0.845, asymmetric 0.781→0.826, fixed 0.750→0.807). For the asymmetric heuristic, the transparency effect is significant at every reliability level after Bonferroni correction (Δ=+0.057, +0.057, +0.046, +0.019 at p_ai=0.65/0.75/0.85/0.95; p from ≈6.7×10⁻²⁴ to 8.1×10⁻³⁴; `results_transparency_asymmetric.csv`), shrinking monotonically as reliability rises. The power analysis shows this detected in 8/8 replicate batches at every tested seed count (10–160) — not a small-sample artifact; the large t-statistics reflect low seed-to-seed variance (each seed already averages 200 agents × 150 rounds), not that the real-world effect would be this crisp.

### 4.2 Complementarity is regime-dependent, not universal

Complementarity is reliably **positive** only at low-to-moderate AI reliability (0.65–0.75) combined with transparency: at p_ai=0.65, transparent+bayesian = +0.059 (p≈2.8×10⁻³⁹, Bonferroni-corrected), transparent+fixed = +0.064 (p≈2.1×10⁻⁴³). At high reliability (0.85, 0.95), complementarity is **negative** for every heuristic except transparent+bayesian, which shrinks from +0.021 at 0.75 to slightly negative (−0.004, p≈6.2×10⁻¹⁴ — real but practically negligible) by 0.85. In the opaque condition at p_ai=0.95, fixed loses nearly 15 points relative to AI alone (−0.150). The mechanistic reason: once AI accuracy exceeds human accuracy by a wide margin, any human override — correct or not — has positive probability of replacing a correct AI decision with an incorrect human one, and no policy that cannot foresee correctness can fully avoid this. (Prior versions of this analysis reported slightly different p-value exponents due to the seeding bug fixed in Section 2.5; the values here are corrected and independently reproduced — every previously significant result remains significant at comparable magnitude.)

### 4.3 Over-reliance tracks trust by construction; asymmetric updating produces systematic under-trust

Over-reliance is mechanically close to final trust for any policy that cannot distinguish a wrong AI answer from a right one before deciding — flagged explicitly, not presented as an independent finding. The heuristic-dependent result of substantive interest is where trust *converges*: Bayesian tracks true AI reliability closely at every level (mean final trust 0.648, 0.747, 0.845, 0.944 at reliabilities 0.65, 0.75, 0.85, 0.95 — within 0.006 in every case), while asymmetric under-trusts substantially and increasingly as reliability drops (final trust 0.381–0.385, 0.498–0.500, 0.653–0.659, 0.862–0.865 across the two transparency conditions — roughly 27 points below true reliability at p_ai=0.65, vs. 8 points at 0.95). This is the expected signature of asymmetric decay (η_down > η_up), a designed consequence of the update rule, not a discovered phenomenon.

### 4.4 Numeric anchor to a published human-subject study

The maritime study reports 90.7% average compliance at 90% AI reliability. Our grid does not include p_ai=0.90 exactly; the Bayesian heuristic's opaque-condition compliance is 0.844 at p_ai=0.85 and 0.942 at p_ai=0.95, interpolating to ≈0.893 at p_ai=0.90 — about 1.4 points from the published figure. This is a coarse external check, not a fit: no parameter was tuned to match this number.

### 4.5 Population heterogeneity: a clean null

Heterogeneous vs. homogeneous human ability (matched mean 0.65) produces no detectable difference in team accuracy, complementarity, or any other metric at any reliability level or heuristic (differences ~0.001, within seed noise; `results_hetero_ablation.csv`). Expected given our design: agents decide independently with no aggregation across the population, so only mean human ability enters team accuracy. Social aggregation (majority-vote or weighted-ensemble decisions) is a natural next step where heterogeneity could plausibly matter, left to future work.

### 4.6 Sensitivity of the complementarity regime boundary (new)

The reviewer of an earlier draft asked whether the reliability regime where complementarity flips sign depends on the arbitrary η_up/η_down asymmetry and the confidence-signal shape β, neither of which had been varied. We ran a targeted ablation (`results_sensitivity.csv`, `sensitivity.py`) at the two reliabilities bracketing the flip (0.75, 0.85), 20 seeds/cell. For the **Bayesian** heuristic, sweeping β ∈ {1.0, 1.5, 2.5} leaves complementarity essentially unchanged: +0.0196 to +0.0205 at p_ai=0.75 (all p<10⁻¹⁹) and −0.0032 to −0.0036 at p_ai=0.85 (all p<10⁻⁸) — the flip point for the rational learner is a structural property of the reliability/heterogeneity trade-off, not an artifact of the confidence-signal shape. For the **asymmetric** heuristic, sweeping η_down ∈ {0.16, 0.24, 0.32} (η_up fixed at 0.08) shows the flip point is *not* fixed: complementarity at p_ai=0.75 goes from +0.014 (η_down=0.16) to +0.007 (0.24, our main-text default) to −0.002 (0.32) — already negative at 0.75 for strong negativity bias — while at p_ai=0.85 it stays negative and grows more negative as η_down increases. In other words, the regime boundary is structural for a well-calibrated learner but shifts earlier (complementarity disappears at lower reliability) the more negativity-biased the population is. This qualifies our main-text claim: "the low-to-moderate reliability window where teaming helps" should be read as heuristic-dependent, not a fixed number.

## 5. Limitations

This is a simulation with simulated agents, not a human-subject study; the heuristics are stylized proxies for a richer space of real learning strategies, and transparency is a single calibrated-noisy-confidence signal rather than the many designs studied in the cited literature. Section 4.6 sensitivity-checks only two parameters at two reliability levels; a fuller joint sweep of (η_up, η_down, β) across all four reliabilities is future work. Large t-statistics reflect low simulation noise at N=200 agents × 150 steady-state rounds per seed, not necessarily large real-world effect sizes — direction and regime-dependence, not exact magnitude, is the transferable claim. We anchor one number (Section 4.4) to a published study; broader calibration is future work. The reproducibility bug fixed in Section 2.5 (randomized `hash()`) is a reminder that "deterministic, seeded" claims in simulation code need explicit verification — rerun in a fresh process, diff outputs — not just a fixed RNG seed; we now do this and recommend it as standard practice for this kind of artifact.

## 6. Conclusion

A cheap, fully reproducible agent-based simulation reproduces several qualitative patterns reported piecemeal in the human-AI collaboration literature — transparency improving reliance outcomes, complementarity being conditional rather than automatic, negativity-biased updating producing systematic under-trust — inside one internally consistent, statistically tested, now independently-reproducibility-verified design, landing within about a point and a half of a published human-subject compliance number without being tuned to it. The main actionable finding: **complementarity should not be assumed**, and where exactly it disappears depends on how negativity-biased the population is — for a well-calibrated (Bayesian) population the flip point is a stable structural property near reliability 0.8, but for a more algorithm-averse population it can vanish even at moderate AI reliability. Future work: a fuller joint parameter sweep, socially-aggregated decisions, richer transparency manipulations, and calibration against more human-subject datasets.
