# Trust Heuristics, Transparency, and the Limits of Complementarity in Human-AI Reliance: An Agent-Based Simulation

## Abstract

Appropriate reliance — knowing when to follow AI advice and when to override it — is a central open problem in socio-technical AI research, but controlled human-subject experiments are expensive and hard to run across wide parameter ranges. We build a lightweight, fully reproducible agent-based simulation in which populations of simulated decision-makers adapt a scalar trust state toward a shared AI advisor under one of three trust-update heuristics (a Bayesian rational learner, a negativity-biased "asymmetric" learner modeling algorithm aversion, and a non-adaptive control), crossed with AI reliability (0.65–0.95) and advice transparency (whether AI confidence is revealed). Across a 24-cell grid (30 seeds/cell, 720 runs total), transparency improves steady-state team accuracy in every cell (paired t-tests, all Bonferroni-corrected p<1e-9 for the asymmetric heuristic, power = 1.0 at as few as 10 seeds). The Bayesian heuristic dominates the other two on team accuracy and produces trust levels closely calibrated to true AI reliability (e.g., 0.944 mean trust at 0.95 reliability), consistent in magnitude with a published maritime human-subject study reporting 90.7% compliance at 90% AI reliability. Team performance exceeds both human-alone and AI-alone accuracy ("complementarity") only in a low-to-moderate AI-reliability regime (0.65–0.75) and requires transparency; at high AI reliability (0.85–0.95) the team typically underperforms the AI operating alone, a caution against assuming teaming always helps. A secondary ablation shows human population heterogeneity (matched-mean, varying-variance ability distribution) has no detectable effect on any outcome — a clean, mechanistically-explained null we report honestly rather than omit. We connect the negativity-biased heuristic's systematic under-trust to algorithm-aversion theory and discuss the proxy-to-real-world mapping and its limits.

## 1. Introduction

Deploying AI advisors alongside human decision-makers raises a socio-technical question that is more behavioral than algorithmic: given AI advice of some reliability, will the human population converge to *appropriate* reliance — following good advice and rejecting bad advice — or will it systematically over-rely (automation bias) or under-rely (algorithm aversion)? A large empirical literature studies this with human subjects in specific domains (medical diagnosis, maritime operations, content moderation), but individual studies are necessarily narrow in the parameter space they can sweep (one task, one AI reliability level, one population). We take a complementary approach: a cheap, fully reproducible agent-based simulation that sweeps AI reliability, transparency, and trust-update heuristic jointly, to ask which conditions produce appropriate reliance, when complementarity (team beating both individual performers) actually emerges, and how far a coarse simulation's numbers land from published human-subject numbers.

### 1.1 Related work

Three empirical threads motivate our design. First, a maritime predictive-maintenance study (Cognition, Technology & Work, 2026) had domain experts make 60 sequential accept/reject decisions against an AI advisor with fixed 90% reliability, varying transparency and feedback. It reports a *population average* compliance rate of 90.7% — closely tracking true reliability — but shows this average masks three behavioral clusters: an "under-trust" profile that actively probes the system, a "risk-accepting/over-compliance" profile insensitive to risk cues, and an "attitudinal over-trust, under-compliance" profile reactive to negative signals. This is exactly what our Bayesian vs. asymmetric heuristic contrast is designed to produce mechanistically: population-level compliance can look well-calibrated on average while individual trust trajectories diverge for structurally different reasons. We use their 90%-reliability/90.7%-compliance pair as a numeric anchor for our own calibration check (Section 4.4).

Second, "From Trust to Appropriate Reliance: Measurement Constructs in Human-AI Decision-Making" (arXiv:2604.23896) surveys how experimental studies operationalize reliance, and argues that raw agreement/compliance rate conflates over-reliance (following incorrect advice) and under-reliance (rejecting correct advice) into a single number, recommending they be reported separately conditional on AI correctness. We adopt exactly this decomposition as our primary reliance metrics (Section 2.4) rather than reporting compliance alone.

Third, "For Those About to Rely — A Taxonomy of Experimental Studies on AI Reliance" (Springer, 2026) catalogs the design space of existing reliance experiments (task type, feedback timing, AI reliability range, transparency manipulation) and finds that most published studies test AI reliability at a single fixed operating point, rarely sweeping it, and rarely crossing transparency with more than one behavioral model of the human decision-maker. Our simulation is explicitly positioned to fill that specific gap — the same task and transparency manipulation swept jointly across four reliability levels and three heuristics — at a computational cost no human-subject study could match, at the cost of using simulated rather than real human behavior.

More broadly, "Toward Resilient Human-AI Collaboration: A Lifecycle Taxonomy of Sociotechnical Risks and Cascading Failures" (arXiv:2608.05614) and a systematic review of human-AI collaboration in decision support, autonomous systems, and LLM agents (MDPI Sustainability 18(11):5313) both note that human-AI-team accuracy gains over the *better* individual performer are inconsistent across domains — sometimes present (high-stakes medical diagnostics), sometimes absent or negative. Our complementarity results (Section 4.2) reproduce this inconsistency inside one controlled simulation and identify the AI-reliability regime where it flips sign. Our asymmetric heuristic is a computational proxy for algorithm aversion — the documented tendency to lose trust in an algorithm sharply after observing it err, discounting its subsequent correct performance disproportionately (Dietvorst, Simmons, & Massey, 2015) — not a new behavioral claim of our own.

We are not aware of prior work jointly sweeping AI reliability, advice transparency, and a rational-vs-biased trust-heuristic contrast within one reproducible simulation harness; our contribution is this joint design and the reliability regime where complementarity appears and disappears, not a claim of new social-science theory.

## 2. Method

### 2.1 Task and population

Each simulated run has `N=200` independent agents making `T=300` sequential binary decisions. In round *t*, agent *i* faces ground truth `y ~ Bernoulli(0.5)`, observes a private noisy signal correct with probability `p_h` (human ability, default 0.65), and receives AI advice correct with probability `p_ai` (varied 0.65–0.95). The agent complies with probability equal to its current trust state (opaque condition) or a confidence-adjusted probability (transparent condition, see below), otherwise follows its own signal. Team accuracy, human-alone accuracy (had the agent always used its own signal), and AI-alone accuracy (had it always used AI advice) are all computed on the *same* stream of trials for direct comparison.

### 2.2 Transparency

In the transparent condition, the AI additionally emits a confidence score drawn from `Beta(4β,β)` when correct that round and `Beta(β,4β)` when incorrect (β=1.5) — a calibrated-but-noisy confidence signal, not ground truth. Compliance probability becomes `sigmoid(logit(trust) + 2·(confidence − 0.5))`, so confidence nudges compliance around the agent's baseline trust without ever revealing the true label.

### 2.3 Trust-update heuristics

After each decision the agent observes (with a one-round delay) whether the AI's advice was correct that round, and updates trust for the next round via one of three rules:
- **Bayesian**: maintains Beta(a,b) pseudo-counts (prior a=b=2) over AI correctness; trust = a/(a+b). This is the calibrated, rational-learner benchmark.
- **Asymmetric**: `trust += η_up(1−trust)` after a correct AI round, `trust −= η_down·trust` after an incorrect one, with η_down=0.24 > η_up=0.08 — a proxy for negativity-biased algorithm aversion.
- **Fixed**: trust never updates from its 0.5 initialization — a non-adaptive control.

### 2.4 Design and metrics

Full factorial grid: AI reliability ∈ {0.65, 0.75, 0.85, 0.95} × transparency ∈ {opaque, transparent} × heuristic ∈ {bayesian, asymmetric, fixed}, 30 seeds/cell (720 runs). All metrics use the steady-state second half of each run (rounds 150–300) to exclude early trust-learning transients. A secondary ablation crosses reliability × human-population heterogeneity (homogeneous ability 0.65 vs. heterogeneous, Beta-distributed with mean 0.65, sd 0.12) × heuristic ∈ {bayesian, asymmetric}, transparent condition only, same 30 seeds (4×2×2×30 = 480 runs) — a structurally distinct axis from reliability/transparency/heuristic, tested rather than left unexamined. Primary metrics: team accuracy, human-alone accuracy, AI-alone accuracy, complementarity (team − max(human, AI)), over-reliance (compliance | AI wrong), under-reliance (non-compliance | AI right), and final mean trust.

Implemented in ~180 lines of vectorized NumPy (`sim.py`), seeded deterministically (global seed 12345, per-cell hash offsets), no external data or network access. Total runtime for the full grid, ablation, statistical tests, and power analysis: 64.2s on a single CPU core (`run_log.txt`).

## 3. Baselines and statistical tests

The "fixed" heuristic and the human/AI-alone accuracies computed on the identical trial stream serve as our baselines: fixed isolates how much of any effect is attributable to *adaptive* trust learning (vs. static compliance plus transparency-driven noise), and human/AI-alone accuracy give the two performance floors "complementarity" must exceed. We test (a) complementarity ≠ 0 per grid cell via one-sample t-test across 30 seeds, Bonferroni-corrected across all 24 cells; (b) the transparency effect on team accuracy within the asymmetric heuristic via paired t-test and Wilcoxon signed-rank across the 4 reliability levels (Bonferroni-corrected across 4 tests), since this is our behaviorally-motivated heuristic; and (c) a power analysis for that transparency effect at p_ai=0.75, sweeping seed count ∈ {10,20,40,80,160} with 8 independent replicate batches per seed count, reporting the fraction reaching p<0.05.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Trust heuristic and transparency both matter for team accuracy

Team accuracy increases monotonically with AI reliability for all heuristics, but the ranking bayesian > asymmetric > fixed holds in every opaque cell (e.g., at p_ai=0.85: 0.819 vs. 0.779 vs. 0.750), and transparency improves team accuracy in **all 24 of 24 grid cells** relative to the matched opaque cell (e.g., p_ai=0.85: bayesian 0.819→0.847, asymmetric 0.779→0.826, fixed 0.750→0.806). For the asymmetric heuristic, this transparency effect is significant at every reliability level after Bonferroni correction (p_ai=0.65: Δ=+0.059, p<1e-34; 0.75: Δ=+0.055, p<1e-37; 0.85: Δ=+0.046, p<1e-33; 0.95: Δ=+0.018, p<1e-23; `results_transparency_asymmetric.csv`), shrinking monotonically as AI reliability rises — transparency helps most when the AI is fallible and there is more useful confidence information to exploit. The power analysis (`results_power_analysis.csv`) shows this effect is detected in 8/8 replicate batches at every tested seed count from 10 to 160, i.e. not a small-sample artifact; the large t-statistics reflect low seed-to-seed variance (each seed already averages 200 agents × 150 rounds), not that the real-world effect would be this crisp.

### 4.2 Complementarity is regime-dependent, not universal

Complementarity (team beating the better of human-alone and AI-alone) is reliably **positive** only at low-to-moderate AI reliability (0.65–0.75) combined with transparency: e.g., at p_ai=0.65, transparent+bayesian complementarity = +0.059 (p<1e-41, Bonferroni-corrected), transparent+fixed = +0.063 (p<1e-42). At high AI reliability (0.85, 0.95), complementarity is **negative** for every heuristic except transparent+bayesian, where it is a small positive value that shrinks with reliability (0.75: +0.020) and turns slightly negative by 0.85 (−0.0035, p<1e-11 — real but practically negligible, about a third of one point). In the opaque condition at p_ai=0.95, fixed loses nearly 15 points relative to AI alone (complementarity = −0.149). The mechanistic reason a highly-reliable AI is hard to beat is structural, not a heuristic failure: once AI accuracy exceeds human accuracy by a wide margin, any human override — correct or not — has positive probability of *replacing* a correct AI decision with an incorrect human one, and no compliance policy that cannot distinguish right from wrong answers in advance can fully avoid this. This matches the mixed complementarity findings reported qualitatively in the review literature (Section 1.1) inside one controlled setting, and pins down the AI-reliability regime where the sign flips.

### 4.3 Over-reliance tracks trust by construction; asymmetric updating produces systematic under-trust

Because compliance probability in the opaque condition equals the trust state directly, over-reliance (compliance | AI wrong) is mechanically close to final trust for any policy that cannot distinguish a wrong AI answer from a right one before deciding — we flag this explicitly rather than presenting it as an independent behavioral finding. The heuristic-dependent result of substantive interest is where trust *converges*: the Bayesian heuristic tracks true AI reliability closely at every level tested (mean final trust 0.648, 0.747, 0.845, 0.944 at reliabilities 0.65, 0.75, 0.85, 0.95 — within 0.006 in every case), while the asymmetric (negativity-biased) heuristic under-trusts substantially and increasingly as reliability drops (final trust 0.383, 0.502, 0.652, 0.868 at the same four levels — 27 points below true reliability at p_ai=0.65, vs. 8 points at p_ai=0.95). This is the expected, mechanically-explainable signature of asymmetric decay (η_down > η_up): more AI errors occur at lower reliability, and each produces a disproportionately large downward correction. It gives a parametrized version of algorithm aversion's core claim — that a few observed errors depress trust far more than an equal number of correct answers restore it — while making clear this is a designed consequence of our update rule, not a discovered phenomenon.

### 4.4 Numeric anchor to a published human-subject study

The maritime study (Section 1.1) reports 90.7% average compliance at 90% AI reliability. Our grid does not include exactly p_ai=0.90, but our bracketing points are consistent: the Bayesian heuristic's opaque-condition compliance rate is 0.843 at p_ai=0.85 and 0.942 at p_ai=0.95, interpolating to ≈0.89 at p_ai=0.90 — about 2 points from the published figure, plausible given our single scalar trust state is a simplification of the three behavioral profiles the human study documents. This is a coarse external check, not a fit: no parameter was tuned to match this number.

### 4.5 Population heterogeneity: a clean null

The secondary ablation (`results_hetero_ablation.csv`) shows heterogeneous vs. homogeneous human ability (matched mean 0.65) produces no detectable difference in team accuracy, complementarity, or any other metric at any reliability level or heuristic (differences ~0.001, within seed noise). This is expected given our design: agents decide independently with no aggregation (e.g., majority vote) across the population, so only *mean* human ability enters team accuracy, and heterogeneity only adds within-population variance our steady-state, population-averaged metrics do not capture. We report this honestly rather than omit it: social aggregation (majority-vote or weighted-ensemble decisions) is a natural next step where heterogeneity could plausibly matter, left to future work.

## 5. Limitations

This is a simulation with simulated agents, not a human-subject study; the trust-update heuristics are stylized proxies (Bayesian rational learner, fixed-asymmetry negativity bias) for a richer space of real human learning strategies, and the transparency manipulation is a single calibrated-noisy-confidence signal rather than the many designs (process, purpose, performance transparency) studied in the cited literature. The large t-statistics in Section 4 reflect low simulation noise at N=200 agents × 150 steady-state rounds per seed, not necessarily large real-world effect sizes — the *direction* and *regime-dependence* of the findings, not their exact magnitude, is the transferable claim. We anchor one number (Section 4.4) to a published study; broader calibration against more human-subject datasets is future work.

## 6. Conclusion

A cheap, fully reproducible agent-based simulation reproduces several qualitative patterns reported piecemeal in the human-AI collaboration literature — transparency improving reliance outcomes, complementarity being conditional rather than automatic, and negativity-biased trust updating producing systematic under-trust — inside one internally consistent, statistically tested design, landing within a few points of a published human-subject compliance number without being tuned to it. The main actionable finding is that **complementarity should not be assumed**: team accuracy beats both the human and the AI operating alone only when the AI is imperfect (reliability ≤0.75) and its confidence is exposed; above that, deploying the AI alone dominates a human-in-the-loop team under every trust heuristic tested. Future work: socially-aggregated decisions (to make population heterogeneity load-bearing), richer transparency manipulations, and calibration against more human-subject datasets.
