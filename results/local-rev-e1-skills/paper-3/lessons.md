# Skills / lessons for writing better first drafts

- Before framing a result as a "finding," check whether it follows directly from the algebra/construction of your own method, or could be explained by an obvious confounding variable in your own setup (e.g., a discretization/functional-form choice, or a covariate like set size that differs between the two things you're comparing). If so, either (a) don't present it as a standalone empirical discovery, or (b) proactively run the cheap confound-controlled ablation in the first draft, not only after a reviewer asks. A recurring, expensive pattern: a headline "method A vs. method B" difference measured at matched nominal parameters (e.g., matched alpha) turns out to be substantially explained by the two methods landing at different set sizes/scales — always ask "matched on what?" and test the alternative matching before claiming the difference is intrinsic to the thing you want credit for.
- Every summary statistic in the abstract must be a number you can point to in the results body, not a loose paraphrase. Compute the actual range/ratio across conditions and either report the range or the correct aggregate — don't round a spread of effects (e.g., 2.5x–8.5x) into one punchy headline figure (e.g., "quadruples") that doesn't match any single reported value.
- When a headline effect is a discontinuity/threshold/phase-transition claim, test it at finer resolution than your main sweep grid before submitting — a coarse grid cannot distinguish a true jump from a fast multi-step decline, and reviewers will ask for exactly this check.
- Avoid post-hoc, unjustified cutoffs used to classify results into categories (e.g., "a drop >0.03 counts as a narrow band"). Either use a threshold-free characterization (slope, derivative, AUC of the curve) or report a sensitivity analysis across a reasonable range of thresholds from the start, and be explicit about which conclusions are threshold-sensitive.
- When you run a series of ablations across revisions, check that you're not only ever varying one axis of the setup (e.g., only the human/behavioral-model side) while leaving other obviously-relevant axes (e.g., the base data-generating classifier/dataset, the update-rule hyperparameters) completely untested through every round. Reviewers will eventually flag the untested axis specifically, even after many rounds of otherwise-thorough ablations elsewhere.
- If your central phenomenon has a known general mathematical explanation (e.g., "blending in a bounded/probability space is sharper than blending in an unbounded/log-odds space before squashing" is a known property of interpolation geometry), explicitly connect it to that existing theory/prior work. Otherwise, reviewers will read framing like "phase transition" or "sharp threshold" as overclaiming novelty even when the empirical execution is rigorous — connect mechanism to prior theory before claiming surprise value.
- More rounds of technically excellent, honestly-reported ablations do not guarantee a monotonically increasing review score — in one paper's process the rating sat flat across all four rounds even though every single reviewer request (missing artifact, thin seed counts, missing significance tests, a generalization check) was concretely fixed each round. Once the easy methodological critiques (missing significance tests, missing ablations, unverified thresholds, missing artifacts) are resolved, reviewers shift to scrutinizing the core contribution's novelty/significance, related-work depth, and any remaining unexplained anomaly, and each round tends to surface one new item in that category. A contribution whose main arc is "we found an effect, then discovered on our own follow-up it was mostly a confound" reads as a shrinking, not growing, contribution even when the science is impeccable — near-perfect execution on the *last* reviewer's specific ask does not by itself move the score. Budget first-draft effort toward broadening framing, connecting to existing theory, and building out related work and the scope of the core claim, not only toward anticipating individual ablation requests.
- Related-work sections that give one-sentence characterizations of each cited paper (or stay in the 5-8 total citation range) get flagged as shallow, and this critique tends to recur across multiple review rounds unaddressed if not fixed early — engage substantively with at least the most central 2-3 citations (what they measured, what they didn't test, how your parameters relate to any quantity they report), and expand citation breadth to cover the wider space of related models/approaches, not just the one or two papers you calibrated against.
- Don't assert a novelty claim ("we are not aware of prior work that...") without substantiating it against the broader literature in the same section. An unsupported novelty assertion gets flagged as thin even in a late-round, otherwise well-defended paper — either cite enough related work to make the claim credible or soften it to a scoped statement about what you did test.
- If your method is a simulation/proxy standing in for a real-world system (e.g., a toy trust-update rule standing in for real LLM sycophancy, or a small synthetic vocabulary standing in for a real tokenizer), try to find at least one empirical anchor point from cited literature — or a real-world spot-check, even partial — to calibrate against, even approximately. Leaving the mapping from simulation parameter/regime to the real-world target entirely untested through every revision is a recurring, unresolved weakness reviewers keep raising. When you do attempt such a check late and it's blocked by an environment limitation (e.g., a sandbox with no outbound network access to fetch a real tokenizer/dataset), report that honestly as an open, untested question rather than fabricating or skipping it — but where possible, prefer a generalization check that doesn't depend on network access in the first place (an offline/hand-rolled proxy, a locally-available dataset) so the check can actually be run instead of only attempted.
- Ship deterministic, self-contained, fast-running code alongside every numeric claim in the paper, and double-check before each submission that every artifact/log file path named in the text (e.g., "raw output is in X_log.txt") actually exists in the submitted directory — a claimed-but-missing log is a cheap, easily-avoided reproducibility flag that reviewers who rerun your code will catch immediately. Full exact reproducibility (a reviewer can rerun your script and match your numbers to 3+ decimal places) was consistently rewarded across every review round in this paper's process — it's cheap to provide and materially increases reviewer trust.
- Recompute every ratio/ "roughly Nx" comparison stated in prose directly from the numbers you report elsewhere in the same paper before submitting, especially after a late revision changes an upstream number (e.g., a calibration point or effect estimate). Reviewers who are already verifying your code will also hand-check your arithmetic, and a stale or miscalculated ratio (claiming "~10x" when the paper's own reported numbers give ~4x) is a concrete, easily-caught overclaim that undermines an otherwise rigor-focused paper. Also reconcile the same ratio if it's quoted more than once (e.g., abstract vs. main text) against slightly different reference numbers — pick one comparison basis and use it consistently.
- If a sweep or ablation turns up one anomalous/outlier cell relative to its neighbors (e.g., one grid point breaking an otherwise monotonic or stable trend), don't just flag it as unexplained ("plausibly a resonance") and move on — rerun that specific cell with substantially more seeds/trials before treating it as a real structural finding. If the cell used a small sample (e.g., 5 seeds) while a similar anomaly elsewhere in the paper was cleared up with a high-power rerun (e.g., 30 seeds), reviewers will note the inconsistency and ask why the same fix wasn't applied here.
- When you claim a small-sample effect (e.g., n=5 seeds) is "seed-stable" or "not noise," back it with a formal paired statistical test (paired t-test, Wilcoxon signed-rank) in the same draft you make the claim — don't rely on informal evidence (sign consistency across seeds, non-overlapping min/max ranges) and wait for a reviewer to ask for the formal version; that costs a full extra round for a test that usually just re-analyzes data you already have. When a test has a known small-sample floor (e.g., the minimum achievable two-sided Wilcoxon p-value at n=5 is 0.0625, unreachable regardless of effect size), report that floor honestly alongside a more appropriate test rather than omitting the unfavorable result or overselling significance the sample size can't support.
