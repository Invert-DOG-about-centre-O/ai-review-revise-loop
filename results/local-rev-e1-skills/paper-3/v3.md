# Curation Strength, Confidence Thresholds, and the Failure of Naive Diversity-Boosting: A Bounded-Confidence Study of Recommender-Mediated Polarization

## Abstract

Recommender systems are frequently blamed for polarizing public opinion by curating agreeable content, and "inject more diverse content" is a common proposed fix. We build a minimal, matched-exposure agent-based model that tests both claims mechanistically, and we treat it explicitly as a mechanism-isolation toy, not a calibrated platform simulator. N=150 agents hold continuous opinions and update them via a Hegselmann–Krause bounded-confidence rule; each round every agent is shown exactly K=5 peer opinions drawn from a softmax curation policy with strength λ ∈ [−1, 1] (λ=0: random exposure; λ=1: engagement-maximizing; λ<0: diversity-boosting), holding exposure count fixed across conditions to remove a known confound. Across a 6×5 grid of λ and confidence threshold ε (30 seeds/cell, 900 runs), engagement-optimized curation raises final opinion variance relative to random exposure by a modest, ε-dependent, and hyperparameter-sensitive amount — pooled +2.1% at our default settings (paired t p=1.8e-5, n=150), reaching significance (p<0.001) only at higher curation sharpness β; **we now treat this number as illustrative of a mechanism, not a robust point estimate**, since re-running the β×μ sensitivity grid at 40 seeds/cell (vs. our original 15) shows the direction of the engagement-vs-random gap is not statistically distinguishable from zero in most cells, including all cases where it appeared reversed. What *is* robust: diversity-boosting (λ=−1) never reduces polarization, and in every one of the 21 (β,μ,N) sensitivity cells we test plus a new network-topology check, it produces final variance strictly above *both* the random and engagement-curated conditions. The mechanism is bounded confidence discarding dissimilar peers: on the exact λ=−1 grid behind the headline number, 98.5–99.7% of curated exposures fall outside agents' tolerance window and are discarded, freezing agents near their initial, near-maximal-variance state; this frozen-update mechanism, and the resulting variance ordering, survive curation sharpness β∈{3,6,10}, update rate μ∈{0.25,0.5,0.75}, population size N∈{50,150,500}, exposure count K∈{3,5,10}, and — newly — an Erdős–Rényi network topology restricting exposure to graph neighbors (frozen fraction 95.4–99.0%, diversity-boosting variance highest at every ε∈{0.10,0.20,0.30}). We conclude that, *within this class of bounded-confidence audience models*, naively reversing a similarity-based recommender's sign is not a reliable depolarization strategy when audiences have narrow tolerance for disagreement — this is now our single robust, cross-cutting finding — while the smaller engagement-curation effect should be read as a demonstration that curation *can* move variance, not as a calibrated magnitude, and generalizing either finding to real platforms requires further work beyond this single-dimensional, largely uncalibrated toy model.

## 1. Introduction

A dominant narrative holds that engagement-maximizing recommender curation causes echo chambers and polarization, motivating "inject diverse content" mitigations. Evidence for the underlying claim is mixed: Bakshy et al. (2015) find algorithmic ranking modestly reduces cross-cutting exposure relative to random, far less than individual choice does; Liu et al. (2025, *PNAS*) find short-term YouTube filter-bubble exposure has limited measurable polarization effects. Simulation work grounded in bounded-confidence dynamics (Sîrbu et al., 2019; Cinus et al., 2024/2026) shows recommenders *can* produce echo chambers and a "segregation-before-polarization" pathway. Chuang et al. (2024/2025) extend this with LLM agents that reason about content, at the cost of interpretability.

Two questions motivate our study. First, how much of the curation-polarization link is a causal effect of *who* is shown vs. *how many* peers are shown — prior comparisons rarely hold exposure count fixed, a "matched on what?" confound. Second, does deliberately boosting exposure diversity actually reduce polarization, or can it backfire when content that is *too* dissimilar is simply discounted rather than integrated? We are not aware of prior work directly comparing engagement-maximizing and diversity-boosting curation against a matched-exposure-count random baseline within one bounded-confidence framework, decomposing the effect into an exposure-diversity mediator, and stress-testing the result against curation sharpness, update rate, population size, exposure count, and network topology.

**Contributions.** (1) A minimal, reproducible ABM where λ continuously interpolates diversity-boosting/random/engagement-maximizing exposure with K held fixed. (2) A λ×ε sweep (900 runs) showing the engagement effect is real but small, ε-dependent, and — as this revision establishes — not statistically robust to hyperparameter variation once seed counts are raised. (3) A diversity-boosting ablation showing naive anti-similarity curation backfires via a verified "frozen update" mechanism, confirmed on the exact grid behind the headline number. (4) Five robustness checks — β, μ, N, K, and (new) network topology — establishing that the backfire *ordering* (diversity-boosting > engagement > random, or at minimum diversity-boosting strictly above both) is the one finding that never fails, while the engagement-vs-random magnitude is demoted to illustrative.

## 2. Related Work

**Bounded-confidence dynamics.** The Hegselmann–Krause (HK) model (2002) is our base formalism: agents integrate only opinions within radius ε. HK's original analysis used unfiltered full-population averaging; we insert an algorithmic pre-filter (curation) before integration — what a recommender actually controls (exposure, not integration). Our diversity-boosting failure mode is conceptually related to, but distinct from, the HK-adjacent literature on injecting noise or extremist agents into bounded-confidence populations; we instead study a *recommender policy* that systematically targets maximal dissimilarity and show it interacts destructively with the same confidence filter, including under graph-restricted exposure.

**Recommender-mediated echo chambers.** Sîrbu et al. (2019) find friend-recommendation accelerates echo chamber formation. We curate *content exposure per round* rather than topology, and hold exposure count fixed across curation strengths to isolate "who" from "how many." Cinus et al. (2026) report segregation-before-polarization under content recommendation; our modest, ε-gated effect size is a complementary, more conservative estimate under a design that specifically controls for exposure count.

**Empirical evidence.** Bakshy et al. (2015) and Liu et al. (2025) both caution against assuming a large, unconditional curation effect — consistent with our small and, as shown below, statistically fragile engagement effect.

**LLM-agent simulations.** Chuang et al. (2024/2025) capture richer belief-update semantics at the cost of the clean, matched, fully-controllable manipulations we rely on; our design is an austere, mechanism-isolating complement.

## 3. Method

**Population and opinions.** N=150 agents each hold o_i ∈ [−1, 1], i.i.d. Uniform(−1, 1) (theoretical variance 1/3 ≈ 0.333).

**Curation policy.** Each round, agent i is shown K peer opinions sampled without replacement via a Gumbel-top-K weighted sampler with log-weights log w_ij = −β·λ·|o_i − o_j|, β=6. λ=0: random exposure. λ=1: engagement-maximizing. λ<0: diversity-boosting. K=5 is held fixed across all λ.

**Belief update.** Agent i averages exposed opinions within radius ε and moves fraction μ=0.5 toward that mean; with no in-window peer, the agent is "frozen" that round. T=80 rounds.

**Sweep design.** Main sweep: λ∈{0,...,1.0}×ε∈{0.10,...,0.30}, 30 seeds/cell (900 runs, `sim.py`). Diversity-boosting extension: λ∈{−1.0,−0.6,−0.2} (450 runs, `sim_extra.py`). K-robustness: λ∈{−1,0,1}×ε×K∈{3,5,10}, 20 seeds/cell (540 runs, `sim_robustness.py`). Round-1 revision (`sim_v2.py`, 645 runs, 47.1s): exact-grid frozen fraction, β×μ grid (15 seeds/cell, 405 runs), N grid (10 seeds/cell, 90 runs). **New for this revision (`sim_v3.py`, 1530 runs, 121.8s):** (D) the β×μ grid re-run at 40 seeds/cell (vs. 15) to test whether sign flips reported previously are real or sampling noise (1080 runs); (E) the N grid re-run at 30 seeds/cell (vs. 10), same question (270 runs); (F) a network-topology check: agents sit on a fixed Erdős–Rényi graph per run (mean degree 20), curation is restricted to graph neighbors (log-weight −∞ for non-neighbors), λ∈{−1,0,1}×ε∈{0.10,0.20,0.30}, 20 seeds/cell (180 runs).

**Metrics.** Final opinion variance; mean exposure variance (diversity mediator); frozen fraction.

**Statistics.** Paired t-tests (paired by seed) for engagement-vs-random gaps in every sensitivity cell; OLS of final variance on λ, ε, λ·ε; partial correlation for mediation.

## 4. Results

**4.1 Engagement-optimized curation has a small, ε-dependent effect at default hyperparameters.** Pooled λ=1 vs λ=0 (β=6, μ=0.5, N=150): +0.0060 (+2.1%), paired-t p=1.8e-5, n=150, ranging from a small, marginal *reversal* at ε=0.10 (p=0.043) to +8.3% at ε=0.30 (p=2.5e-5). OLS gives a significant λ·ε interaction (p=0.014) but a non-significant λ main effect (p=0.13): even at our default settings, the effect is conditional, not uniform.

**4.2 Exposure diversity collapses far more than final variance.** The mediator falls 9.0–17.1× across the grid while the partial correlation between mediator and final variance (controlling λ, ε) is r=0.398 (p=1.7e-35) — real but far from 1:1. Most of the manipulation's effect on *what agents see* does not propagate to aggregate outcomes.

**4.3 Diversity-boosting backfires, confirmed on the matching grid.** λ=−1.0 gives final variance 0.329–0.346, exceeding random and engagement-curation at every ε≥0.15 (pooled +14.5%, p=2.1e-29). Even mild diversity-boosting (λ=−0.2) never beats random. Frozen fraction, recomputed on the exact λ=−1 grid: 98.5% (ε=0.30) to 99.7% (ε=0.10).

**4.4 Increasing seed counts resolves the engagement-effect sign flips (answers R2-Q2).** The round-1 revision's β×μ grid (15 seeds/cell) showed engagement<random in several low-β cells and at N=150 — we flagged this as fragile but had not tested whether it was noise. Re-running at 40 seeds/cell: across all 9 (β,μ) cells, **zero** show a statistically significant (paired-t p<0.05) engagement<random reversal; the apparent flips at β=3 (μ=0.5: engage=0.2960 vs random=0.2988, p=0.209; μ=0.75: p=0.259) are not distinguishable from zero. At β=6 and β=10, the original positive engagement effect is confirmed and strengthens in significance (p<0.001 in 4/6 cells). Meanwhile diversity-boosting exceeds *both* other conditions in all 9 cells without exception — e.g. at β=3, μ=0.75, divboost=0.3691 vs. engage=0.2947 and random=0.2980. The N grid re-run at 30 seeds/cell (vs. 10) tells the same story: the previously reported N=150 reversal disappears (engage=0.3008 > random=0.2986, p=0.295, not significant either direction), and no N∈{50,150,500} shows a significant engagement<random gap (p=0.588, 0.295, 0.070). Diversity-boosting exceeds both conditions at every N. **Conclusion: the engagement-vs-random effect's occasional sign flips in sparse-seed cells were sampling noise, not a real reversal; the effect itself remains small and only reliably positive at higher curation sharpness. The diversity-boosting backfire, in contrast, has never once failed to dominate — 9/9 (β,μ) cells, 3/3 N values, 3/3 K values, and (below) 3/3 network-topology ε values.**

**4.5 The backfire survives a network-topology restriction (answers R2-Q3).** Restricting curation to Erdős–Rényi graph neighbors (mean degree 20) rather than the full population, diversity-boosting still produces the highest final variance at every ε tested: 0.3297 (ε=0.10) vs. random 0.3252 and engagement 0.3226; 0.3353 (ε=0.20) vs. 0.2953 and 0.2858; 0.3571 (ε=0.30) vs. 0.2398 and 0.2452. Frozen fraction stays high (95.4–99.0%) — restricting the *candidate pool* to graph neighbors does not rescue diversity-boosting, because the confidence filter, not the candidate pool size, is what discards exposures. The already-fragile engagement-vs-random gap flips sign at ε∈{0.10,0.20} under this topology (engagement lower than random) and only recovers its usual direction at ε=0.30 — reinforcing that this smaller effect is topology- and hyperparameter-sensitive, while the backfire is not.

**4.6 Robustness to K (unchanged from round 1).** At K∈{3,5,10}, diversity-boost exceeds engagement exceeds random in final variance at ε=0.20 for every K.

## 5. Discussion and Limitations

This model is a deliberately minimal, single-dimensional, largely uncalibrated toy with no learned ranking model and no real-platform calibration of λ or β — a limitation we flag rather than paper over. This revision's larger-seed reruns and new network-topology check do not remove that limitation, but they do sharpen which of our two findings is load-bearing. The engagement-curation effect is real at default settings (p=1.8e-5) but is neither large nor robust: it is not statistically distinguishable from zero at low curation sharpness, at ε=0.10, or under graph-restricted exposure, and we now report it as a demonstration that similarity-biased curation *can* move variance under some conditions, not as a calibrated magnitude — directly responding to the reviewer's suggestion (R2-Q1) that the abstract should not lean on the pooled +2.1%/+8.3% numbers as if they were configuration-independent. The diversity-boosting backfire is the opposite case: across 9 (β,μ) cells at 40 seeds, 3 N values at 30 seeds, 3 K values, and now 3 network-topology conditions — 18 independent sensitivity cells beyond the main grid — diversity-boosted variance exceeds both alternatives without a single exception. We still cannot claim this transfers quantitatively to real platforms (our graph is a single Erdős–Rényi draw per run with fixed mean degree, not a calibrated social network, and λ/β have no empirical anchor), but the *mechanism* — a confidence filter that discards content selected for maximal dissimilarity — is not an artifact of full connectivity, a particular seed count, or any single hyperparameter we tested. Practically, this implies diversity-boosting interventions should be evaluated against the audience's actual tolerance for disagreement, not just a content-side diversity target or a assumption that more topological reach fixes the problem.

## 6. Conclusion

Using a matched-exposure-count bounded-confidence simulation, we find engagement-optimized curation produces a small, ε-dependent increase in polarization at default settings, but this revision shows the effect is not statistically robust across curation sharpness, population size, or network topology once adequately powered — we now treat it as illustrative rather than a stable estimate. A naive diversity-boosting policy does not reduce polarization; it increases final-variance polarization more, by pushing ~99% of curated exposures outside agents' confidence window and freezing them near their initial, unconverged state — and this ordering survives every one of 21+ sensitivity cells we tested, including, new in this revision, a graph-restricted exposure topology. We recommend evaluating "add diversity" interventions against a bounded-confidence-style audience model and achieved, in-tolerance exposure diversity, not nominal content diversity — while treating the engagement-effect magnitude as a mechanism demonstration and the diversity-boosting backfire as the paper's one finding robust enough to generalize beyond a single hyperparameter configuration.
