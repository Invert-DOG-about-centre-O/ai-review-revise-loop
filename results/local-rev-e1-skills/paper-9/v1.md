# Task Heterogeneity Governs Which Probabilistic Uncertainty Signal Wins: A Controlled Simulation of Self-Consistency, Semantic Entropy, and Confidence Calibration

## Abstract

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly paired with sampling-based probabilistic methods — self-consistency (majority vote over sampled generations), semantic-entropy-based selective prediction, and confidence calibration (verbalized vs. frequency-based) — to quantify and act on uncertainty. These are usually evaluated one at a time, on one benchmark, so it is unclear how their relative value depends on a basic property of the task pool: the *heterogeneity* of per-question difficulty. We build a fully synthetic, seeded, reproducible simulation in which each of $N=500$ questions has a latent per-sample correctness probability $p_i$ drawn from one of four heterogeneity regimes (near-homogeneous to bimodal/extreme), with $k$-sample generations, semantically clustered wrong answers, a verbalized (overconfident) confidence signal, and a frequency-based (sample-agreement) confidence signal. Self-consistency accuracy gains over single-sample decoding shrink monotonically as heterogeneity increases (43.9pp at low heterogeneity vs. 13.0pp at extreme heterogeneity, both $p<10^{-20}$, 30 replicates of $N=500$), roughly bracketing the 11–17.9 percentage-point gains reported for real LLMs on GSM8K/SVAMP/AQuA (Wang et al., 2022). More novel: which confidence signal is better calibrated *flips sign* with heterogeneity — verbalized confidence has lower ECE than frequency confidence under low/moderate heterogeneity (e.g., 0.15 vs. 0.30, $p\approx10^{-38}$), but frequency confidence wins under extreme heterogeneity (0.08 vs. 0.14, $p\approx10^{-18}$). This flip is robust across three overconfidence-transform parameterizations. Semantic-entropy AUROC for flagging wrong self-consistency answers is comparatively stable (0.82–0.87) across regimes. A power analysis shows our headline effects are detectable at just 3 replicates given large effect sizes. We conclude that "self-consistency helps" and "frequency confidence is well-calibrated" are not heterogeneity-invariant facts, and practitioners should characterize their task-difficulty distribution before choosing an uncertainty-quantification method.

## 1. Introduction

Modern LLM deployments increasingly rely on sampling multiple generations and aggregating them probabilistically rather than trusting a single greedy decode. Three families of methods dominate this space: (1) **self-consistency**, which samples $k$ reasoning chains and takes a majority vote over final answers (Wang et al., 2022); (2) **semantic entropy**, which clusters sampled generations by meaning-equivalence (via NLI) and uses the entropy of the resulting cluster distribution as an uncertainty/hallucination signal (Kuhn et al., 2023; Farquhar et al., 2024); and (3) **confidence calibration**, which asks whether a model's stated or derived confidence tracks its actual accuracy, comparing model-verbalized confidence against frequency-based signals such as sample agreement (Xiong et al., 2023-style verbalized-confidence studies; recent work on self-generated-distractor calibration, arXiv:2509.25532).

Each of these methods has been validated empirically, but almost always in isolation and on a fixed benchmark: self-consistency gains are reported per-dataset (+17.9pp GSM8K, +11.0pp SVAMP, +12.2pp AQuA; Wang et al., 2022), semantic entropy AUROC is reported per-dataset (~0.60 on some newer cross-model benchmarks, e.g. arXiv:2606.07528, higher in the original line of work), and calibration studies typically report a single overconfidence finding without decomposing *why* miscalibration magnitude differs across settings. What is largely missing is a controlled study of how one property of the task distribution — heterogeneity of per-question difficulty — governs the relative value of these methods. This matters practically: self-consistency on a homogeneous-difficulty internal QA set has a different expected payoff than on a benchmark mixing trivial and adversarial items, and the same holds for choosing between verbalized confidence and sample-agreement rate as a routing/abstention signal.

**Contribution.** We do not claim self-consistency "works" or that semantic entropy "detects hallucinations" — both are well-established, expected effects (a majority vote over independent, better-than-chance estimators improving accuracy is a direct consequence of Condorcet-jury-type reasoning, not a new discovery). Our contribution is narrower and, we believe, useful: a controlled simulation that (a) quantifies *how much* the self-consistency gain and calibration ranking depend on task-difficulty heterogeneity, holding everything else fixed, (b) shows a heterogeneity-driven **sign flip** in which confidence signal is better calibrated, a result we verify is not an artifact of one arbitrary parameter choice, and (c) anchors our simulated self-consistency gains against real published numbers to sanity-check that our synthetic regime brackets realistic operating points.

### Related Work

**Self-consistency** (Wang et al., 2022) established that sampling $k$ chain-of-thought traces and majority-voting the final answer substantially outperforms greedy or single-sample decoding, with gains concentrated on tasks where errors are diverse rather than systematic — reported gains of +17.9pp (GSM8K), +12.2pp (AQuA), and +11.0pp (SVAMP) over single-sample baselines. Follow-up work (e.g., weighted/early-stopping variants, Mirror-Consistency) refines *how* votes are aggregated but, to our knowledge, does not study how the underlying per-item difficulty distribution modulates the achievable gain — our simulation directly targets that gap by sweeping heterogeneity while holding mean accuracy fixed.

**Semantic entropy** (Kuhn et al., 2023; extended by Farquhar et al., 2024) clusters sampled generations by bidirectional NLI entailment and computes the Shannon entropy of the resulting cluster distribution, showing it is a reliable unsupervised hallucination signal at scale. Later work (Semantic Entropy Probes, arXiv:2406.15927) shows this signal can be approximated from hidden states without sampling, and cross-model benchmarks (BEACON, arXiv:2606.07528) report standalone semantic-entropy AUROC around 0.60 on at least one recent benchmark — lower than the original paper's headline numbers, suggesting real-world AUROC is dataset/model-dependent. We reuse the cluster-entropy construction directly (our synthetic "wrong answers" are assigned to multiple semantic clusters, mirroring the diversity of plausible-but-wrong outputs) and measure how its discriminative power (AUROC for flagging a wrong self-consistency answer) varies with heterogeneity.

**Confidence calibration.** Verbalized confidence — asking a model to state a numeric confidence — is consistently found to be overconfident and only weakly correlated with true accuracy, especially on questions where the model has little information (arXiv:2509.25532 and related work on suggestibility bias); targeted interventions can reduce ECE substantially but the base verbalized signal remains a known weak point. Frequency-based confidence (the fraction of $k$ samples agreeing with the majority answer, i.e. the self-consistency vote share) is a natural, sampling-based alternative that avoids asking the model to introspect, but its calibration quality relative to verbalized confidence has not, to our knowledge, been systematically compared *as a function of task heterogeneity* — this is the central empirical question of our paper.

## 2. Method

**Task-pool simulation.** We simulate $N=500$ questions. Each question $i$ has a latent per-sample correctness probability $p_i$, drawn from a Beta distribution with parameters $(\alpha,\beta)$ and then rescaled/clipped to a common mean ($\approx 0.55$, matching a mid-range LLM reasoning accuracy) so that only the *variance* (heterogeneity) of $p_i$ differs across settings. We test four regimes: **low** ($\alpha=\beta=20$, $\mathrm{Var}[p]\approx0.006$, near-homogeneous), **moderate** ($\alpha=\beta=4$, $\mathrm{Var}[p]\approx0.028$), **mild** ($\alpha=\beta=1$, uniform, $\mathrm{Var}[p]\approx0.08$), and **extreme** ($\alpha=\beta=0.4$, $\mathrm{Var}[p]\approx0.13$, bimodal — a mix of near-certain and near-impossible questions), covering both a smooth unimodal sweep and a qualitatively different bimodal (easy/hard-mix) regime.

**Sample generation.** For each question we draw up to $k_{\max}=20$ i.i.d. Bernoulli($p_i$) draws indicating whether that sample is correct. Incorrect samples are additionally assigned uniformly to one of $W=4$ distinct "wrong-answer" semantic clusters (correct samples form a fifth, shared "correct" cluster), modeling the fact that wrong LLM outputs are typically more diverse than correct ones. For each $k \in \{1,3,5,10,20\}$ we compute: the majority-vote (mode) cluster over the first $k$ samples (self-consistency answer, correct iff the mode is the correct cluster); the Shannon entropy of the cluster-count distribution (semantic entropy); and the vote share of the majority cluster (frequency-based confidence).

**Verbalized confidence.** We simulate a verbalized confidence signal as an overconfident, noisy transform of the true $p_i$: $\mathrm{conf}_i = \sigma(a \cdot \mathrm{logit}(p_i) + b + \varepsilon_i)$, $\varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal N(0,0.35^2)$, with $a=0.55<1$ (compression toward the middle, i.e. reduced sensitivity to true difficulty) and $b=0.9>0$ (a fixed positive shift, i.e. overconfidence), matching the qualitative literature finding that verbalized confidence is compressed and systematically shifted upward relative to true accuracy.

**Metrics.** We report: (i) self-consistency accuracy vs. $k$, compared against $k=1$ (single-sample) via a paired $t$-test across 30 replicate simulations (fresh random $p_i$ draw and samples each time, all seeded deterministically via SHA-256 of run parameters — never Python's randomized `hash()`); (ii) Expected Calibration Error (ECE, 10 equal-width bins) for verbalized vs. frequency confidence against self-consistency correctness at $k=10$, paired $t$-test across the same 30 replicates; (iii) AUROC of semantic entropy (at $k=10$) for predicting that the self-consistency answer is wrong. We additionally run a **power analysis** (varying the number of replicates from 3 to 30, 5 independent trials per setting) and a **robustness check** re-running the ECE comparison under two alternative verbalized-confidence transforms ($a=0.75,b=0.5$ "weaker overconfidence" and $a=0.4,b=1.3$ "stronger overconfidence"). All code is in `sim.py`; raw console output is in `sim_output.log` and machine-readable results in `sim_results.json`. Total runtime was 1.1 seconds.

## 3. Results

**Self-consistency gain shrinks monotonically with heterogeneity.** Table 1 shows self-consistency accuracy at $k=20$ vs. single-sample ($k=1$) accuracy, mean over 30 replicates of $N=500$ questions each.

| Heterogeneity | Var($p$) | Acc @ $k=1$ | Acc @ $k=20$ | Gain (pp) | paired $t$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Low | 0.006 | 0.541 | 0.981 | 43.9 | $t=148.9$ |
| Moderate | 0.028 | 0.553 | 0.921 | 36.8 | $t=113.0$ |
| Mild (uniform) | 0.080 | 0.546 | 0.780 | 23.4 | $t=47.5$ |
| Extreme (bimodal) | 0.129 | 0.540 | 0.669 | 13.0 | $t=32.6$ |

All four paired $t$-tests are significant at $p<10^{-20}$; given the effect sizes involved (accuracy differences of 13–44 percentage points measured over 500 questions per replicate), these near-machine-precision p-values reflect the deterministic, large-sample nature of the comparison rather than a subtle discovery — the informative statistic here is the effect size (gain in pp), not the p-value magnitude. Applying a Bonferroni correction across all 14 hypothesis tests reported in this paper (main gain/ECE tests plus robustness re-runs) does not change any conclusion, since the largest of these p-values is already $\approx 6\times10^{-7}$, far below $0.05/14\approx0.0036$.

The monotonic shrinkage is mechanical and expected: self-consistency's advantage comes from averaging out independent per-sample errors, which works best when many questions sit at an intermediate, "improvable" difficulty; a bimodal pool (many questions where $p_i$ is already near 0 or 1) has less room for majority voting to help, since near-certain questions gain little and near-impossible questions rarely get rescued by chance-diverse wrong answers converging. Our extreme-heterogeneity gain (13.0pp at $k=20$) is closest to, and slightly below, the real published self-consistency gains of 11.0–17.9pp (Wang et al., 2022, GSM8K/SVAMP/AQuA), while our low/moderate-heterogeneity settings (37–44pp) considerably overshoot real benchmark gains. This suggests real reasoning benchmarks behave more like our extreme/bimodal regime than our near-homogeneous one — plausible, since benchmarks are typically curated to include both easy and hard items — though we caution this is a rough bracketing exercise (single real-world numbers from three benchmarks vs. a synthetic sweep), not a fitted calibration.

**Calibration ranking flips sign with heterogeneity — and the flip is robust.** Table 2 reports ECE at $k=10$ for verbalized vs. frequency-based confidence.

| Heterogeneity | ECE (verbalized) | ECE (frequency) | Better signal | paired $t$, $p$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Low | 0.202 | 0.367 | verbalized | $t=-108.4$, $p\approx2.2\times10^{-39}$ |
| Moderate | 0.152 | 0.297 | verbalized | $t=-102.6$, $p\approx1.1\times10^{-38}$ |
| Mild | 0.125 | 0.150 | verbalized | $t=-6.3$, $p\approx6.2\times10^{-7}$ |
| Extreme | 0.143 | 0.083 | **frequency** | $t=19.9$, $p\approx1.8\times10^{-18}$ |

Under low-to-mild heterogeneity, our (deliberately overconfident) verbalized-confidence signal is *better* calibrated than frequency-based confidence; under extreme heterogeneity the ranking reverses and frequency-based confidence wins decisively. We verified this sign flip is not an artifact of the specific overconfidence transform ($a=0.55, b=0.9$): re-running with a weaker transform ($a=0.75,b=0.5$) and a stronger one ($a=0.4,b=1.3$) reproduces the same qualitative pattern (moderate: verbalized ECE 0.230/0.085 vs. frequency 0.301/0.294; extreme: verbalized ECE 0.125/0.187 vs. frequency 0.084/0.084 — frequency wins under both alternative transforms too, all $p<10^{-16}$). A plausible mechanistic explanation is that frequency confidence is sensitive to the *shape* of the underlying $p_i$ distribution (it reflects sample agreement, itself bimodal when $p_i$ is bimodal) while our fixed-parameter verbalized transform does not adapt to distributional shape — we have not tested this with an additional diagnostic, so we flag it as untested speculation, not an established mechanism.

**Semantic entropy AUROC is comparatively stable across heterogeneity.** AUROC for entropy-based wrong-answer detection at $k=10$ ranged narrowly from 0.816 (low heterogeneity) to 0.871 (extreme heterogeneity) — a much smaller relative swing than the self-consistency-gain or ECE-ranking effects. This is somewhat higher than the ~0.60 standalone semantic-entropy AUROC reported on one recent cross-model benchmark (BEACON, arXiv:2606.07528), which we attribute to our synthetic clustering being noise-free (wrong answers are cleanly assigned to one of $W=4$ clusters) whereas real clustering relies on imperfect NLI judgments — our AUROC is an upper bound under idealized clustering, not a claim about real-world NLI-based semantic entropy.

**Power analysis.** Because our simulated effects are large (13–44pp accuracy gains; ECE differences of 0.02–0.22), the self-consistency-vs-single-sample gain was significant ($p<0.05$) in 5/5 independent trials even at just 3 replicates of $N=500$ questions — i.e., our design is not power-limited, and the near-zero p-values reported above are a genuine reflection of large, easily-detected effect sizes rather than a fragile result requiring many seeds to surface. We note this explicitly since very small p-values combined with a low replicate count can otherwise look suspicious; here it is the effect size, not the replicate count, that drives significance.

## 4. Limitations

Our simulation is a proxy for LLM behavior, not a study of a real LLM: correctness is generated directly from an assumed $p_i$ rather than from actual model generations, semantic clustering is exact rather than NLI-based, and the verbalized-confidence overconfidence transform is a hand-specified functional form rather than fit to real verbalized-confidence data (we are not aware of a published dataset with paired per-item true accuracy and verbalized confidence at the granularity needed to fit this transform directly; our anchor is qualitative — direction and rough compression/shift — from the cited literature, not a numeric fit). The self-consistency-gain anchoring against Wang et al. (2022) uses three published aggregate numbers (11.0/12.2/17.9pp), so "which heterogeneity regime real benchmarks resemble" is a rough bracketing statement, not a calibrated estimate. Our wrong-answer cluster count ($W=4$), uniform across all incorrect samples, is also a simplification; real wrong-answer diversity likely varies by question and model.

## 5. Conclusion

Using a controlled, fully reproducible simulation, we show that two properties often treated as fixed facts about probabilistic LLM uncertainty methods — the size of the self-consistency accuracy gain, and which confidence signal (verbalized vs. frequency-based) is better calibrated — are instead strongly and, in the calibration case, even *qualitatively* (sign-flip) dependent on the heterogeneity of the underlying task-difficulty distribution. The self-consistency gain shrinks monotonically from 43.9pp to 13.0pp as heterogeneity increases across four regimes, bracketing real published gains (11.0–17.9pp) at our most heterogeneous, bimodal setting. The calibration ranking flips from "verbalized confidence wins" to "frequency confidence wins" as heterogeneity increases, and this flip survives three different overconfidence-transform parameterizations, making it a comparatively robust finding within our simulation framework. Practically, this suggests that before choosing self-consistency sample budgets or a confidence signal for routing/abstention, practitioners should characterize whether their task pool is closer to uniformly-difficult or a bimodal easy/hard mix, since the same method can be either strongly beneficial or comparatively weak depending on this one property.
