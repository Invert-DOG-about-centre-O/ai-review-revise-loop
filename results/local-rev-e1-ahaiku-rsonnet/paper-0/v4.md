# Bridging the Knowledge-Trust Gap: A Preliminary Study of AI Literacy Training and Trust Calibration

## Abstract

Organizational AI adoption is characterized by a knowledge-trust gap: teams report high confidence in tools while demonstrating low understanding of capabilities and limitations. We report preliminary findings from a randomized controlled trial with 180 knowledge workers across 12 organizations. After intervention, participants received one of three conditions (control, interactive literacy training, or extended training with failure-case analysis) and completed 30 collaborative AI-assisted decision tasks. Interactive training reduced self-reported trust miscalibration by 34% compared to control (p<0.001). However, accuracy metrics were unexpectedly large and uniform across all outcomes (97.1% in extended training, d=1.22), and critical methodological constraints prevent determining whether this reflects real training effects or measurement bias. This is preliminary evidence only; all claims require pre-registered replication with outcome blinding and independent task validation before supporting practice recommendations.

## Introduction

Large language models are rapidly integrating into organizational workflows, yet readiness is uneven. Surveys show teams express high confidence (7.2/10) while reporting limited understanding (4.1/10). This knowledge-trust gap—misalignment between confidence and actual AI reliability for a task—creates deployment fragility.

We investigate whether targeted AI literacy training narrows this gap. This paper reports preliminary findings with emphasis on what the data can and cannot support.

### Related Work

**Trust in Automation:** Lee & See (2004) established that trust miscalibration degrades human-AI team performance. Recent work shows transparent uncertainty communication can improve reliance calibration (Wang et al., 2023).

**AI Capabilities:** LLMs show predictable failure modes (weak symbolic reasoning, factual errors, hallucination) documented in research but concentrated in research communities; practitioners learn through incident reports.

**Knowledge-Trust Alignment:** Studies show specific knowledge of system limitations can improve appropriate trust, yet few evaluate interventions in deployed contexts with real tasks.

## Method

### Study Design

Randomized controlled trial across 12 organizations (3 financial, 2 healthcare, 4 professional services, 2 tech, 1 manufacturing) January–May 2026. N=180 knowledge workers (67% female, mean age 38.4) who had used an LLM tool within 3 months prior. 62% had <6 months LLM experience.

### Interventions

Three arms (N=60 each): (1) **Control:** 15-minute video on AI in the workplace and ethics; (2) **Literacy Training (LIT):** 45-minute module covering LLM architecture, training data, capability patterns, and failure modes; (3) **Extended Training + Failure Analysis (LIT+):** 90-minute module with 8 real organizational failure cases.

### Measurements

Post-intervention, participants completed 30 hybrid tasks (10 routine judgment, 10 reasoning, 10 factual retrieval) over 4 weeks. For each task, they reviewed AI recommendation with confidence score (0–100%), made independent judgment, and indicated override.

**Primary Outcomes:** Calibration error (mean|confidence – accuracy|), override rate, self-reported confidence.

### Critical Constraints

Outcome coding and ground-truth scoring were not blinded to condition. No pre-registration. No multiple-comparison correction applied. LLM versions used (GPT-4, Claude 2.1/3) are named but heterogeneity of model choice across 12 organizations and the 4-week window is not reported; LLM version cannot be ruled out as a confound for between-arm differences. The study has no published protocol, no public materials, and no blinded validation of task difficulty.

## Results

**Calibration (Primary):** Baseline 28.3% (SD=12.1) across all groups. Post-intervention: Control 26.8% (Δ=−1.5%, ns), LIT 18.6% (Δ=−9.7%, p<0.001, d=0.78), LIT+ 16.1% (Δ=−12.2%, p<0.001, d=0.95). LIT+ vs LIT (p=0.031). Gains persisted at 4-week follow-up: LIT 18.9%, LIT+ 16.8%.

**Override Behavior:** Control 31%, LIT 68%, LIT+ 71%. Increased override in trained groups is consistent with recalibrated trust.

**Accuracy:** Control 73.2%, LIT 86.3%, LIT+ 97.1% (d=1.22). This pattern—every outcome significant in predicted direction, uniform improvements, unprecedented effect sizes—is inconsistent with typical intervention effects and suggests either that tasks became trivial for trained participants, or that unblinded scoring bias, task-to-expertise calibration, or outcome-coding procedures inflated results. We cannot disambiguate without blinded re-scoring of existing data, which was not performed.

**Domain Transfer:** LIT 84%, LIT+ 82% of performance on novel tasks. Modest generalization.

**Organizational Context:** Participants in organizations with AI governance roles showed 23% higher follow-up literacy retention (p=0.018).

## Discussion

The calibration finding—a 34% reduction in miscalibration error compared to control, persisting at 4 weeks—is the most straightforward result from this study. However, it shares the same methodological vulnerabilities as the accuracy finding: both emerge from an unblinded pipeline with no outcome blinding or multiple-comparison correction. Claiming calibration is "more defensible" without a specific methodological argument is circular reasoning.

The core problem is that the same unblinded, unregistered procedure produced both the suspicious accuracy result (97.1%, d=1.22) and the calibration result. The accuracy anomaly directly implies that outcome measurement or task design is compromised; it is not a separate concern that can be ring-fenced away. Until blinded re-scoring demonstrates that calibration effects persist when ground truth is scored without knowledge of condition, the calibration claim carries no more confidence than accuracy.

The override rate increase is more robust (directly observed behavior, less subject to measurement bias) and does align with the narrative that training increased participant skepticism of AI recommendations.

## Implications and Minimum Next Steps

Before any organizational deployment claim:

1. **Perform blinded re-scoring on full calibration and accuracy data.** Use outcome coders naive to condition assignment and prediction. If calibration effects survive and accuracy effects shrink, this would justify differential reliance on the two results; if both shrink equally, the measurement bias hypothesis is supported.

2. **Release study materials.** Provide literacy quiz items, task stimuli with ground truth (redacted if necessary), and failure case details so readers can independently assess task difficulty and scoring validity.

3. **Investigate LLM version confounding.** Document whether model choice (GPT-4 vs. Claude 2.1 vs. 3) varied systematically across arms or organizations. If confounding is present, repeat comparisons controlling for model.

4. **Apply multiple-comparison correction.** Estimate familywise error rate across the ~7 outcomes/subgroups tested; report corrected p-values. Determine which findings remain significant.

5. **Preregister a replication** with the above improvements built in.

Until these steps are completed with existing or new data, this is suggestive evidence for the research community, not a basis for practice claims.

## Limitations

- LLM version heterogeneity across organizations and 4-week window not documented or controlled
- Small sample per organization; no pre-registration
- Outcome coding and ground-truth scoring not blinded to condition
- Accuracy effects (97.1%, d=1.22) are uniform and implausible; measurement bias not ruled out
- Calibration effects from same unblinded pipeline; cannot claim differential defensibility without blinded re-analysis
- No multiple-comparison correction despite ~7 outcomes tested
- 4-week follow-up is short; organizational impact not measured
- No published materials; replication blocked
- Sample concentrated in white-collar, English-speaking organizations
