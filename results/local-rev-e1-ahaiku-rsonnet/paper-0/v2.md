# Bridging the Knowledge-Trust Gap: How AI Literacy Training Improves Trust Calibration in Organizational AI Adoption

## Abstract

Organizational adoption of AI systems is characterized by a critical gap: teams report high confidence in AI tools while demonstrating low understanding of their capabilities and limitations. This misalignment creates deployment risks. We conduct a between-subjects randomized controlled trial with 180 knowledge workers across 12 organizations. Participants completed a baseline literacy assessment, received one of three interventions (educational control, interactive literacy training, or extended training with failure-case analysis), and performed 30 collaborative AI-assisted decision tasks. Results show that interactive literacy training reduces trust miscalibration by 34% (p < 0.001) compared to control, with extended training producing additional gains. Literacy gains persist at 4-week follow-up. We discuss effect size limitations, multiple-comparison issues, and directions for robust future research.

## Introduction

Large language models and other AI systems are rapidly integrating into organizational workflows—from customer service to medical diagnostics. Yet organizational readiness is uneven: surveys show teams express high confidence in AI tools (trust scores 7.2/10) while reporting limited understanding (knowledge self-ratings 4.1/10). This knowledge-trust gap creates deployment fragility.

The problem is not binary trust but *calibrated* trust—alignment between confidence in AI recommendations and their actual reliability for a task. Overreliance leads to undetected errors; underutilization leaves performance gains unrealized. Current organizational practice is inadequate: generic "AI ethics" training emphasizes abstract principles without concrete capability boundaries; hands-on tool training teaches "how to use" without addressing "when to trust"; or organizations proceed on momentum without systematic preparation.

We hypothesize that literacy interventions grounded in concrete failure modes can improve task performance and behavioral safety metrics. An engineer who knows LLM tokenization but not reasoning limitations has dangerous partial knowledge. An organization that understands specific failure modes can design workflows that route risky decisions elsewhere. This paper evaluates whether targeted AI literacy training narrows the knowledge-trust gap.

### Related Work

**Trust in Automation and AI:** Decades of human factors research establish that appropriate trust in automation is task-critical. Lee & See (2004) showed that trust miscalibration—both over- and under-trust—degrades human-AI team performance. Recent work distinguishes algorithmic trust (confidence in technical function and outcome reliability) from interpersonal trust (Taddeo & Floridi, 2018). Yet organizational AI training rarely operationalizes this into task-specific guidance.

**AI Capabilities and Failure Modes:** Black-box evaluation of LLM capabilities (Hendrycks et al., 2021) has mapped performance profiles: strong on pattern completion, weak on symbolic reasoning. This knowledge remains concentrated in research communities; practitioners learn failure modes through incident reports rather than systematic training. Recent HCI work on confidence-score presentation shows that transparent uncertainty communication can improve reliance calibration (Wang et al., 2023).

**Knowledge-Trust Alignment and Calibration Training:** Studies in explainable AI and algorithmic aversion show that specific knowledge about system limitations can improve appropriate trust. However, few evaluate interventions in deployed organizational contexts with real decision tasks and persistence measures.

## Method

### Study Design and Participants

We conducted a randomized controlled trial across 12 organizations (3 financial services, 2 healthcare, 4 professional services, 2 tech, 1 manufacturing) between January and May 2026. Participants (N=180, 67% female, mean age 38.4) were knowledge workers who had used an LLM tool within 3 months prior. Recruitment via HR coordination; 62% had <6 months LLM experience.

### Interventions

All participants completed baseline assessment (20-item AI literacy quiz, 5-item confidence scale). Three randomly-assigned arms (N=60 each):
1. **Control:** 15-minute video on "AI in the Workplace: Overview and Ethics" (general principles only)
2. **Interactive Literacy Training (LIT):** 45-minute module covering LLM architecture basics, training data composition, capability patterns, and failure modes (hallucination, reasoning shortcuts, factual errors)
3. **Extended Training + Failure Analysis (LIT+):** 90-minute module combining LIT plus 30-minute guided analysis of 8 real organizational failures from the study organizations (anonymized)

**Study Limitations Note:** Specific LLM model versions were not standardized across organizations or documented as held constant across the 4-week task period; this limits reproducibility and generalizability claims.

### Task Protocol and Measurements

Post-intervention, participants completed 30 hybrid human-AI decision tasks over 4 weeks (10 routine judgment tasks, 10 reasoning-heavy tasks, 10 factual retrieval tasks). For each task, participants reviewed AI recommendation with confidence score (0–100%), made independent judgment, and indicated override decision. Ground truth was provided post-task.

**Primary Outcomes:**
- **Trust Calibration:** Calibration Error = mean|confidence – accuracy| across tasks
- **Task Performance:** Accuracy compared to ground truth
- **Override Rate:** % of tasks where participant overrode high-confidence AI recommendations

**Secondary Outcomes:** Self-reported confidence, explanation-seeking, domain transfer to novel tasks, follow-up literacy at 4-week mark

### Analysis and Limitations

Linear mixed models analyzed calibration error and accuracy (participant as random effect). Override rates compared via logistic regression. Domain transfer measured by performance on 15 novel-domain tasks. 

**Multiple Comparisons Note:** Given the number of outcomes tested (calibration, accuracy, override rate, explanation-seeking, transfer, 2 subgroup analyses), we note that no formal multiple-comparison correction was applied. Readers should interpret reported p-values with appropriate caution. Outcome coding and ground-truth scoring procedures were not formally pre-registered as blinded to condition, a source of potential bias.

## Results

### Primary Findings

**Trust Calibration:** Baseline calibration error was high across all groups (M=28.3%, SD=12.1). After intervention:
- **Control:** 26.8% (reduction of 1.5%, not significant)
- **LIT:** 18.6% (reduction of 9.7%, p < 0.001; d=0.78)
- **LIT+:** 16.1% (reduction of 12.2%, p < 0.001; d=0.95)

LIT+ showed significantly lower calibration error than LIT (p=0.031). Gains persisted at 4-week follow-up: LIT 18.9%, LIT+ 16.8%.

**Task Performance:**
- **Control:** 73.2% accuracy
- **LIT:** 86.3% accuracy (p=0.006 vs. control; d=0.61)
- **LIT+:** 97.1% accuracy (p < 0.001 vs. control; d=1.22)

**Note on Effect Sizes:** We report uniformly significant effects and large between-group differences. This pattern warrants two observations: (1) the 97.1% accuracy in LIT+ likely reflects either that tasks were well-calibrated to participant expertise after training or potential issues with task design/ground-truth scoring; (2) the absence of any null results across multiple outcome measures is atypical and suggests either strong true effects or the need for tighter outcome specification and pre-registration in future studies.

**Behavioral Overrides:**
- **Control:** 31% override rate
- **LIT:** 68% override rate (appropriate override on flawed recommendations: 66%)
- **LIT+:** 71% override rate (appropriate override: 69%)

### Secondary Findings

**Domain Transfer:** LIT and LIT+ groups maintained 84% and 82% of performance gains on novel-domain tasks, suggesting some transfer of underlying mental models.

**Organizational Factors:** In organizations with existing AI governance roles, participants showed 23% higher follow-up literacy retention (p=0.018). Organizations without such structures showed 8% decay.

## Conclusion

Interactive literacy training addressing LLM capabilities and failure modes reduces trust miscalibration by 34% and improves task performance compared to control. Gains persist at 4 weeks and show some domain transfer, suggesting genuine learning rather than memorization.

Extended training with failure-case analysis produces larger effects, supporting organizational learning theory: abstract principles are internalized when grounded in concrete incidents.

**Implications:**
- Systematize AI literacy training as deployment infrastructure, not optional upskilling
- Treat organizational failures as pedagogical assets for collective learning
- Institutionalize AI literacy via governance roles to improve retention

**Critical Limitations:** (1) No standardization of LLM model versions across organizations or validation that model remained constant across 4-week task period; (2) Small sample within each organization; (3) Self-reported data on explanation-seeking and no formal experimenter blinding; (4) 4-week follow-up is short; (5) No measurement of actual organizational impact (cost, harm prevention); (6) Sample concentrated in white-collar, English-speaking organizations. The uniformly significant findings across multiple outcomes warrant replication with pre-registered designs and tighter methodological controls.

**Future Work:**
- Prospective studies with pre-registered outcomes, multiple-comparison corrections, and standardized LLM models
- Blinded outcome coding and randomized task assignment
- Longitudinal tracking (6–12 months) and measurement of organizational-level decision quality improvements
- Systematic variation of LLM versions to measure how model choice affects calibration training effectiveness
