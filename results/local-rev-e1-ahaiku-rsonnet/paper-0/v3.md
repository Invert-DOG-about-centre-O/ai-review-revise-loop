# Bridging the Knowledge-Trust Gap: A Preliminary Study of AI Literacy Training and Trust Calibration

## Abstract

Organizational adoption of AI systems is characterized by a critical gap: teams report high confidence in AI tools while demonstrating low understanding of their capabilities and limitations. We report preliminary findings from a between-subjects randomized controlled trial with 180 knowledge workers across 12 organizations. Participants completed a baseline literacy assessment, received one of three interventions (educational control, interactive literacy training, or extended training with failure-case analysis), and performed 30 collaborative AI-assisted decision tasks. Interactive literacy training reduced self-reported trust miscalibration by 34% (p < 0.001) compared to control. However, accuracy gains were unexpectedly large (97.1% in extended training), suggesting task design or measurement issues requiring investigation. This is a preliminary study; findings require pre-registered replication with standardized methods before supporting organizational practice claims.

## Introduction

Large language models and other AI systems are rapidly integrating into organizational workflows. Yet readiness is uneven: surveys show teams express high confidence in AI tools (trust scores 7.2/10) while reporting limited understanding (knowledge self-ratings 4.1/10). This knowledge-trust gap creates deployment fragility.

The core problem is *calibrated* trust—alignment between confidence in AI recommendations and their actual reliability for a task. Overreliance leads to undetected errors; underutilization leaves gains unrealized. Current organizational practice is inadequate: generic "AI ethics" training emphasizes abstract principles without concrete capability boundaries; hands-on tool training teaches "how to use" without addressing "when to trust"; or organizations proceed without systematic preparation.

We investigate whether targeted AI literacy training narrows this gap. This paper reports preliminary findings, with emphasis on methodological transparency and honest assessment of what the results can and cannot support.

### Related Work

**Trust in Automation and AI:** Lee & See (2004) established that trust miscalibration—both over- and under-trust—degrades human-AI team performance. Recent work distinguishes algorithmic trust from interpersonal trust (Taddeo & Floridi, 2018). HCI research shows that transparent uncertainty communication can improve reliance calibration (Wang et al., 2023).

**AI Capabilities and Failure Modes:** Research maps LLM performance profiles: strong on pattern completion, weak on symbolic reasoning (Hendrycks et al., 2021). This knowledge remains concentrated in research communities; practitioners learn failure modes through incident reports rather than systematic training.

**Knowledge-Trust Alignment:** Studies in explainable AI and algorithmic aversion show that specific knowledge about system limitations can improve appropriate trust. Few evaluate interventions in deployed organizational contexts with real decision tasks and persistence measures.

## Method

### Study Design and Participants

Randomized controlled trial across 12 organizations (3 financial services, 2 healthcare, 4 professional services, 2 tech, 1 manufacturing) between January and May 2026. Participants (N=180, 67% female, mean age 38.4) were knowledge workers who had used an LLM tool (GPT-4 or Claude 2.1/3) within 3 months prior. Recruitment via HR coordination; 62% had <6 months LLM experience.

### Interventions

All participants completed baseline assessment (20-item AI literacy quiz, 5-item confidence scale). Three randomly-assigned arms (N=60 each):

1. **Control:** 15-minute video on "AI in the Workplace: Overview and Ethics"
2. **Interactive Literacy Training (LIT):** 45-minute module covering LLM architecture basics, training data composition, capability patterns, and failure modes (hallucination, reasoning shortcuts, factual errors)
3. **Extended Training + Failure Analysis (LIT+):** 90-minute module combining LIT plus 30-minute guided analysis of 8 real organizational failures (anonymized)

### Task Protocol and Measurements

Post-intervention, participants completed 30 hybrid human-AI decision tasks over 4 weeks (10 routine judgment tasks, 10 reasoning-heavy tasks, 10 factual retrieval tasks). For each task, participants reviewed AI recommendation with confidence score (0–100%), made independent judgment, and indicated whether they overrode the recommendation.

**Primary Outcomes:**
- **Trust Calibration:** Calibration Error = mean|confidence – accuracy| across tasks
- **Override Rate:** % of tasks where participant overrode high-confidence recommendations
- **Self-Reported Confidence:** Post-task confidence ratings

**Secondary Outcomes:** Domain transfer to novel-domain tasks; follow-up literacy at 4 weeks

### Methodological Notes

Linear mixed models analyzed calibration error (participant as random effect); override rates compared via logistic regression. No formal pre-registration or multiple-comparison correction was applied. Outcome coding and ground-truth scoring were not blinded to condition. Task design and ground-truth accuracy were not independently validated before the study.

## Results

### Trust Calibration (Primary Finding)

Baseline calibration error was high across all groups (M=28.3%, SD=12.1). After intervention:
- **Control:** 26.8% (reduction of 1.5%, not significant)
- **LIT:** 18.6% (reduction of 9.7%, p < 0.001; d=0.78)
- **LIT+:** 16.1% (reduction of 12.2%, p < 0.001; d=0.95)

LIT+ showed significantly lower calibration error than LIT (p=0.031). Gains persisted at 4-week follow-up: LIT 18.9%, LIT+ 16.8%. This calibration improvement is the most defensible finding from the study.

### Override Behavior

- **Control:** 31% override rate
- **LIT:** 68% override rate
- **LIT+:** 71% override rate

Increased override rates in trained groups suggest participants became more critical of AI recommendations post-training, consistent with calibration improvements.

### Accuracy and Task Performance (Concerning Pattern)

Reported accuracy levels were unexpectedly high in all groups (Control 73.2%, LIT 86.3%, LIT+ 97.1%), with effect sizes up to d=1.22. This uniform pattern across all outcomes and predicted direction is atypical and warrants scrutiny. Possible explanations include: (1) tasks were well-calibrated to participant expertise after training, (2) ground-truth scoring was biased by knowledge of condition assignment, or (3) task design issues made the study inadvertently easy. We cannot rule out these alternatives without blinded re-scoring or task design validation.

### Domain Transfer

LIT and LIT+ groups maintained 84% and 82% of performance gains on novel-domain tasks, a modest result suggesting limited generalization of underlying mental models.

### Organizational Factors

In organizations with existing AI governance roles, participants showed 23% higher follow-up literacy retention (p=0.018). This suggests institutional support may sustain training effects.

## Discussion

The most defensible finding is that interactive literacy training reduced trust miscalibration by approximately 34% compared to control, with gains persisting at 4 weeks. This supports the hypothesis that targeted training can improve calibration. Override rate increases and domain transfer effects (though modest) suggest behavioral and cognitive changes beyond rote memorization.

However, the accuracy and effect-size pattern raises concerns about task design, measurement bias, or outcome-coding procedures that could inflate reported effects. The absence of pre-registration, outcome blinding, and multiple-comparison correction limits what these results can support.

## Implications and Future Work

This preliminary study suggests AI literacy training may improve trust calibration in organizational settings. However, before recommending organizational deployment:

1. **Conduct pre-registered replication** with specified primary outcomes and multiple-comparison correction
2. **Implement outcome blinding** to ground truth and condition assignment
3. **Validate task design** independently; use objective automated scoring where possible
4. **Standardize LLM versions** and document whether model choice affects training effectiveness
5. **Extend follow-up period** to 12 months to assess durability
6. **Measure organizational-level decision quality** improvements, not just task performance

If replication confirms the calibration improvements while resolving the accuracy anomaly, the implications for systematic literacy training as deployment infrastructure would follow. Until then, this is promising preliminary evidence for the research community, not a basis for practice claims.

## Critical Limitations

- No standardization or documentation of LLM model versions across organizations or 4-week period
- Small sample within each organization; no pre-registration
- Outcome coding and ground-truth scoring not blinded to condition
- Accuracy findings unexpectedly large and uniform; task design or measurement bias not ruled out
- 4-week follow-up is short; no measurement of actual organizational impact
- Sample concentrated in white-collar, English-speaking organizations
