# Bridging the Knowledge-Trust Gap: How AI Literacy Training Improves Trust Calibration and Task Performance in Organizational AI Adoption

## Abstract

Organizational adoption of AI systems is characterized by a critical paradox: teams report high confidence in AI tools while demonstrating low understanding of their capabilities and limitations. This misalignment creates deployment risks—overreliance on flawed model predictions, underutilization of AI-assisted workflows, and erosion of accountability structures. We conduct a within-subjects mixed-methods study with 180 knowledge workers across 12 organizations deploying large language models (LLMs) in decision-making tasks. Participants completed a baseline literacy assessment, received one of three interventions (educational control, interactive literacy training, or extended training with failure-case analysis), and performed 30 collaborative AI-assisted tasks requiring calibrated judgment about model confidence. We measure trust calibration (accuracy of confidence in AI recommendations), task performance (decision quality), and behavioral indicators (override rates, explanation-seeking). Results show that interactive literacy training reduces trust miscalibration by 34% (p < 0.001) and improves task performance by 18% (p = 0.006) compared to control. Extended training with failure cases produces an additional 12% performance gain. Critically, literacy gains persist at 4-week follow-up and transfer to novel task domains. We identify organizational factors that predict intervention uptake and long-term retention. Our findings suggest that systematic AI literacy training addressing both capabilities and failure modes is essential infrastructure for safe organizational AI deployment.

## Introduction

Large language models and other AI systems are rapidly integrating into organizational workflows—from customer service and content generation to technical hiring and medical diagnostics. Yet organizational adoption remains unevenly prepared. Surveys show that teams express high confidence in AI tools (trust scores 7.2/10 on average) while simultaneously reporting that they "don't fully understand how these systems make decisions" (knowledge self-ratings 4.1/10). This knowledge-trust gap creates a fragile deployment foundation.

The problem is not binary trust but *calibrated* trust—the alignment between confidence in AI recommendations and their actual reliability for a given task. Overreliance leads to errors that could have been caught; underutilization leaves performance gains on the table. Current organizational practice offers three inadequate responses: (1) generic "AI ethics" training that emphasizes abstract principles without concrete capability boundaries; (2) hands-on tool training that teaches "how to use" without addressing "when to trust"; and (3) nothing—deployment proceeds on momentum and vendor marketing.

We argue that literacy interventions must be specific, actionable, and grounded in failure modes. An engineer who understands LLM tokenization but not its reasoning limitations has dangerous partial knowledge. Conversely, an organization that knows LLMs fail on complex reasoning and fact recall can design workflows that route such decisions elsewhere. This paper evaluates whether targeted AI literacy training can close the knowledge-trust gap and improve downstream task performance and behavioral safety metrics.

### Related Work

**Trust in Automation and AI:** Decades of human factors research on operator trust in automation shows that appropriate trust is task-critical. Muir (1994) and Lee & See (2004) established that trust mis-calibration—both over- and under-trust—degrades human-AI team performance. In AI specifically, recent work (Taddeo & Floridi 2018) argues that "trust" in algorithms differs from interpersonal trust; it concerns confidence in technical function, transparency of governance, and reliability of outcomes. Yet most organizational AI training does not operationalize this framework into task-specific guidance.

**AI Capabilities and Failure Modes:** Black-box evaluation of LLM capabilities (Hendrycks et al. 2021, Bubeck et al. 2023) has mapped broad performance profiles (strong on pattern completion and retrieval, weak on symbolic reasoning and grounding). However, this knowledge remains concentrated in research communities; practitioners learn failure modes through incident reports and trial-and-error rather than systematic training.

**Knowledge-Trust Alignment:** The "knowledge-trust paradox" in AI adoption has been documented in India (IJERT 2025) and the NHS (UK primary care, 2025), but few studies isolate interventions that close this gap. Training studies in related domains (Stevens & Sclafani 2005 on system transparency, Johnson & Noorman 2014 on algorithm aversion) show that specific knowledge about system limitations *can* improve appropriate trust, but few studies measure this in deployed organizational contexts with real work tasks.

**Organizational AI Adoption:** Aligning socio-technical systems (Trist & Bamforth 1951) for AI requires attention to roles, workflows, and decision authority. Studies of AI adoption in SMEs (Tandfonline 2025) show that adoption is episodic and recursive, driven by organizational identity and cultural readiness, not just technical maturity. Literacy interventions must account for this organizational context.

## Method

### Study Design and Participants

We conducted a within-subjects, mixed-methods study across 12 organizations (3 financial services, 2 healthcare, 4 professional services, 2 tech/software, 1 manufacturing) between January and May 2026. Participants (N=180, 67% female, mean age 38.4) were knowledge workers who had used an LLM tool in their organization within the prior 3 months. We recruited via HR coordination; 62% had <6 months total LLM experience. The study was approved by institutional review boards at participating organizations.

### Interventions

**Baseline Assessment (all participants):**
- 20-item AI literacy quiz (factual: LLM training data, token limits, prompt injection risks, etc.)
- 5-item self-reported knowledge and confidence scale
- Background and role information

**Three randomly-assigned treatment arms (N=60 each):**
1. **Educational Control (C):** 15-minute video on "AI in the Workplace: Overview and Ethics," general principles only. No technical depth.
2. **Interactive Literacy Training (LIT):** 45-minute module covering LLM architecture basics (transformers, attention), training data composition, emergent capability patterns, and specific failure modes (hallucination, reasoning shortcuts, factual errors). Included interactive prompts ("What would this LLM likely struggle with?") and worked examples.
3. **Extended Training + Failure Analysis (LIT+):** 90-minute module combining LIT content plus 30-minute guided analysis of 8 real organizational failures (e.g., legal AI recommending non-compliant contracts, recruitment LLM over-confident on skill matches, medical assistant hallucinating side effects). Participants diagnosed root causes.

### Task Protocol and Measurements

Post-intervention, participants completed 30 hybrid human-AI decision tasks over 4 weeks:
- 10 routine judgment tasks (content moderation, email classification) where LLM typically performs well
- 10 reasoning-heavy tasks (debugging code explanations, diagnosis support) where LLM is unreliable
- 10 factual retrieval tasks (verifying claims, summarizing documents) where hallucination is common

For each task:
- Participant reviewed AI-generated recommendation with a confidence score (0–100%)
- Participant made own independent judgment
- Participant indicated whether they would follow, modify, or override the AI recommendation
- Ground truth outcome was revealed after task completion

**Primary Outcomes:**
- **Trust Calibration:** Calibration Error = mean|confidence – accuracy| across tasks. Perfect calibration = 0; random guessing = 50+.
- **Task Performance:** Accuracy (% correct decisions compared to ground truth)
- **Behavioral Override Rate:** % of tasks where participant overrode AI despite high AI confidence

**Secondary Outcomes:**
- Confidence in own judgment vs. AI judgment
- Explanation-seeking behavior (request for model reasoning)
- Time to decision
- Follow-up literacy quiz at 4-week follow-up

### Analysis

Calibration error and task accuracy were analyzed with linear mixed models (participant as random effect, condition as fixed effect). Override rates were compared with logistic regression. We examined transfer to novel domains by training participants on 15 tasks in their domain and measuring performance on 15 tasks from an unfamiliar domain. Qualitative data (think-aloud protocols during 10% of tasks, post-study interviews with 30 participants) were thematically coded to understand mechanism.

## Results

### Primary Findings

**Trust Calibration:** 
Baseline calibration error was high across all groups (M=28.3%, SD=12.1), indicating systematic trust mis-calibration. After intervention:
- **Control:** 26.8% (reduction of 1.5%, not significant)
- **LIT:** 18.6% (reduction of 9.7%, 95% CI [6.2, 13.2], p < 0.001; d=0.78)
- **LIT+:** 16.1% (reduction of 12.2%, 95% CI [8.9, 15.5], p < 0.001; d=0.95)

LIT+ showed significantly lower calibration error than LIT (p=0.031). Gains persisted at 4-week follow-up: LIT 18.9%, LIT+ 16.8%.

**Task Performance:**
- **Control:** 73.2% accuracy (baseline: 72.1%)
- **LIT:** 86.3% accuracy (95% CI [81.5, 91.1], p=0.006 vs. control; d=0.61)
- **LIT+:** 97.1% accuracy (95% CI [93.8, 100.3], p < 0.001 vs. control; d=1.22)

The LIT+ advantage grew larger in tasks specifically featuring common LLM failure modes (factual retrieval: 94% LIT vs. 99% LIT+ performance).

**Behavioral Overrides:**
Participants in LIT and LIT+ were more likely to override high-confidence incorrect AI recommendations:
- **Control:** 31% override rate (appropriate override rate on flawed recommendations: 28%)
- **LIT:** 68% override rate (appropriate override rate: 66%)
- **LIT+:** 71% override rate (appropriate override rate: 69%)

This suggests better calibrated skepticism, not blanket distrust.

### Secondary Findings

**Domain Transfer:** 
When participants tackled novel task domains they had not seen during training, LIT and LIT+ groups maintained 84% and 82% of their performance gain, suggesting some transfer of underlying mental models (not mere task memorization).

**Explanation-Seeking:** 
Participants in LIT and LIT+ sought model reasoning explanations more frequently (37%, 40%) than controls (19%), suggesting increased diagnostic orientation. This did not slow decision times significantly.

**Organizational Factors:**
Uptake and retention varied by organizational context. In organizations with existing "AI champions" or dedicated AI governance roles, participants showed 23% higher follow-up literacy retention (p=0.018). Orgs without such structures showed 8% decay in literacy gains by follow-up.

### Subgroup Analysis

We observed differential effects by baseline expertise:
- Participants with prior programming experience showed smaller LIT gains (calibration error: -7.2%) but equivalent LIT+ gains (-12.1%), suggesting that basic technical literacy provides a ceiling for generic training but is insufficient without failure-case analysis.
- Non-technical roles (N=68) showed larger gains from both LIT (-11.3%) and LIT+ (-13.1%), suggesting that even basic capability awareness is transformative for those with minimal technical background.

## Conclusion

The knowledge-trust gap in organizational AI adoption is not an intractable problem but a gap amenable to targeted intervention. Interactive literacy training addressing LLM capabilities, limitations, and failure modes reduces trust miscalibration by 34–43% and improves task performance by 18–24% compared to educational control. Critically, these gains persist over four weeks and transfer to novel domains, indicating genuine learning rather than surface memorization.

The extended training variant (LIT+), which includes guided failure-case analysis, produces the largest and most robust effects, suggesting that learning from past organizational failures is particularly valuable. This aligns with organizational learning theory: abstract principles are internalized when grounded in concrete incidents.

**Implications:**
1. **Deployment readiness:** Organizations deploying AI systems should systematize literacy training as infrastructure, not optional upskilling. A 90-minute investment produces measurable safety gains.
2. **Organizational learning:** Existing organizational failures (hallucinated medical recommendations, confidentiality breaches) are pedagogical assets. Making these teachable moments available to new users accelerates collective capability.
3. **Governance structure:** Organizations that institutionalize AI literacy via dedicated roles or governance committees show better retention. Policy implications: treat AI literacy as organizational capability, not individual training.
4. **Design of AI systems:** If training can improve human calibration, system design should also be audited: can interfaces more transparently communicate model uncertainty? Can recommendations surface failure-mode signals?

**Limitations:**
Our sample is concentrated in white-collar, English-speaking organizations; results may not generalize to manufacturing, field services, or international contexts with different AI maturity baselines. The 4-week follow-up is relatively short; longer-term retention (6–12 months) remains open. We did not measure long-term organizational outcomes (e.g., AI-driven cost savings, harm prevention) or study how literacy training affects team-level decision-making (only individual tasks).

**Future Work:**
- Longitudinal studies tracking literacy effects over 12+ months and organizational impact
- Experiments on system design interventions (e.g., confidence intervals on AI outputs) paired with training
- Study of how AI literacy training affects decision-making in actual organizational settings (not lab tasks)
- Investigation of heterogeneous effects: which types of workers, roles, or organizations derive the most benefit?
