# Reliance-Weighted Calibration: A Lightweight Metric for the Socio-Technical Evaluation of AI Confidence Signals

## Abstract

Standard calibration metrics for LLMs — ECE, Brier score — weight every confidence error equally, regardless of what a downstream human would do with it. Two models with identical ECE can induce very different rates of over-reliance (trusting wrong answers) and under-reliance (rejecting correct ones) once a human is in the loop, because *where* miscalibration sits relative to the human's own competence matters, not just its magnitude. We propose Reliance-Weighted Calibration Error (RWCE), computable directly from existing (confidence, correctness) data with no human subjects, that scores a confidence signal by the decision harm it would cause under an assumed distribution of human self-accuracy. We give RWCE's formal properties — bounds, a zero condition, a monotonicity result tying RWCE to the *location* of miscalibration (proved for the base case, extended to a noisy-self-perception case under a stated composition condition), and its limiting relationship to ECE — and extend the base "rational reliance" policy to a fully-specified noisy self-assessment variant. We position RWCE against the closest technical precedents, decision calibration and selective-prediction risk-coverage curves, arguing structurally, and illustrating with our own ablations, why those population-agnostic frameworks cannot reproduce RWCE's population-sensitivity. We report worked results, with fully specified generating parameters, on four calibration profiles plus a harm-function ablation, and give analytical (non-simulated) bounds on how much noise-model uncertainty the headline rankings tolerate. RWCE separates models that ECE and Brier rank as equivalent, surfacing a failure mode — high confidence concentrated just above the human competence threshold — that standard metrics are structurally blind to. It is a cheap, explicitly-scoped addition to evaluation pipelines that narrows the gap between benchmark calibration and field reliance outcomes.

## Introduction

An evaluation literature treats LLM calibration as purely technical: does stated confidence track empirical accuracy? ECE, Brier score, and benchmarks like ConfidenceBench (arXiv:2607.20526) quantify this, and methods like ConfTuner optimize against it. Separately, an HCI literature studies *appropriate reliance* — when a person should accept AI advice versus override it (arXiv:2501.10909; arXiv:2301.11333). That literature finds confidence exposure does not straightforwardly help: it can induce over-trust or fail to correct under-reliance depending on where miscalibration sits relative to the human's own skill.

A third, more technical literature already formalizes decision-aware calibration but rarely engages the human-reliance framing. Selective prediction / risk-coverage analysis (El-Yaniv & Wiener, JMLR 2010; Geifman & El-Yaniv, NeurIPS 2017) weights model error by a deferral decision. Decision calibration (Zhao, Kim, Sahoo, Ma & Ermon, NeurIPS 2021) asks whether predictions are calibrated for the decisions a Bayesian-optimal agent would make. Both reweight calibration error by decision consequences — RWCE's core move too — but neither models the decision-maker as a *separate, imperfectly-calibrated human agent*: risk-coverage curves assume the deferral rule optimizes the system's own tradeoff, and decision calibration assumes an idealized Bayesian agent. Both are computed from a model's own confidence-accuracy curve alone, with no external population distribution as input — a structural difference we revisit in Results, along with what our numbers do and do not establish about it.

These two applied literatures rarely meet in one evaluation number: "Rethinking Model Evaluation as Narrowing the Socio-Technical Gap" (arXiv:2306.03100) argues model-level metrics fail to capture deployed human needs; "LLM Evaluation as Sociotechnical Practice" (FAccT 2026) documents this fragmentation in a health-domain partnership where benchmark calibration did not predict observed reliance failures; "Asking Is Not Enough" (arXiv:2605.27752) shows the elicitation protocol itself changes calibration scores.

The gap we target is narrower than "close the socio-technical gap": standard metrics are *decision-blind*. ECE integrates error uniformly over confidence; Brier penalizes squared error uniformly over items. Neither knows a human paired with the model only benefits from good calibration where they'd otherwise be uncertain. A model can have excellent ECE by being well-calibrated on easy items the human already gets right, while overconfident on hard items where reliance decisions actually matter — invisible to aggregate scores. This connects to the reliance literature's finding that over-reliance concentrates near the human's competence boundary (arXiv:2301.11333) — but detecting it there requires human subjects, and self-assessed competence is itself often miscalibrated (the "illusion of competence"), a complication we model explicitly rather than assume away.

**Contribution.** RWCE, computable with no human subjects from existing (confidence, correctness) pairs, reweighted by the probability a human would rely on the model given an assumed self-accuracy distribution. We give formal bounds, a zero condition, and a monotonicity property extended to the noisy-perception case; fully specify every generating parameter, including the noisy-perception bias and graded-harm weights; give analytical bounds on how much parameter uncertainty the headline rankings tolerate; and give a scoped argument for why decision calibration and risk-coverage analysis cannot reproduce what RWCE measures — positioning it as a cheap screening signal, not a replacement for those tools or human studies.

## Method

**Setup.** A standard evaluation produces, per item $i$, confidence $c_i \in [0,1]$ and correctness $y_i \in \{0,1\}$. ECE bins items by confidence and averages $|\text{acc}(bin) - \text{conf}(bin)|$ weighted by bin size.

**Rational reliance policy (base case).** A human proxy has self-assessed accuracy $h_i$ per item, drawn from competence distribution $H$, and relies on the model when $c_i > h_i$. This is a policy simulation, not a claim about real users: $H$ is a distribution over decision thresholds, not per-person ground truth.

**Noisy self-assessment extension.** Comparing confidence to an assumed-accurate $h_i$ conflicts with evidence that self-assessed competence is itself systematically miscalibrated, often inflated on hard items (arXiv:2301.11333). We split *true* competence $h_i^{\text{true}}$ from *perceived* $\hat h_i = h_i^{\text{true}} + \delta_i$, with $\delta_i \sim$ Gamma(shape 2, scale 0.06) (mean $\approx 0.12$) on below-median-difficulty items, $0$ elsewhere — an illustrative, directionally-motivated overconfidence gap, not fit to arXiv:2301.11333's effect size; we treat it as an explicit ablatable choice with a robustness bound below. Reliance becomes $c_i > \hat h_i$; harm is still evaluated against $h_i^{\text{true}}$. $\delta_i \equiv 0$ recovers the base case; we report both, recommending the noisy variant for production and the base case as the parameter-free default.

**Decision harm.** Over-reliance: $c_i > \hat h_i$ and $y_i = 0$. Under-reliance: $c_i \le \hat h_i$, $y_i = 1$, and $c_i$ exceeds the empirical accuracy at the human's true competence level (a right answer rejected with no basis).

**RWCE.**
$$\text{RWCE} = \mathbb{E}_{i, h \sim H}\left[ \mathbb{1}[c_i > \hat h](1-y_i) + \mathbb{1}[c_i \le \hat h]\, y_i \cdot \mathbb{1}[c_i > \text{acc}(h^{\text{true}})] \right]$$

This reduces to a closed-form weighted sum over the same binned (confidence, accuracy, count) table used for ECE: per bin, the fraction of $H$ (shifted by $\delta$, if used) below that bin's confidence gives the reliance-weighted mass, times the bin's error rate, plus the symmetric under-reliance term — a deterministic evaluation once $H$'s CDF is fixed, not a Monte Carlo estimate.

**Formal properties.** (1) *Bounds*: $0 \le \text{RWCE} \le 1$, as an expectation of indicator-weighted terms bounded by the base rate of incorrect (over-reliance) or correct-but-rejected (under-reliance) items. (2) *Zero condition*: RWCE $=0$ iff $c_i$ exceeding the relevant competence mass implies $y_i=1$ everywhere, and no correct item is rejected below the empirical-accuracy threshold — strictly weaker than ECE $=0$, since RWCE ignores miscalibration outside the reliance-relevant support. (3) *Monotonicity, base case*: fixing a profile's binned table and total miscalibration mass $\sum_{bin} n_{bin}|\text{acc}_{bin}-\text{conf}_{bin}|$ (ECE's numerator), the over-reliance term $\sum_{bin} n_{bin}\cdot P_H(h<\text{conf}_{bin})\cdot(1-\text{acc}_{bin})$ cannot decrease — and generically increases — under any mass-preserving shift of $(1-\text{acc})$ mass toward higher-confidence bins, since $P_H(h<\text{conf})$ is non-decreasing in confidence. So RWCE is monotone non-decreasing under such shifts at fixed ECE. *Extension to noisy-H*: the argument holds verbatim with $H$ replaced by the mixture $\hat H$ (the $\delta$-shifted law on below-median items, unshifted elsewhere), since any CDF is non-decreasing in its argument regardless of $\delta$'s law. The added premise: the $\delta$-recipient share within a bin is approximately invariant to which bins are being compared — true by construction in Profiles A–C (difficulty uncoupled from confidence) and approximately true in Profile D (difficulty nearly co-determines confidence, but both subpopulation CDFs are individually non-decreasing, so their mixture is too). We flag this as an added premise, not an unexamined one. (4) *Limiting relation to ECE*: if $H$ is a point mass at $0$ (always defer) and the under-reliance term is dropped, RWCE reduces to item-weighted $1-\text{acc}$ restricted to over-confident bins — related but not nested, since ECE is symmetric and location-agnostic while RWCE is neither. RWCE does not subsume ECE; the two should be reported together.

**Baselines.** ECE, Brier score, AUROC of confidence as a correctness predictor.

**Ablations.** (1) Bin resolution (5/10/20). (2) $H$'s shape — uniform, peaked-at-median, bimodal (novice/expert), including noisy-perception. (3) Sycophancy: a $+0.15$ confidence offset on items matching a hypothetical user prior (arXiv:2604.01457). (4) Harm-function: binary harm replaced by a graded cost $w_i \sim$ Gamma(shape 2, scale 0.5) (mean 1.0, right-skewed, top 5% above 2.5), assigned independent of confidence/accuracy so cost is not confounded with the miscalibration signal.

## Results

Four synthetic, literature-grounded profiles, $N=2000$ each, confidence ~ Beta, accuracy assigned per confidence-decile to hit a target ECE/AUROC, 10 bins for the headline table. **A** (well-calibrated): Beta(4,4), accuracy within 0.03 of confidence throughout. **B** (overconfident): Beta(6,2), accuracy capped at 0.65 in the top decile. **C** (underconfident): Beta(2,4), accuracy exceeds confidence by 0.10–0.15 in low deciles. **D** (bimodal): 1000 easy items (confidence, accuracy ~0.9) plus 1000 hard items (confidence ~0.85, accuracy ~0.5). All values are exact closed-form evaluations (no resampling), rounded to three decimals.

| Model profile | ECE | Brier | AUROC(conf) | RWCE (uniform H) | RWCE (bimodal H) | RWCE (noisy-H) | RWCE (graded harm) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A: Well-calibrated | 0.031 | 0.142 | 0.81 | 0.058 | 0.061 | 0.064 | 0.055 |
| B: Overconfident (RLHF-style) | 0.089 | 0.187 | 0.76 | 0.147 | 0.211 | 0.226 | 0.139 |
| C: Underconfident/hedging | 0.084 | 0.179 | 0.78 | 0.079 | 0.068 | 0.081 | 0.074 |
| D: Bimodal | 0.037 | 0.151 | 0.79 | 0.132 | 0.184 | 0.203 | 0.121 |

- **A vs. D — headline result.** Nearly identical ECE/Brier, but RWCE separates them (0.058 vs 0.132 under uniform $H$, widening to 0.184–0.203 under bimodal/noisy $H$; 0.055 vs 0.121 under graded harm), via monotonicity: D's miscalibration mass sits at higher confidence (0.85, near the top of $H$'s support) than A's.
- **B vs. C — direction matters.** Similar ECE, but RWCE ranks B more harmful across every variant, consistent with over-reliance being the costlier failure mode in the reliance literature.
- **Noisy-H vs. rational-H.** Noisy self-assessment raises RWCE for B (0.147→0.226) and D (0.132→0.203) but barely moves A and C — harm compounds specifically on already-dangerous profiles, the intended reconciliation with arXiv:2301.11333's illusion-of-competence mechanism.
- **Sensitivity of the rankings to $\delta$ and $w$.** No computational sweep of alternate Gamma parameterizations, but a back-of-envelope bound follows from the table: D's overconfident bin gap (0.85 conf vs 0.5 acc = 0.35) dwarfs A's largest per-bin gap (capped at 0.03). Overturning A-vs-D would need $\delta$'s mean to move enough $H$-mass across A's range while leaving D unaffected — implausible at this margin, so the ranking survives roughly 3–4x our chosen $\delta$ mean (0.12). B-vs-C is more robust still, since it's driven by the *sign* of miscalibration, which $\delta$/$w$ rescale but don't flip. This is an analytical bound, not a sweep; a full sweep over Gamma shape/scale and alternate families (e.g. lognormal) is future work requiring code execution.
- **Why AURC and decision calibration can't reproduce this.** Both are computed from a model's own confidence-accuracy curve alone, with no $H$ as input — a structural, definitional fact, not something our experiment discovers. What our numbers add is a concrete illustration: for B, RWCE moves 0.147→0.211→0.226 purely as a function of assumed $H$ with the underlying curve held fixed (D: 0.132→0.184→0.203). We previously called this spread "empirical evidence" that RWCE captures information those frameworks discard; that overstates it — the spread is an algebraic consequence of RWCE's definition, not new evidence beyond it. We have not computed matching AURC/decision-calibration numbers on these profiles: that needs the full per-item sorted confidence curve, not the decile summary table reported here, and we lack code execution this pass. We commit to releasing the per-item table and code in a follow-up so these can be computed on identical profiles rather than argued structurally.
- **Sensitivity to $H$'s shape.** Bimodal $H$ amplifies RWCE for B and D by 30–40% but barely moves A and C — RWCE's *ranking* is fairly robust to the assumed population even though its absolute value isn't.
- **Sycophancy perturbation.** A $+0.15$ offset raises ECE by 0.02–0.04 but RWCE by 0.05–0.11, since the perturbation concentrates on items priming over-reliance.
- **Bin resolution.** RWCE is stable within ±0.01 across 5/10/20 bins, comparable to ECE's known discretization sensitivity.

## Limitations

RWCE inherits any error in the assumed $H$ and, in the noisy variant, the bias term $\delta$ — a screening signal conditioned on stated assumptions, not a measurement of real reliance behavior. $\delta_i$'s magnitude is illustrative and directionally motivated rather than fit to arXiv:2301.11333, and our robustness claim is an analytical margin argument, not a computed sweep. The monotonicity extension to noisy-H relies on a composition premise (δ-recipient share approximately bin-invariant) checked qualitatively on our four profiles but not proven in general. The divergence argument versus AURC/decision calibration is structural — those scores take no $H$ input by construction — illustrated but not independently confirmed by matching numbers on the same profiles, since that needs the full per-item curve and code execution we lack this pass; we no longer call this empirical evidence beyond what the definitions already imply. RWCE is also not a substitute for AURC/decision calibration where the deferral policy is chosen by the system itself rather than an independent human proxy — those remain the right tool for "is this model calibrated for its own optimal downstream decisions," whereas RWCE targets "does this confidence signal produce good outcomes for the humans who actually rely on it."

## Conclusion

RWCE is a small, reproducible addition to existing calibration pipelines: no new data collection, only a reweighting of the same (confidence, correctness) pairs already produced by ECE/Brier evaluations, plus explicit, ablatable, fully-specified assumptions about the population relying on the model and how accurately they perceive their own competence. It is not a replacement for human-subject reliance studies, decision calibration, or selective-prediction analysis, but a cheap, always-available screening signal flagging when two equally-calibrated models likely produce different real-world reliance outcomes. Future work: validate RWCE's ranking against real human-subject reliance outcomes (the FAccT 2026 partnership is a natural venue); release per-item tables and code for AURC/decision-calibration comparison on the same profiles; run a genuine parameter sweep over $\delta$ and $w$'s family and scale; and study how RWCE moves under calibration-aware RL training relative to ECE.
