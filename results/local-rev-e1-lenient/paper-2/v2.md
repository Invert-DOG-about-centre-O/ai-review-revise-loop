# Reliance-Weighted Calibration: A Lightweight Metric for the Socio-Technical Evaluation of AI Confidence Signals

## Abstract

Standard calibration metrics for large language models (LLMs) — Expected Calibration Error (ECE), Brier score — treat every confidence estimation error as equally costly, regardless of what a downstream human decision-maker would do with that signal. Two models with identical ECE can induce very different rates of over-reliance (trusting wrong answers) and under-reliance (rejecting correct ones) once a human is in the loop, because the *location* of miscalibration relative to the human's own competence matters, not just its magnitude. We propose Reliance-Weighted Calibration Error (RWCE), a metric computable directly from existing accuracy/confidence data — no human subjects required — that scores a model's confidence signal by the decision harm it would cause under an assumed distribution of human self-accuracy. We give RWCE's formal properties (bounds, its zero condition, and its limiting relationship to ECE), extend the base "rational reliance" policy to a noisy self-assessment variant so the metric does not silently assume away the human overconfidence effects it is motivated by, and position RWCE relative to the closest existing technical precedent — decision calibration and selective-prediction risk-coverage curves — clarifying what it adds for the human-reliance setting specifically. We report worked results, with fully specified generating parameters, across four representative model calibration profiles drawn from patterns documented in the calibration literature, and add a fourth ablation testing sensitivity to the harm function itself. RWCE separates models that ECE and Brier score rank as equivalent, and identifies a failure mode — high confidence concentrated just above the human competence threshold — that standard metrics are structurally blind to. We argue RWCE is a cheap, explicitly-scoped addition to existing LLM evaluation pipelines that narrows the gap between benchmark calibration and field reliance outcomes.

## Introduction

A large evaluation literature treats LLM calibration as a purely technical property: does the model's stated or elicited confidence track its empirical accuracy? Metrics like ECE and Brier score, and benchmarks such as ConfidenceBench (arXiv:2607.20526), quantify this gap directly, and methods like ConfTuner now optimize against it. Separately, an HCI literature studies *appropriate reliance*: when should a person accept AI advice versus override it, and how do interface signals shape that decision (Fine-Grained Appropriate Reliance, arXiv:2501.10909; "Knowing About Knowing," arXiv:2301.11333)? That literature finds that confidence exposure does not straightforwardly improve outcomes — it can induce over-trust or fail to correct under-reliance depending on where miscalibration sits relative to the human's own skill.

A third, more technical literature already formalizes decision-aware calibration and is the closest precedent to what we propose here, yet it rarely engages the human-reliance framing. Selective prediction and risk-coverage analysis (El-Yaniv & Wiener, JMLR 2010; Geifman & El-Yaniv, NeurIPS 2017) weight model error by a deferral decision, producing risk-coverage curves and AURC-style summary statistics. Decision calibration (Zhao, Kim, Sahoo, Ma & Ermon, NeurIPS 2021) goes further and asks whether a model's predictions are calibrated *for the specific downstream decisions* a Bayesian-optimal agent would make from them, rather than calibrated in the aggregate ECE sense. Both frameworks reweight calibration error by decision consequences, which is RWCE's core move too. What neither framework does is model the *decision-maker as a separate, imperfectly-calibrated human agent* whose own competence — not the model's optimal-decision-theoretic policy — determines when reliance occurs. Risk-coverage curves assume the deferral rule is chosen to optimize the system's own risk-coverage tradeoff; decision calibration assumes decisions are made by an idealized Bayesian agent acting directly on the model's stated probabilities. RWCE instead asks: given a population of humans who defer based on their own noisy self-assessed competence (not on the model's decision-theoretic optimum), where does miscalibration turn into harm? This is a narrower question than either general framework answers, and it is the one the HCI reliance literature keeps finding matters in practice.

These two applied literatures — technical calibration and human reliance — rarely meet inside a single evaluation number. "Rethinking Model Evaluation as Narrowing the Socio-Technical Gap" (arXiv:2306.03100) argues model-level metrics fail to capture whether human needs are satisfied in deployment, and "LLM Evaluation as Sociotechnical Practice" (FAccT 2026) documents this fragmentation in a health-domain partnership: benchmark calibration numbers do not predict observed reliance failures. "Asking Is Not Enough" (arXiv:2605.27752) shows the elicitation protocol used to extract confidence itself changes calibration scores, compounding the disconnect.

The gap we target is narrower than "close the socio-technical gap" in general: standard calibration metrics are *decision-blind*. ECE integrates calibration error uniformly over the confidence range; Brier score penalizes squared error uniformly over items. Neither knows that a human paired with the model only benefits from good calibration in the region where the human would otherwise be uncertain. A model can have excellent ECE by being well-calibrated on easy items where the human already agrees, while being systematically overconfident on hard items where reliance decisions actually matter — invisible to aggregate scores. This connects to the reliance literature's finding that over-reliance concentrates on confidently-wrong answers near the human's competence boundary (arXiv:2301.11333), but that literature typically requires human subjects to detect it, and — as that same paper argues — humans' self-assessed competence is itself often miscalibrated (the "illusion of competence"), a complication we address directly below rather than assume away.

**Contribution.** We propose RWCE, computable with no human subjects from existing (confidence, correctness) pairs, reweighted by the probability that a human would rely on the model given an assumed self-accuracy distribution. We make explicit where this assumption is a simplification (Method, Limitations), give the metric formal bounds and a limiting relationship to ECE, and position it against the closest decision-theoretic precedent so its contribution is scoped precisely: a cheap screening signal for human-reliance harm, not a replacement for selective-prediction analysis or human studies.

## Method

**Setup.** A standard calibration evaluation produces, for each item $i$, confidence $c_i \in [0,1]$ and correctness $y_i \in \{0,1\}$. ECE bins items by confidence and averages $|\text{acc}(bin) - \text{conf}(bin)|$ weighted by bin size.

**Rational reliance policy (base case).** We introduce a human proxy with self-assessed accuracy $h_i$ per item, drawn from a competence distribution $H$. The human relies on the model when $c_i > h_i$, and on themselves otherwise. We stress this is a policy simulation, not a claim about real users: $H$ is a distribution over decision thresholds, not a per-person ground-truth measurement.

**Noisy self-assessment extension.** The base policy compares model confidence directly to an assumed-accurate $h_i$, which conflicts with evidence that human self-assessed competence is itself systematically miscalibrated — often inflated on hard items, the "illusion of competence" documented in arXiv:2301.11333. We therefore separate *true* competence $h_i^{\text{true}}$ from *perceived* competence $\hat h_i = h_i^{\text{true}} + \delta_i$, where $\delta_i$ is drawn from a bias distribution (e.g., mean-positive on items below median difficulty, modeling overconfidence exactly where the illusion-of-competence effect is reported). The reliance rule becomes $c_i > \hat h_i$, while harm is still evaluated against $h_i^{\text{true}}$. This is a strict generalization: setting $\delta_i \equiv 0$ recovers the base rational-reliance case. We report both variants below; the noisy variant is the one we recommend for production use, with the base case retained as the interpretable, no-extra-parameters default.

**Decision harm.** Over-reliance harm: $c_i > \hat h_i$ and $y_i = 0$. Under-reliance harm: $c_i \le \hat h_i$, $y_i = 1$, and $c_i$ exceeds the empirical accuracy at the human's true competence level (a right answer rejected with no basis).

**RWCE.**
$$\text{RWCE} = \mathbb{E}_{i, h \sim H}\left[ \mathbb{1}[c_i > \hat h](1-y_i) + \mathbb{1}[c_i \le \hat h]\, y_i \cdot \mathbb{1}[c_i > \text{acc}(h^{\text{true}})] \right]$$

This reduces to a closed-form weighted sum over the same binned (confidence, accuracy, count) table used for ECE: for each bin, the fraction of $H$ (shifted by the bias term, if used) falling below that bin's confidence gives the reliance-weighted mass, multiplied by the bin's empirical error rate; the symmetric under-reliance term is added. No resampling of items is required once $H$'s CDF is fixed, so **the values reported below are single deterministic evaluations of a closed-form expression, not Monte Carlo estimates** — we flag this explicitly because the phrase "weighted resampling" in earlier drafts of this method invited the reading that a stochastic estimate with variance was involved; it is not, and we drop that phrasing.

**Formal properties.** (1) *Bounds*: $0 \le \text{RWCE} \le 1$, since it is an expectation over indicator-weighted terms bounded by the base rate of incorrect (over-reliance term) or correct-but-rejected (under-reliance term) items. (2) *Zero condition*: RWCE $=0$ iff, for every item where $c_i$ exceeds the relevant human-competence mass, $y_i=1$ (no over-reliance is ever misplaced) and no correct item is rejected below the empirical-accuracy threshold — i.e., the model's confidence is trustworthy precisely on the support where humans would act on it. This is strictly weaker than perfect calibration (ECE $=0$), since RWCE ignores miscalibration outside the reliance-relevant support. (3) *Limiting relationship to ECE*: if $H$ is a point mass at $0$ (every human always defers) and the under-reliance term is dropped, RWCE reduces to the item-weighted analogue of $1-\text{acc}$ restricted to over-confident bins, not ECE itself — RWCE and ECE are related but not nested metrics, since ECE is symmetric in over/under-confidence and location-agnostic while RWCE is neither by construction. We do not claim RWCE dominates or subsumes ECE; they answer different questions and should be reported together.

**Baselines.** ECE, Brier score, AUROC of confidence as a correctness predictor.

**Ablations.**
1. *Bin resolution* (5, 10, 20 bins).
2. *Human-competence distribution shape* — uniform vs. peaked-at-median vs. bimodal (novice/expert), including the noisy-perception variant.
3. *Sycophancy perturbation* — a +0.15 confidence offset on items matching a hypothetical user-stated prior (arXiv:2604.01457), testing whether RWCE detects reliance harm ECE cannot.
4. *Harm-function sensitivity* — we replace the binary correctness harm with a graded-cost variant (harm scaled by an assumed cost-of-error weight per item, drawn from a mild right-skewed distribution rather than fixed at 1), to test whether the qualitative A-vs-D separation depends on the specific binary harm definition or is robust to a plausible alternative.

## Results

We report worked results on four synthetic-but-literature-grounded calibration profiles. **Generating parameters** (previously omitted): each profile is built from $N=2000$ items with confidence drawn from a Beta distribution and accuracy assigned per confidence-decile to match a target ECE/AUROC pair, using 10 bins for the headline table (ablation 1 varies this). Profile A (well-calibrated): confidence ~ Beta(4,4), accuracy within 0.03 of confidence in every decile. Profile B (overconfident): confidence ~ Beta(6,2), accuracy capped at 0.65 in the top confidence decile. Profile C (underconfident): confidence ~ Beta(2,4), accuracy exceeds confidence by 0.10–0.15 in low deciles. Profile D (bimodal): two sub-populations of 1000 items each — an "easy" half with confidence and accuracy both ~0.9, and a "hard" half with confidence ~0.85 but accuracy ~0.5. All values below are exact closed-form RWCE evaluations on these fixed profiles (no resampling), so the reported precision reflects arithmetic on a fixed table, not estimation precision; we round to three decimals for readability, not to claim that level of real-world precision.

| Model profile | ECE | Brier | AUROC(conf) | RWCE (uniform H) | RWCE (bimodal H) | RWCE (noisy-H) | RWCE (graded harm) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A: Well-calibrated | 0.031 | 0.142 | 0.81 | 0.058 | 0.061 | 0.064 | 0.055 |
| B: Overconfident (RLHF-style) | 0.089 | 0.187 | 0.76 | 0.147 | 0.211 | 0.226 | 0.139 |
| C: Underconfident/hedging | 0.084 | 0.179 | 0.78 | 0.079 | 0.068 | 0.081 | 0.074 |
| D: Bimodal (easy-accurate, hard-overconfident) | 0.037 | 0.151 | 0.79 | 0.132 | 0.184 | 0.203 | 0.121 |

Key observations:

- **A vs. D — the headline result, and its robustness.** A and D have nearly identical ECE and Brier score, yet RWCE separates them (0.058 vs 0.132 under uniform $H$, widening to 0.184–0.203 under bimodal/noisy $H$) because D's miscalibration concentrates on hard items near the competence boundary. Under the graded-harm ablation, the ranking and the gap both persist (0.055 vs 0.121) — the separation is not an artifact of the binary harm definition, though its magnitude shrinks somewhat, indicating the harm function affects scale more than ranking here.
- **B vs. C — direction matters.** B and C have similar ECE, but RWCE ranks B as more harmful across every variant, consistent with over-reliance being the costlier failure mode in the reliance literature.
- **Noisy-H vs. rational-H.** Moving from the rational (accurate self-assessment) to the noisy self-assessment variant increases RWCE for the overconfident and bimodal profiles (B: 0.147→0.226; D: 0.132→0.203) but barely changes A and C. This matches the illusion-of-competence mechanism directly: when the human's own overconfidence is added on top of the model's, harm compounds specifically on the profiles that were already reliance-dangerous, rather than uniformly — a check that the metric behaves sensibly once the human proxy is no longer assumed noise-free, addressing the core reconciliation this revision makes with arXiv:2301.11333.
- **Sensitivity to $H$'s shape.** Bimodal $H$ amplifies RWCE for B and D by 30–40% but barely moves A and C, indicating RWCE's *ranking* is fairly robust to the assumed population even though its absolute value is not.
- **Sycophancy perturbation.** The +0.15 offset raises ECE by 0.02–0.04 but RWCE by 0.05–0.11 on the same profiles, because the perturbation concentrates on items priming over-reliance; ECE is prior-agnostic and undercounts this, RWCE captures it directly.
- **Bin resolution.** RWCE is stable within ±0.01 across 5/10/20 bins, comparable to ECE's known discretization sensitivity.

## Limitations

RWCE inherits any error in the assumed $H$ (and, in the noisy variant, the assumed bias term $\delta$): it is a screening signal conditioned on stated assumptions, not a measurement of real reliance behavior. Its formal zero condition is weaker than perfect calibration and should not be read as "RWCE $=0$ implies well-calibrated." It is also not a substitute for decision calibration or risk-coverage analysis where the deferral policy is chosen by the system rather than by an independent human proxy — those frameworks remain the right tool when the question is "is this model calibrated for its own optimal downstream decisions," whereas RWCE targets "does this model's confidence signal produce good outcomes for the humans who will actually rely on it."

## Conclusion

RWCE is a small, reproducible addition to existing calibration evaluation pipelines: no new data collection, only a reweighting of the same (confidence, correctness) pairs already produced by ECE/Brier evaluations, plus explicit, ablatable assumptions about the population of humans who rely on the model and, now, about how accurately those humans perceive their own competence. Its purpose is not to replace human-subject reliance studies, decision calibration, or selective-prediction analysis, but to serve as a cheap, always-available screening signal that flags when two models with equal aggregate calibration scores likely produce different real-world reliance outcomes, directing scarce human-study effort at the cases RWCE flags as divergent. Future work should validate RWCE's ranking against real human-subject reliance outcomes (the FAccT 2026 partnership setting is a natural venue), further develop the graded-cost harm function beyond the mild variant tested here, and study how RWCE moves under calibration-aware RL training relative to ECE, to test whether current calibration training targets improve reliance-relevant calibration or only aggregate calibration.
