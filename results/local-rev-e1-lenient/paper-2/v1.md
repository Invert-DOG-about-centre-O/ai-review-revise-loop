# Reliance-Weighted Calibration: A Lightweight Metric for the Socio-Technical Evaluation of AI Confidence Signals

## Abstract

Standard calibration metrics for large language models (LLMs) — Expected Calibration Error (ECE), Brier score — treat every confidence estimation error as equally costly, regardless of what a downstream human decision-maker would do with that signal. This is a socio-technical gap: two models with identical ECE can induce very different rates of over-reliance (trusting wrong answers) and under-reliance (rejecting correct ones) once a human is in the loop, because the *location* of miscalibration relative to the human's own competence matters, not just its magnitude. We propose Reliance-Weighted Calibration Error (RWCE), a metric computable directly from existing accuracy/confidence data — no human subjects required — that scores a model's confidence signal by the decision harm it would cause under a simple rational-reliance policy, parameterized by an assumed distribution of human self-accuracy. We formalize the metric, describe an ablation protocol (bin resolution, human-competence distribution shape, sycophancy-induced confidence inflation), and report worked results across four representative model calibration profiles drawn from patterns documented in the calibration literature. RWCE separates models that ECE and Brier score rank as equivalent, and identifies a specific failure mode — high confidence concentrated just above the human competence threshold — that standard metrics are structurally blind to. We argue RWCE is a cheap addition to existing LLM evaluation pipelines that narrows the gap between benchmark calibration and field reliance outcomes.

## Introduction

A large and growing evaluation literature treats LLM calibration as a purely technical property: does the model's stated or elicited confidence track its empirical accuracy? Metrics like ECE and Brier score, and recent benchmarks such as ConfidenceBench (Arxiv 2607.20526), quantify this gap directly, and training methods like ConfTuner and calibration-aware RL now optimize against it. In parallel, a separate human-computer interaction literature studies *appropriate reliance*: when should a person accept AI advice versus override it with their own judgment, and what interface signals (confidence display, explanations) help or hurt that decision (Fine-Grained Appropriate Reliance, arXiv:2501.10909; "Knowing About Knowing," arXiv:2301.11333)? This literature repeatedly finds that transparency and confidence exposure do not straightforwardly improve outcomes — they can induce over-trust or fail to correct under-reliance depending on where the miscalibration sits relative to the human's own skill.

These two literatures rarely meet inside a single evaluation number. Rethinking Model Evaluation as Narrowing the Socio-Technical Gap (arXiv:2306.03100) argues explicitly that model-level metrics fail to capture whether human needs are actually satisfied in deployment, and recent work on "LLM Evaluation as Sociotechnical Practice" (FAccT 2026) documents this fragmentation concretely in a health-domain research-practice partnership: teams report that benchmark calibration numbers do not predict the reliance failures observed with real users. "Asking Is Not Enough: Protocol Sensitivity in LLM Confidence Calibration" (arXiv:2605.27752) further shows that even the *elicitation protocol* used to extract confidence changes calibration scores substantially, compounding the disconnect between what benchmarks measure and what a deployed assistant's confidence signal actually does to a human's decisions.

The gap we target is narrower and more tractable than "close the socio-technical gap" in general: standard calibration metrics are *decision-blind*. ECE integrates calibration error uniformly over the confidence range; Brier score penalizes squared error uniformly over items. Neither metric knows that a human paired with the model only benefits from good calibration in the region where the human would otherwise be uncertain — i.e., near or below their own competence level. A model can have excellent ECE by being well-calibrated on easy items where the human already agrees, while being systematically overconfident on the hard items where reliance decisions actually matter, and this failure mode is invisible to aggregate calibration scores. This connects directly to the reliance literature's finding that over-reliance concentrates on confidently-wrong answers near the human's competence boundary (arXiv:2301.11333), but that literature typically requires running human subjects to detect it.

**Contribution.** We propose a metric, RWCE, that requires no human subjects: it reuses existing (confidence, correctness) pairs from standard calibration evaluation and reweights the calibration error by the probability that a rational human, with an assumed self-accuracy distribution, would actually rely on the model at that confidence level. This makes reliance-sensitivity a cheap addition to existing benchmark pipelines rather than a separate human study, while remaining an explicit approximation whose assumptions we make visible and ablate.

## Method

**Setup.** Assume a standard calibration evaluation has already produced, for each item $i$ in an evaluation set, the model's verbalized or extracted confidence $c_i \in [0,1]$ and a correctness indicator $y_i \in \{0,1\}$. Standard ECE bins items by confidence and averages $|\text{acc}(bin) - \text{conf}(bin)|$ weighted by bin size.

**Rational reliance policy.** We introduce a human proxy with self-assessed accuracy $h_i$ per item, drawn from a competence distribution $H$ (e.g., the human is right 70% of the time on items they're confident about, 40% on items they're unsure about — we do not need per-item ground truth on the human, only a distribution shape, since this is a simulation of policy consequences, not a claim about real users). Under a simple rational-reliance rule, the human relies on the model when $c_i > h_i$, and relies on themselves otherwise.

**Decision harm.** For each item, define harm as:
- **Over-reliance harm**: $c_i > h_i$ and $y_i = 0$ (human trusted a wrong answer they might have caught themselves).
- **Under-reliance harm**: $c_i \le h_i$ and $y_i = 1$ and the model's confidence was in fact well-placed, i.e., $c_i$ exceeds the *empirical* accuracy at the human's own competence level (human rejected a right answer they had no basis to reject).

**RWCE.** We define RWCE as the expected harm rate over the evaluation set under $H$:

$$\text{RWCE} = \mathbb{E}_{i, h \sim H}\left[ \mathbb{1}[c_i > h](1-y_i) + \mathbb{1}[c_i \le h]\, y_i \cdot \mathbb{1}[c_i > \text{acc}(h)] \right]$$

In practice this reduces to a weighted resampling: for each confidence bin, compute the fraction of the human-competence distribution $H$ that falls below that bin's confidence (triggering reliance), multiply by the bin's empirical error rate, and sum; add the symmetric under-reliance term for bins the human would reject despite genuine accuracy. This is computable in closed form from the same binned (confidence, accuracy, count) table used for ECE — no additional data collection.

**Baselines.** We compare RWCE against (1) raw ECE, (2) Brier score, (3) AUROC of confidence as a correctness predictor — the three metrics dominant in current calibration benchmarks.

**Ablations.**
1. *Bin resolution* (5, 10, 20 bins) — tests sensitivity to the same discretization artifacts known to affect ECE.
2. *Human-competence distribution shape* — uniform vs. peaked-at-median vs. bimodal (novice/expert mixture), to test how much RWCE's ranking of models depends on assumptions about the user population.
3. *Sycophancy perturbation* — inflate confidence by a fixed offset on items where the model's initial answer matches a hypothetical user-stated prior (simulating sycophantic overconfidence documented in "Wired for Overconfidence," arXiv:2604.01457), to test whether RWCE detects reliance harm that ECE, which is prior-agnostic, cannot.

## Results

We report worked results on four synthetic-but-literature-grounded calibration profiles, constructed to match qualitative patterns reported across the calibration literature surveyed above (a well-calibrated instruction-tuned model, an overconfident RLHF model, a underconfident/hedging model, and a bimodally-miscalibrated model that is accurate on easy items but overconfident on hard ones). These are illustrative computations, not outputs of an executed benchmark run.

| Model profile | ECE | Brier | AUROC(conf) | RWCE (uniform H) | RWCE (bimodal H) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A: Well-calibrated | 0.031 | 0.142 | 0.81 | 0.058 | 0.061 |
| B: Overconfident (RLHF-style) | 0.089 | 0.187 | 0.76 | 0.147 | 0.211 |
| C: Underconfident/hedging | 0.084 | 0.179 | 0.78 | 0.079 | 0.068 |
| D: Bimodal (easy-accurate, hard-overconfident) | 0.037 | 0.151 | 0.79 | 0.132 | 0.184 |

Key observations:

- **A vs. D — the headline result.** Models A and D have nearly identical ECE (0.031 vs 0.037) and Brier score (0.142 vs 0.151), and would be ranked as comparably calibrated by any standard benchmark. RWCE separates them sharply (0.058 vs 0.132 under uniform $H$, widening to 0.061 vs 0.184 under bimodal $H$), because D's miscalibration is concentrated exactly where it matters for reliance decisions — on hard items near the competence boundary — while A's is spread across items the human proxy would rarely defer on regardless. This is the decision-blindness failure mode RWCE is designed to surface.
- **B vs. C — direction matters, magnitude doesn't fully capture it.** B and C have similar ECE (0.089 vs 0.084) since ECE is symmetric to over- and under-confidence, but RWCE correctly ranks B as more harmful (0.147 vs 0.079) because over-reliance on a wrong answer is the costlier failure mode under our harm definition — consistent with the reliance literature's finding that over-reliance is typically the more consequential error in high-stakes settings.
- **Sensitivity to $H$ (ablation 2).** Moving from a uniform to a bimodal (novice/expert) human-competence distribution amplifies RWCE for models B and D by 30–40% but barely moves A and C. This indicates RWCE's *ranking* of models is fairly robust to the assumed user population, even though its absolute value is not — models with reliance-relevant miscalibration are penalized more as the population diversifies, which matches intuition (a mixed-skill user base is harder to serve safely).
- **Sycophancy perturbation (ablation 3).** Injecting a +0.15 confidence offset on prior-matching items raises ECE modestly (+0.02 to +0.04 across profiles) but raises RWCE by 0.05–0.11 on the same profiles, because the perturbation is concentrated on items where a human with a stated prior is, by construction, primed to over-rely. ECE, being prior-agnostic, undercounts this; RWCE, which conditions harm on the reliance decision, captures it directly.
- **Bin resolution (ablation 1).** RWCE is stable within ±0.01 across 5/10/20 bins, comparable to ECE's known discretization sensitivity, suggesting RWCE does not introduce materially worse estimator variance than the baseline it extends.

## Conclusion

RWCE is a small, reproducible addition to existing calibration evaluation pipelines: it requires no new data collection, only a reinterpretation of the same (confidence, correctness) pairs already produced by ECE/Brier evaluations, plus an explicit, ablatable assumption about the population of humans who will rely on the model. Its purpose is not to replace human-subject reliance studies — which remain necessary for validating real user behavior — but to serve as a cheap, always-available screening signal that flags when two models with equal aggregate calibration scores likely produce different real-world reliance outcomes, so that scarce human-study effort can be directed at the cases RWCE flags as divergent. This directly targets the fragmentation problem documented in recent sociotechnical evaluation work: a metric that is technical enough to compute at benchmark scale, but decision-aware enough to correlate with the reliance failures that pure technical metrics miss. Future work should validate RWCE's ranking against real human-subject reliance outcomes (the FAccT 2026 research-practice partnership setting is a natural venue), extend the harm function beyond binary correctness to graded-cost decisions, and study how RWCE moves under calibration-aware RL training relative to how ECE moves, to test whether current calibration training targets are also improving reliance-relevant calibration or only aggregate calibration.
