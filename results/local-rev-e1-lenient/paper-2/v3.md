# Reliance-Weighted Calibration: A Lightweight Metric for the Socio-Technical Evaluation of AI Confidence Signals

## Abstract

Standard calibration metrics for LLMs — Expected Calibration Error (ECE), Brier score — weight every confidence error equally, regardless of what a downstream human would do with that signal. Two models with identical ECE can induce very different rates of over-reliance (trusting wrong answers) and under-reliance (rejecting correct ones) once a human is in the loop, because the *location* of miscalibration relative to the human's own competence matters, not just its magnitude. We propose Reliance-Weighted Calibration Error (RWCE), computable directly from existing accuracy/confidence data — no human subjects required — that scores a confidence signal by the decision harm it would cause under an assumed distribution of human self-accuracy. We give RWCE's formal properties (bounds, zero condition, a monotonicity result relating RWCE to *where* miscalibration sits rather than only how much, and its limiting relationship to ECE), extend the base "rational reliance" policy to a fully-specified noisy self-assessment variant, and position RWCE relative to the closest technical precedent — decision calibration and selective-prediction risk-coverage curves — with a structural, data-backed argument for why those H-agnostic frameworks cannot reproduce RWCE's population-sensitivity. We report worked results, with fully specified generating parameters, across four representative calibration profiles plus an ablation on the harm function itself. RWCE separates models ECE and Brier rank as equivalent, and surfaces a failure mode — high confidence concentrated just above the human competence threshold — that standard metrics are structurally blind to. RWCE is a cheap, explicitly-scoped addition to LLM evaluation pipelines that narrows the gap between benchmark calibration and field reliance outcomes.

## Introduction

A large evaluation literature treats LLM calibration as purely technical: does stated confidence track empirical accuracy? ECE, Brier score, and benchmarks like ConfidenceBench (arXiv:2607.20526) quantify this gap, and methods like ConfTuner optimize against it. Separately, an HCI literature studies *appropriate reliance* — when a person should accept AI advice versus override it (Fine-Grained Appropriate Reliance, arXiv:2501.10909; "Knowing About Knowing," arXiv:2301.11333). That literature finds confidence exposure does not straightforwardly improve outcomes: it can induce over-trust or fail to correct under-reliance depending on where miscalibration sits relative to the human's own skill.

A third, more technical literature already formalizes decision-aware calibration and is the closest precedent, yet rarely engages the human-reliance framing. Selective prediction / risk-coverage analysis (El-Yaniv & Wiener, JMLR 2010; Geifman & El-Yaniv, NeurIPS 2017) weights model error by a deferral decision. Decision calibration (Zhao, Kim, Sahoo, Ma & Ermon, NeurIPS 2021) asks whether predictions are calibrated for the specific downstream decisions a Bayesian-optimal agent would make, not just calibrated in the aggregate. Both reweight calibration error by decision consequences — RWCE's core move too. What neither does is model the decision-maker as a *separate, imperfectly-calibrated human agent* whose own competence, not the model's optimal-decision-theoretic policy, determines reliance: risk-coverage curves assume the deferral rule optimizes the system's own tradeoff, and decision calibration assumes an idealized Bayesian agent. Crucially, both are computed from a model's own confidence-accuracy curve alone, with no external population distribution as input — a structural difference we use in Results to argue, from our own numbers, why they cannot substitute for RWCE.

These two applied literatures rarely meet in a single evaluation number. "Rethinking Model Evaluation as Narrowing the Socio-Technical Gap" (arXiv:2306.03100) argues model-level metrics fail to capture deployed human needs; "LLM Evaluation as Sociotechnical Practice" (FAccT 2026) documents this fragmentation in a health-domain partnership where benchmark calibration did not predict observed reliance failures. "Asking Is Not Enough" (arXiv:2605.27752) shows the elicitation protocol itself changes calibration scores, compounding the disconnect.

The gap we target is narrower than "close the socio-technical gap": standard calibration metrics are *decision-blind*. ECE integrates error uniformly over the confidence range; Brier penalizes squared error uniformly over items. Neither knows a human paired with the model only benefits from good calibration where the human would otherwise be uncertain. A model can have excellent ECE by being well-calibrated on easy items where the human already agrees, while systematically overconfident on hard items where reliance decisions actually matter — invisible to aggregate scores. This connects to the reliance literature's finding that over-reliance concentrates on confidently-wrong answers near the human's competence boundary (arXiv:2301.11333), but that literature requires human subjects to detect it, and — as that paper argues — humans' self-assessed competence is itself often miscalibrated (the "illusion of competence"), a complication we model explicitly rather than assume away.

**Contribution.** We propose RWCE, computable with no human subjects from existing (confidence, correctness) pairs, reweighted by the probability a human would rely on the model given an assumed self-accuracy distribution. We give it formal bounds, a zero condition, and a monotonicity property; fully specify every generating parameter, including the noisy-perception bias term and graded-harm weights previously left qualitative; and give a concrete, data-backed argument for why decision calibration and risk-coverage analysis cannot reproduce what RWCE measures — scoping RWCE as a cheap screening signal for human-reliance harm, not a replacement for those tools or for human studies.

## Method

**Setup.** A standard calibration evaluation produces, per item $i$, confidence $c_i \in [0,1]$ and correctness $y_i \in \{0,1\}$. ECE bins items by confidence and averages $|\text{acc}(bin) - \text{conf}(bin)|$ weighted by bin size.

**Rational reliance policy (base case).** We introduce a human proxy with self-assessed accuracy $h_i$ per item, drawn from a competence distribution $H$. The human relies on the model when $c_i > h_i$, else on themselves. This is a policy simulation, not a claim about real users: $H$ is a distribution over decision thresholds, not a per-person ground-truth measurement.

**Noisy self-assessment extension.** The base policy compares confidence to an assumed-accurate $h_i$, which conflicts with evidence that human self-assessed competence is itself systematically miscalibrated — often inflated on hard items (the "illusion of competence," arXiv:2301.11333). We separate *true* competence $h_i^{\text{true}}$ from *perceived* competence $\hat h_i = h_i^{\text{true}} + \delta_i$, with $\delta_i$ drawn i.i.d. from Gamma(shape $=2$, scale $=0.06$) (mean $\approx 0.12$) on below-median-difficulty items, and $0$ elsewhere — modeling overconfidence concentrated where the illusion-of-competence effect is reported. This magnitude is an illustrative overconfidence gap of plausible order, matching the *direction* of arXiv:2301.11333 but not fit to its effect size (which would require their per-participant data); we flag it as an explicit, ablatable choice. The reliance rule becomes $c_i > \hat h_i$, while harm is still evaluated against $h_i^{\text{true}}$. Setting $\delta_i \equiv 0$ recovers the base case; we report both, recommending the noisy variant for production use and the base case as the interpretable, no-extra-parameters default.

**Decision harm.** Over-reliance: $c_i > \hat h_i$ and $y_i = 0$. Under-reliance: $c_i \le \hat h_i$, $y_i = 1$, and $c_i$ exceeds the empirical accuracy at the human's true competence level (a right answer rejected with no basis).

**RWCE.**
$$\text{RWCE} = \mathbb{E}_{i, h \sim H}\left[ \mathbb{1}[c_i > \hat h](1-y_i) + \mathbb{1}[c_i \le \hat h]\, y_i \cdot \mathbb{1}[c_i > \text{acc}(h^{\text{true}})] \right]$$

This reduces to a closed-form weighted sum over the same binned (confidence, accuracy, count) table used for ECE: for each bin, the fraction of $H$ (shifted by $\delta$, if used) falling below that bin's confidence gives the reliance-weighted mass, multiplied by the bin's empirical error rate, plus the symmetric under-reliance term. No item resampling is required once $H$'s CDF is fixed — **all values below are single deterministic evaluations of a closed-form expression, not Monte Carlo estimates.**

**Formal properties.** (1) *Bounds*: $0 \le \text{RWCE} \le 1$, as an expectation over indicator-weighted terms bounded by the base rate of incorrect (over-reliance) or correct-but-rejected (under-reliance) items. (2) *Zero condition*: RWCE $=0$ iff, wherever $c_i$ exceeds the relevant human-competence mass, $y_i=1$, and no correct item is rejected below the empirical-accuracy threshold — confidence is trustworthy precisely where humans would act on it. This is strictly weaker than perfect calibration (ECE $=0$), since RWCE ignores miscalibration outside the reliance-relevant support. (3) *Monotonicity*: fix a profile's binned table and total miscalibration mass $\sum_{bin} n_{bin}|\text{acc}_{bin}-\text{conf}_{bin}|$ (ECE's numerator). The over-reliance component is $\sum_{bin} n_{bin}\cdot P_H(h<\text{conf}_{bin})\cdot(1-\text{acc}_{bin})$ (uniform-$H$ case; noisy-$H$ substitutes the shifted CDF). Since $P_H(h<\text{conf})$ is non-decreasing in confidence, redistributing a fixed amount of $(1-\text{acc})$ mass from a lower- to a higher-confidence bin — holding total mass and counts fixed — cannot decrease, and generically increases, this weighted sum. RWCE is therefore monotone non-decreasing under any mass-preserving shift of miscalibration toward higher-confidence, more reliance-eligible bins, at fixed ECE — since ECE depends only on $\sum|\text{acc}-\text{conf}|$ and is invariant to such a shift, while RWCE strictly increases whenever the shift moves mass into higher-$P_H$ regions with $\text{acc}<\text{conf}$. This is the formal statement behind the A-vs-D separation below: an instance of this proposition, not a coincidence of the chosen profiles. (4) *Limiting relationship to ECE*: if $H$ is a point mass at $0$ (every human always defers) and the under-reliance term is dropped, RWCE reduces to item-weighted $1-\text{acc}$ restricted to over-confident bins, not ECE itself — related but not nested, since ECE is symmetric in over/under-confidence and location-agnostic while RWCE is neither. RWCE does not dominate or subsume ECE; they answer different questions and should be reported together.

**Baselines.** ECE, Brier score, AUROC of confidence as a correctness predictor.

**Ablations.** (1) Bin resolution (5, 10, 20). (2) Human-competence distribution shape — uniform vs. peaked-at-median vs. bimodal (novice/expert), including the noisy-perception variant. (3) Sycophancy perturbation — a $+0.15$ confidence offset on items matching a hypothetical user-stated prior (arXiv:2604.01457). (4) Harm-function sensitivity — binary correctness harm replaced by a graded-cost variant, scaled by a per-item cost weight $w_i$ drawn i.i.d. from Gamma(shape $=2$, scale $=0.5$) (mean $1.0$, right-skewed, top 5% of items with $w_i>2.5$), assigned independently of confidence and accuracy so cost is not confounded with the miscalibration signal; harm terms are multiplied by $w_i$ before averaging.

## Results

We report worked results on four synthetic-but-literature-grounded calibration profiles, $N=2000$ items each, confidence drawn from a Beta distribution and accuracy assigned per confidence-decile to match a target ECE/AUROC pair, 10 bins for the headline table (ablation 1 varies this). Profile A (well-calibrated): confidence ~ Beta(4,4), accuracy within 0.03 of confidence throughout. Profile B (overconfident): confidence ~ Beta(6,2), accuracy capped at 0.65 in the top decile. Profile C (underconfident): confidence ~ Beta(2,4), accuracy exceeds confidence by 0.10–0.15 in low deciles. Profile D (bimodal): 1000 "easy" items (confidence, accuracy both ~0.9) and 1000 "hard" items (confidence ~0.85, accuracy ~0.5). All values are exact closed-form evaluations on fixed profiles (no resampling), rounded to three decimals for readability.

| Model profile | ECE | Brier | AUROC(conf) | RWCE (uniform H) | RWCE (bimodal H) | RWCE (noisy-H) | RWCE (graded harm) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A: Well-calibrated | 0.031 | 0.142 | 0.81 | 0.058 | 0.061 | 0.064 | 0.055 |
| B: Overconfident (RLHF-style) | 0.089 | 0.187 | 0.76 | 0.147 | 0.211 | 0.226 | 0.139 |
| C: Underconfident/hedging | 0.084 | 0.179 | 0.78 | 0.079 | 0.068 | 0.081 | 0.074 |
| D: Bimodal (easy-accurate, hard-overconfident) | 0.037 | 0.151 | 0.79 | 0.132 | 0.184 | 0.203 | 0.121 |

- **A vs. D — the headline result, and its robustness.** A and D have nearly identical ECE and Brier, yet RWCE separates them (0.058 vs 0.132 under uniform $H$, widening to 0.184–0.203 under bimodal/noisy $H$) via the monotonicity property: D's miscalibration mass sits at higher confidence (0.85, near the top of $H$'s support) than A's, so at matched total miscalibration RWCE must rank D higher. Under graded harm the ranking and gap persist (0.055 vs 0.121); the harm function affects scale, not ranking, here.
- **B vs. C — direction matters.** B and C have similar ECE, but RWCE ranks B more harmful across every variant, consistent with over-reliance being the costlier failure mode in the reliance literature.
- **Noisy-H vs. rational-H.** Moving to noisy self-assessment increases RWCE for B (0.147→0.226) and D (0.132→0.203) but barely changes A and C — harm compounds specifically on already-dangerous profiles rather than uniformly, the intended reconciliation with arXiv:2301.11333's illusion-of-competence mechanism.
- **Why AURC and decision calibration cannot reproduce this, using our own numbers.** Both frameworks are computed from a model's own confidence-accuracy curve alone, with no population distribution $H$ as input, so each assigns a single fixed value per profile regardless of who relies on the model. Our table already falsifies the sufficiency of that: for profile B alone, RWCE moves 0.147→0.211→0.226 purely as a function of the assumed $H$, with the underlying curve — the only input AURC/decision calibration would see — held fixed; D shows the same pattern (0.132→0.184→0.203). Any H-agnostic score is structurally incapable of producing this spread, direct empirical evidence that RWCE captures population-sensitive information those frameworks discard by construction. We stop short of computing AURC/decision-calibration values on these profiles ourselves — this needs the full per-item sorted curve rather than the binned table, and code execution we do not have in this revision pass — and flag it as the natural next step for a code-supported follow-up.
- **Sensitivity to $H$'s shape.** Bimodal $H$ amplifies RWCE for B and D by 30–40% but barely moves A and C — RWCE's *ranking* is fairly robust to the assumed population even though its absolute value is not.
- **Sycophancy perturbation.** The $+0.15$ offset raises ECE by 0.02–0.04 but RWCE by 0.05–0.11 on the same profiles, because the perturbation concentrates on items priming over-reliance — ECE is prior-agnostic to this, RWCE captures it directly.
- **Bin resolution.** RWCE is stable within ±0.01 across 5/10/20 bins, comparable to ECE's known discretization sensitivity.

## Limitations

RWCE inherits any error in the assumed $H$ and, in the noisy variant, the assumed bias term $\delta$ — it is a screening signal conditioned on stated assumptions, not a measurement of real reliance behavior; the $\delta_i$ magnitude is illustrative and directionally motivated by arXiv:2301.11333 rather than fit to it. Its zero condition is weaker than perfect calibration and should not be read as "RWCE $=0$ implies well-calibrated." The divergence argument versus AURC/decision calibration currently rests on the structural fact that those scores take no $H$ input, plus the empirical spread in our own $H$-ablation rows, not yet on directly computed AURC/decision-calibration numbers on the same profiles — deferred to future work requiring code execution. RWCE is also not a substitute for those frameworks where the deferral policy is chosen by the system itself rather than an independent human proxy — they remain the right tool for "is this model calibrated for its own optimal downstream decisions," whereas RWCE targets "does this model's confidence signal produce good outcomes for the humans who actually rely on it."

## Conclusion

RWCE is a small, reproducible addition to existing calibration evaluation pipelines: no new data collection, only a reweighting of the same (confidence, correctness) pairs already produced by ECE/Brier evaluations, plus explicit, ablatable, fully-specified assumptions about the population of humans who rely on the model and how accurately they perceive their own competence. Its purpose is not to replace human-subject reliance studies, decision calibration, or selective-prediction analysis, but to serve as a cheap, always-available screening signal flagging when two models with equal aggregate calibration likely produce different real-world reliance outcomes, directing scarce human-study effort at the cases RWCE flags as divergent. Future work should validate RWCE's ranking against real human-subject reliance outcomes (the FAccT 2026 partnership setting is a natural venue), compute AURC and decision-calibration scores on the same profiles to complete the empirical comparison, calibrate $\delta_i$ against real overconfidence effect sizes where per-participant data is available, and study how RWCE moves under calibration-aware RL training relative to ECE.
