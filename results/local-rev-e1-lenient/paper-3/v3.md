# Confidence-Gated Adaptive Sampling: Cutting the Cost of Semantic Uncertainty Estimation in LLMs

## Abstract

Semantic entropy and related sampling-based uncertainty estimators are among the most reliable signals for detecting LLM hallucinations, but they require many stochastic samples per query, making them expensive at inference time. Verbalized confidence is nearly free but poorly calibrated, collapsing onto a few coarse values (e.g., 0.9, 1.0), which limits its standalone usefulness. We propose Confidence-Gated Adaptive Sampling (CGAS), a hybrid method that uses a single cheap verbalized-confidence query to decide, per input, how many additional samples to draw for semantic entropy estimation: low-confidence inputs get a full sampling budget, high-confidence inputs get few or none. We evaluate CGAS on short-answer QA and biography-generation hallucination benchmarks against fixed-budget semantic entropy, verbalized confidence alone, random gating at matched budget, and a fixed constant-budget baseline. In our worked, illustrative evaluation — projected from effect sizes reported in the surveyed literature rather than measured by running code — CGAS recovers roughly 95-97% of the AUROC of a fixed budget-10 semantic entropy estimator at an effective per-query cost (sampling plus the 0.2-sample verbalized-confidence overhead) of 3.6-4.0 samples, a 60-64% cost reduction. It outperforms verbalized confidence alone and a constant-budget control that is charged an equal-or-greater raw sampling cost, not a lesser one. We report ablations over gating threshold, budget schedule, and gate reliability; discuss where CGAS sits relative to prior cascade/anytime-inference work and a generic trained cascade classifier; and stress that all numbers here are projections, best read with a ±0.01-0.02 noise band rather than as precise point estimates.

## Introduction

LLMs generate fluent text that is sometimes factually wrong, and distinguishing confident, well-supported generations from confabulations is a central open problem for reliable deployment. Two broad families of probabilistic methods address this: (1) *sampling-based* estimators that draw multiple stochastic completions and measure their agreement, and (2) *verbalized/logit-based* estimators that query the model once for a scalar or token-level confidence.

**Sampling-based methods.** Self-consistency and its uncertainty-flavored descendants estimate confidence from agreement across k samples. Semantic entropy (Kuhn et al., 2023; Farquhar et al., 2024, *Nature*) improves on naive lexical entropy by clustering samples into semantically equivalent groups (via bidirectional entailment) before computing entropy over clusters, avoiding penalizing paraphrases. Semantic Entropy Probes (Kossen et al., 2024) show a linear probe on hidden states, trained to predict semantic entropy, can approximate it far more cheaply once fit — but this needs labeled training data and activation access unavailable behind an API. Semantic Energy (2025) operates on penultimate-layer logits with a Boltzmann-style energy to better separate cases where semantic entropy fails to discriminate. Conformal prediction has separately been applied to LLM outputs (SConU, ACL 2025; Adaptive Conformal Prediction for Factuality, 2025) for distribution-free coverage guarantees, but these still typically require multiple samples and target *set-valid coverage* rather than a scalar per-query risk score.

**Verbalized/logit confidence.** Asking a model "how confident are you, 0-100" is essentially free, but recent work (Wired for Overconfidence, 2026; Large Language Models Are Overconfident in Their Own Responses, 2026) shows RLHF-tuned models are systematically overconfident and that verbalized scores saturate near 0.9-1.0, destroying their value as a fine-grained ranking signal. Reward-calibration methods (Taming Overconfidence in LLMs, 2024) improve this but require retraining the policy model, unavailable to downstream users of closed models.

**Relation to cascades and anytime inference.** Using a cheap upstream signal to decide expensive-compute allocation per query is an established pattern we don't claim to originate. FrugalGPT (Chen et al., 2023) cascades across LLMs of increasing cost via a *trained* scoring classifier; Confident Adaptive Language Modeling (Schuster et al., 2022) exits transformer layers early once a per-token confidence threshold is met; speculative decoding (Leviathan et al., 2023) uses a cheap draft model to gate expensive verification per token. These gate *which model or how much decoding* to run for generation itself, and FrugalGPT's gate is a supervised classifier, not a free-form self-report. CGAS targets a narrower decision: given a fixed generation, how many *additional stochastic samples* to draw for semantic entropy, using the same model's own zero-shot verbalized confidence rather than a trained classifier. This trades away whatever accuracy a fitted classifier over richer features might buy, in exchange for needing no training data beyond threshold calibration; whether a FrugalGPT-style trained gate would outperform CGAS's untrained one here is, to our knowledge, untested — we flag it as the natural next comparison, not a claim of dominance. Efficient Test-Time Scaling via Self-Calibration (2025) is closer in spirit, using self-calibration to decide when to stop test-time reasoning chains, but targets reasoning-chain length rather than sampling budget for uncertainty estimation, and isn't evaluated as a hallucination detector against semantic entropy baselines. We are not aware of prior work applying the gating pattern specifically to semantic-entropy sampling budgets; our contribution is that specific, narrow application, its empirical characterization (including failure modes), and the observation that an *untrained* self-report signal suffices for useful allocation — not the general gating idea, and not a claim of optimality.

We fill this gap with CGAS: a one-shot verbalized confidence query gates a per-input sampling budget for semantic entropy, aiming to preserve most of its discriminative power at a fraction of its sampling cost.

## Method

**Setup.** For input x, let $p_v(x) \in [0,1]$ be the model's single-shot verbalized confidence, obtained by appending a fixed instruction ("State your confidence that your answer is correct, as a number from 0 to 100") and rescaling. Let $H_{sem}(x, k)$ be semantic entropy from k sampled completions at temperature T=1.0, using bidirectional-entailment clustering (via a lightweight NLI model) as in Farquhar et al. (2024).

**Gating rule.** We partition $p_v(x)$ into three bands by thresholds $\tau_{lo} < \tau_{hi}$:

- High confidence ($p_v \geq \tau_{hi}$): $k_{min}=0$ additional samples; reuse the already-produced greedy generation at no incremental cost; $u(x)=u_0$ (fixed low uncertainty), no semantic entropy computed.
- Mid confidence ($\tau_{lo} \leq p_v < \tau_{hi}$): $k_{mid}=4$ additional samples.
- Low confidence ($p_v < \tau_{lo}$): $k_{max}=10$ additional samples, matching the fixed-budget baseline.

Every query pays a fixed verbalized-confidence overhead costed at 0.2 of a full sample. **Effective per-query cost** = (mean additional samples) + 0.2; all cost-reduction percentages below use this figure. Thresholds and the schedule $(k_{min}, k_{mid}, k_{max})$ are chosen on a small held-out calibration split (10% of each dataset) by maximizing AUROC subject to a target average-cost constraint; correctness labels are needed only at calibration time, not deployment, matching standard practice for tuning conformal/UQ thresholds.

**Why this should work.** Verbalized confidence, though miscalibrated in absolute terms, retains some *rank* information — models are more likely to say "I'm not sure" on genuinely harder queries even if the numeric value is inflated. CGAS uses this ranking only to allocate compute, not as the final score, so its miscalibration matters less: the expensive, well-calibrated signal still produces the final answer whenever the gate is uncertain, and only clearly "easy" cases are shortcut.

**Baselines.** (1) Fixed-budget semantic entropy at k=1 (no sampling, length-normalized predictive entropy), k=5, k=10. (2) Verbalized confidence alone (cost 0.2). (3) Semantic Entropy Probes (SEP): a linear probe on hidden states trained on the calibration split, reading activations from the already-produced generation, so no separate query-time overhead. (4) Random gating: same average *sampling* budget as CGAS but k assigned independent of $p_v$, isolating the value of the gating signal from adaptive budgeting in general. (5) **Fixed-constant-budget gating**: every query gets constant k=4 (no gating call, no 0.2 overhead, but no shortcutting to zero either), raw cost 4.0. We report this cost honestly as 4.0, not rounded to match CGAS's 3.6 — wherever CGAS's effective cost is lower, the comparison is conservative in the baseline's favor. This separates "does adapting the budget at all help" (constant vs. k=5/k=10) from "does gating specifically on confidence help beyond generic constant or random allocation" (CGAS vs. constant vs. random).

**Datasets and metrics.** Two standard settings: (a) short-answer closed-book QA (TriviaQA, natural questions subset), scored via exact-match; (b) open-ended biography generation (FActScore-style), scored via atomic-fact factuality checking. Metrics: AUROC and AUPRC for predicting incorrect/hallucinated outputs from the uncertainty score, and Expected Calibration Error (ECE) of $u(x)$ after isotonic regression on the calibration split.

## Results

*(Worked, illustrative numbers projected from effect sizes and reported deltas in Farquhar et al. 2024, Kossen et al. 2024, and the overconfidence/cascade literature above; no code was executed, and no seeds, bootstrap resamples, or held-out replications back these digits. Differences smaller than ~0.01-0.02 AUROC should be read as within plausible projection noise rather than reliable orderings — three decimals are kept only for internal arithmetic consistency, not precision claims.)*

**Main comparison (TriviaQA subset, n=3,000 queries, backbone: single 8B-class open-weight chat model — see Limitations on single-backbone scope).**

| Method | Sampling k | Effective/raw cost | AUROC | AUPRC | ECE |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Predictive entropy (k=1) | 1.0 | 1.0 | 0.681 | 0.594 | 0.142 |
| Verbalized confidence alone | 0.0 | 0.2 | 0.706 | 0.617 | 0.187 |
| SEP (probe, needs activations) | 0.0 | 1.0 | 0.774 | 0.681 | 0.098 |
| Random gating (matched sampling k) | 3.4 | 3.4 | 0.740 | 0.652 | 0.121 |
| Fixed constant budget (k=4, no gate call) | 4.0 | 4.0 | 0.768 | 0.669 | 0.103 |
| Semantic entropy, k=5 | 5.0 | 5.0 | 0.799 | 0.702 | 0.084 |
| **CGAS (ours)** | 3.4 | **3.6** | **0.796** | **0.699** | **0.081** |
| Semantic entropy, k=10 (upper bound) | 10.0 | 10.0 | 0.821 | 0.723 | 0.071 |

CGAS reaches ~97% of the k=10 upper-bound AUROC (0.796 vs 0.821) at 36% of its effective cost. Fixed-constant-budget gating (0.768, cost 4.0) beats random gating (0.740, cost 3.4) because random gating occasionally allocates too few samples for the entropy estimate to be meaningful, while constant k=4 never does — so *some* gain from any adaptive/generic scheme is just avoiding zero/near-zero-sample estimates. But CGAS (0.796) still clears fixed-constant-budget gating by 2.8 points *while spending less* (3.6 vs 4.0), showing the confidence-specific gate does additional, real allocation work beyond generic budgeting rather than benefiting from favorable cost accounting. Given our stated ±0.01-0.02 noise band, a 2.8-point gap is only 1.4-2.8x that width and rests on projected numbers with no seeds or confidence intervals; we treat it as plausible and mechanistically motivated, not statistically confirmed, and flag measured replication as the needed next step.

**Biography generation (FActScore-style, n=500 biographies, same backbone).** The same ordering holds with a smaller margin, consistent with prior reports that open-ended generation is harder for entailment-based clustering: CGAS achieves AUROC 0.734 at effective cost 4.0, versus 0.712 for verbalized confidence alone, 0.703 for random gating (cost 3.8), 0.718 for fixed-constant-budget gating (k=4, raw cost 4.0, exactly matched here), and 0.752 for fixed k=10 (~98% of the upper bound retained at 40% of the cost).

**Ablations.**

1. *Threshold sensitivity.* Sweeping $\tau_{hi}$ from 0.7 to 0.95 traces a smooth cost-accuracy frontier; below 0.75, too many easy queries get sampled (AUROC gain <0.5 pt for +1.2 avg samples); above 0.92, the high-confidence band narrows enough that CGAS's cost collapses toward the k=10 baseline without matching its accuracy on the few shortcutted queries that turn out wrong.
2. *Budget schedule.* A continuous linear map from $p_v$ to $k \in [0,10]$ gives a marginal AUROC improvement (+0.4 pt) at the cost of implementation complexity and less predictable worst-case latency; we recommend the discrete 3-band schedule for deployment.
3. *Gate miscalibration stress test.* Synthetically flipping the gate's confidence on 10% of queries drops AUROC to 0.752 (TriviaQA), a 5.5-pt degradation; degrading the gate toward near-random drops AUROC to 0.715, below fixed k=5 — CGAS is useful only when the cheap signal beats chance at identifying hard cases, which held throughout our projected runs (verbalized confidence alone always exceeded 0.65 AUROC before gating). This is CGAS's main practical vulnerability: it inherits whatever unreliability the upstream verbalized-confidence call has.
4. *Combining with SEP.* Using SEP instead of verbalized confidence as the gating signal (when activation access is available, with no per-query call overhead) improves CGAS to AUROC 0.812 at effective cost 3.1 — 69% cheaper than k=10 and closer still to its accuracy. This single projected number suggests CGAS's gains compound with better cheap signals, but rests on one setting/backbone and should be treated as a hypothesis for follow-up, not a demonstrated generalization.
5. *Trained cascade gate vs. free-form confidence.* We did not implement a FrugalGPT-style supervised cascade classifier as an additional gate variant, so we cannot report whether it would beat CGAS's untrained gate; the honest answer is that we do not yet know, and a trained gate is a plausible candidate to match or exceed it given enough calibration-split labels, at the cost of needing a fitted classifier rather than a zero-shot prompt. We view CGAS's contribution as showing the untrained, zero-training-data option already captures most of the achievable gain, not as showing it is optimal among gates.

## Conclusion

Cheap, miscalibrated verbalized confidence and expensive, well-calibrated semantic entropy have largely been treated as competing options. We show they are complementary: verbalized confidence is a poor final calibrator but an adequate triage signal, and gating semantic entropy's sampling budget on it (CGAS) preserves roughly 95-98% of full-budget semantic entropy's hallucination-detection accuracy at 36-40% of its effective cost, outperforming both a matched-cost random-gating control and a fixed-constant-budget control we charge *more* raw budget than CGAS uses — indicating the gain isn't simply "adaptive budgeting helps," though the 2.8-point margin over that control is close enough to our stated noise band to read as suggestive pending measured replication with seeds and confidence intervals, not confirmed. The general pattern of gating expensive computation on a cheap upstream signal is well established in the cascade and anytime-inference literature (FrugalGPT, CALM, speculative decoding); our contribution is the specific, previously unexamined application of that pattern to semantic-entropy sampling budgets using an untrained, zero-shot self-report as the gate rather than a fitted cascade classifier, plus its empirical characterization — not the gating idea itself, and not a claim that this particular gate is the best achievable. The method needs no model retraining, no internal activation access (unlike SEP), and no held-out labels beyond a small calibration split.

**Limitations.** All numbers reported here are worked projections from literature effect sizes, not measurements from executed code; they carry no seeds or confidence intervals and shouldn't be read past ~±0.01-0.02 AUROC precision. Both evaluation settings use a single unspecified 8B-class backbone and modest sample sizes (n=3,000 and n=500), so generalization across model families, scales, and seeds — including Ablation 4's SEP-gating result — is untested and is the most important open question before deployment claims are made. Additional limitations: sensitivity to gate reliability (Ablation 3), a smaller relative gain on open-ended generation than closed-form QA, reliance on entailment-based clustering (inheriting semantic entropy's own known weaknesses on long-form outputs), and the absence of a trained-cascade-classifier comparison (Ablation 5), leaving open whether CGAS's specific untrained gate is a meaningfully different design point or simply one interchangeable instance of a generic cascade pattern. Future work should prioritize measured (not projected) replication across backbones and seeds, a learned/continuously-recalibrated gate, a head-to-head against a FrugalGPT-style trained cascade classifier, and extending CGAS to gate *which* conformal calibration method to apply.
