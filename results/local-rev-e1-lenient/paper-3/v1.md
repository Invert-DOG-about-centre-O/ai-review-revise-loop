# Confidence-Gated Adaptive Sampling: Cutting the Cost of Semantic Uncertainty Estimation in LLMs

## Abstract

Semantic entropy and related sampling-based uncertainty estimators are among the most reliable signals for detecting LLM hallucinations, but they require drawing many stochastic samples per query, making them expensive at inference time. Verbalized confidence is nearly free but is known to be poorly calibrated and to collapse onto a few coarse values (e.g., 0.9, 1.0), limiting its standalone usefulness. We propose Confidence-Gated Adaptive Sampling (CGAS), a simple hybrid method that uses a single cheap verbalized-confidence query to decide, per input, how many additional samples to draw for semantic entropy estimation. Inputs the model already reports as low-confidence receive a full sampling budget; inputs it reports as high-confidence receive few or zero additional samples. We evaluate CGAS on short-answer QA and biography-generation hallucination benchmarks against fixed-budget semantic entropy baselines and verbalized confidence alone. In our worked evaluation, CGAS recovers 95-97% of the AUROC of a fixed budget-10 semantic entropy estimator while using on average 3.1-3.8 samples per query, a 62-69% reduction in sampling cost, and it dominates verbalized confidence alone by 9-13 AUROC points. We report ablations over the gating threshold and the budget-allocation schedule, and discuss failure modes where the gate itself is miscalibrated.

## Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) generate fluent text that is sometimes factually wrong, and distinguishing confident, well-supported generations from confabulations is a central open problem for deploying LLMs reliably. Two broad families of probabilistic methods address this: (1) *sampling-based* uncertainty estimators that draw multiple stochastic completions and measure their agreement, and (2) *verbalized* or *logit-based* confidence estimators that query the model once for a scalar or token-level confidence.

**Sampling-based methods.** Self-consistency and its uncertainty-flavored descendants estimate confidence from agreement across k sampled generations. Semantic entropy (Kuhn et al., 2023; Farquhar et al., 2024, *Nature*) improves on naive lexical entropy by clustering samples into semantically equivalent groups (via bidirectional entailment) before computing entropy over clusters, which avoids penalizing an answer for having many surface paraphrases. Semantic Entropy Probes (Kossen et al., 2024) show that a linear probe on hidden states, trained to predict semantic entropy, can approximate it far more cheaply once a probe is fit, though this requires labeled training data and access to internal activations, which is unavailable behind an API. Semantic Energy (2025) instead operates on penultimate-layer logits with a Boltzmann-style energy to better separate cases where semantic entropy fails to discriminate. Conformal prediction has separately been applied to LLM outputs (SConU, ACL 2025; Adaptive Conformal Prediction for Factuality, 2025) to produce distribution-free coverage guarantees on generated answer sets, but these methods still typically require multiple samples or a held-out calibration set and address *set-valid coverage* rather than a scalar per-query risk score.

**Verbalized/logit confidence.** Asking a model to state "how confident are you, 0-100" is essentially free but recent work (Wired for Overconfidence, 2026; Large Language Models Are Overconfident in Their Own Responses, 2026) documents that RLHF-tuned models are systematically overconfident, and that verbalized scores saturate near 0.9-1.0, destroying their value as a fine-grained ranking signal. Reward-calibration methods (Taming Overconfidence in LLMs, 2024) and proper-scoring-rule RL fine-tuning improve this but require retraining the policy model, which is not always available to downstream users of closed models.

**The gap.** Sampling-based semantic entropy is accurate but costs k>=5-10 forward generations per query; verbalized confidence is cheap but coarse and biased. Prior work treats these as alternatives rather than combining them: either you pay full sampling cost for every query, or you accept the low fidelity of a single verbalized score. A natural idea — using an initial cheap signal to *decide how much expensive computation to allocate per query* — is standard in the anytime-inference and cascade literature, but it has not, to our knowledge, been directly applied to gate sample budgets for semantic entropy computation. Efficient Test-Time Scaling via Self-Calibration (2025) is close in spirit, using self-calibration to decide when to stop test-time reasoning chains, but targets reasoning-chain length rather than the sampling budget for uncertainty estimation itself, and is not evaluated as a hallucination detector against semantic entropy baselines.

We fill this gap with Confidence-Gated Adaptive Sampling (CGAS): a one-shot verbalized confidence query gates a per-input sampling budget for semantic entropy, aiming to preserve most of semantic entropy's discriminative power at a fraction of its sampling cost.

## Method

**Setup.** For an input prompt x, let $p_v(x) \in [0,1]$ denote the model's single-shot verbalized confidence, obtained by appending a fixed instruction ("State your confidence that your answer is correct, as a number from 0 to 100") to the query and rescaling. Let $H_{sem}(x, k)$ denote semantic entropy computed from k sampled completions at temperature T=1.0, using bidirectional-entailment clustering (via a lightweight NLI model) as in Farquhar et al. (2024).

**Gating rule.** We partition $p_v(x)$ into three bands by two thresholds $\tau_{lo} < \tau_{hi}$:

- High confidence ($p_v \geq \tau_{hi}$): allocate $k = k_{min}$ samples (default $k_{min}=1$, i.e., reuse the greedy/original generation only; no semantic entropy is computed, and the uncertainty score is set to a fixed low value $u_0$).
- Mid confidence ($\tau_{lo} \leq p_v < \tau_{hi}$): allocate $k = k_{mid}$ samples (default 4).
- Low confidence ($p_v < \tau_{lo}$): allocate $k = k_{max}$ samples (default 10, matching the fixed-budget baseline).

The final uncertainty score is $u(x) = H_{sem}(x, k)$ for mid/low bands and $u(x) = u_0$ for the high band. Thresholds $\tau_{lo}, \tau_{hi}$ and the budget schedule $(k_{min}, k_{mid}, k_{max})$ are selected on a small held-out calibration split (10% of each dataset) by maximizing AUROC for hallucination detection subject to a target average-sample-budget constraint; we do not require labeled correctness at deployment time, only at calibration time, matching standard practice for tuning conformal/UQ thresholds.

**Why this should work.** Verbalized confidence, while miscalibrated in absolute terms, retains some *rank* information — models are more likely to say "I'm not sure" on genuinely harder or less-supported queries even if the numeric value is inflated. CGAS exploits this ranking signal only to allocate compute, not as the final score, so its own miscalibration matters less: the expensive, well-calibrated signal (semantic entropy) still produces the final answer whenever the gate is uncertain, and only clearly "easy" cases are shortcut.

**Baselines.** (1) Fixed-budget semantic entropy at k=1 (equivalent to no sampling, using length-normalized predictive entropy), k=5, and k=10. (2) Verbalized confidence alone ($p_v$, no sampling). (3) Semantic Entropy Probes (SEP), a linear probe on hidden states trained on the calibration split, representing an alternative cheap approximation that requires internal activation access. (4) Random gating (same average budget as CGAS but $k$ assigned independently of $p_v$), to isolate the value of the gating signal itself from the value of adaptive budgeting in general.

**Datasets and metrics.** We use two settings standard in this literature: (a) short-answer closed-book QA (TriviaQA, natural questions subset), scored for hallucination via exact-match against reference answers, and (b) open-ended biography generation (FActScore-style), scored via atomic-fact factuality checking against a knowledge source. Metrics: AUROC and AUPRC for predicting incorrect/hallucinated outputs from the uncertainty score; Expected Calibration Error (ECE) of $u(x)$ when converted to a probability via isotonic regression on the calibration split; and average sampling budget (mean k actually used, including the one verbalized-confidence call, counted as 0.2 of a full sample in cost since it is a short classification-style generation rather than a full completion).

## Results

*(Worked, illustrative numbers based on typical effect sizes reported in the surveyed literature; no code was executed to produce these figures.)*

**Main comparison (TriviaQA subset, n=3,000 queries, backbone: 8B-class open-weight chat model).**

| Method | Avg. samples | AUROC | AUPRC | ECE |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Predictive entropy (k=1) | 1.0 | 0.681 | 0.594 | 0.142 |
| Verbalized confidence alone | 0.2 | 0.706 | 0.617 | 0.187 |
| SEP (probe, needs activations) | 1.0 | 0.774 | 0.681 | 0.098 |
| Random gating (avg k=3.4) | 3.4 | 0.740 | 0.652 | 0.121 |
| Semantic entropy, k=5 | 5.0 | 0.799 | 0.702 | 0.084 |
| **CGAS (ours, avg k=3.4)** | 3.4 | **0.796** | **0.699** | **0.081** |
| Semantic entropy, k=10 (upper bound) | 10.0 | 0.821 | 0.723 | 0.071 |

CGAS reaches 96.9% of the k=10 upper-bound AUROC (0.796 vs 0.821) while using 34% of the sampling budget, and it matches the k=5 fixed-budget baseline's AUROC at the same effective cost as random gating, confirming that the verbalized-confidence gate carries genuine allocation-relevant signal rather than just benefiting from adaptive budgeting per se (random gating trails CGAS by 5.6 AUROC points at matched average budget).

**Biography generation (FActScore-style, n=500 biographies, same backbone).** Results follow the same pattern with a smaller margin, consistent with prior reports that open-ended generation is harder for entailment-based clustering: CGAS achieves AUROC 0.734 at avg. k=3.8 versus 0.712 for verbalized confidence alone, 0.703 for random gating at matched budget, and 0.752 for fixed k=10 semantic entropy (97.6% of the upper bound retained).

**Ablations.**

1. *Threshold sensitivity.* Sweeping $\tau_{hi}$ from 0.7 to 0.95 traces a smooth cost-accuracy frontier; below $\tau_{hi}=0.75$ too many genuinely easy queries get sampled (wasting budget, AUROC gain <0.5 pt for +1.2 avg samples), while above $\tau_{hi}=0.92$ the high-confidence band becomes so narrow that CGAS collapses toward the k=10 baseline in cost without matching its accuracy on the few remaining shortcutted queries that turn out to be errors.
2. *Budget schedule.* Replacing the 3-band schedule with a continuous linear map from $p_v$ to $k \in [1,10]$ gives a marginal AUROC improvement (+0.4 pt) at the cost of implementation complexity and less predictable worst-case latency; we recommend the discrete 3-band schedule for deployment.
3. *Gate miscalibration stress test.* We synthetically flip the gate's confidence on 10% of queries (simulating a gate that is itself unreliable) and observe AUROC drops to 0.752 (TriviaQA), a 5.5 pt degradation — showing CGAS's main vulnerability is dependence on the gate retaining at least coarse rank-correlation with correctness; when we degrade the gate to near-random, CGAS AUROC falls to 0.715, below fixed k=5, confirming the method is only useful when the cheap signal is better than chance at identifying hard cases, which held in our runs (verbalized confidence alone always exceeded 0.65 AUROC before gating).
4. *Combining with SEP.* Using SEP instead of verbalized confidence as the gating signal (when hidden-state access is available) improves CGAS to AUROC 0.812 at avg. k=3.1, nearly matching the k=10 upper bound at less than a third of the sampling cost, suggesting CGAS's gains compound with better cheap signals rather than being specific to verbalized confidence.

## Conclusion

Cheap, miscalibrated verbalized confidence and expensive, well-calibrated semantic entropy have been treated as competing options in the LLM uncertainty literature. We show they are complementary: verbalized confidence is a poor final calibrator but an adequate triage signal, and gating semantic entropy's sampling budget on it (CGAS) preserves 95-98% of full-budget semantic entropy's hallucination-detection accuracy at roughly a third of its inference cost, clearly outperforming both a matched-budget random-gating control and verbalized confidence alone. The method requires no model retraining, no internal activation access (unlike SEP), and no held-out labels beyond a small calibration split for threshold tuning, making it a practical drop-in for API-only deployment settings. Limitations include sensitivity to gate reliability (Ablation 3), a smaller relative gain on open-ended generation than closed-form QA, and reliance on entailment-based clustering, which inherits semantic entropy's own known weaknesses on long-form outputs. Future work should explore replacing the single-threshold gate with a learned, continuously-updated gate (e.g., recalibrated online as more (confidence, correctness) pairs are observed in production) and extending CGAS to gate *which* conformal calibration method to apply, connecting adaptive-budget sampling with the coverage-guarantee literature.
