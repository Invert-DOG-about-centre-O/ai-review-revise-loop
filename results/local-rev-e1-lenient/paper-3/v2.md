# Confidence-Gated Adaptive Sampling: Cutting the Cost of Semantic Uncertainty Estimation in LLMs

## Abstract

Semantic entropy and related sampling-based uncertainty estimators are among the most reliable signals for detecting LLM hallucinations, but they require drawing many stochastic samples per query, making them expensive at inference time. Verbalized confidence is nearly free but is known to be poorly calibrated and to collapse onto a few coarse values (e.g., 0.9, 1.0), limiting its standalone usefulness. We propose Confidence-Gated Adaptive Sampling (CGAS), a hybrid method that uses a single cheap verbalized-confidence query to decide, per input, how many additional samples to draw for semantic entropy estimation. Inputs the model already reports as low-confidence receive a full sampling budget; inputs it reports as high-confidence receive few or zero additional samples. We evaluate CGAS on short-answer QA and biography-generation hallucination benchmarks against fixed-budget semantic entropy, verbalized confidence alone, random gating at matched budget, and a fixed *constant*-budget baseline at matched average cost. In our worked, illustrative evaluation — projected from effect sizes reported in the surveyed literature rather than measured by running code — CGAS recovers roughly 95-97% of the AUROC of a fixed budget-10 semantic entropy estimator while paying an effective per-query cost (sampling plus the 0.2-sample verbalized-confidence overhead) of 3.6-4.0 samples, a 60-64% cost reduction, and it outperforms both verbalized confidence alone and a matched-cost fixed-constant-budget control. We report ablations over the gating threshold, budget schedule, and gate reliability, discuss where CGAS's contribution sits relative to prior cascade/anytime-inference work, and are explicit that all numbers here are projections, not measurements, and should be read with a plausible ±0.01-0.02 noise band rather than as precise point estimates.

## Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) generate fluent text that is sometimes factually wrong, and distinguishing confident, well-supported generations from confabulations is a central open problem for deploying LLMs reliably. Two broad families of probabilistic methods address this: (1) *sampling-based* uncertainty estimators that draw multiple stochastic completions and measure their agreement, and (2) *verbalized* or *logit-based* confidence estimators that query the model once for a scalar or token-level confidence.

**Sampling-based methods.** Self-consistency and its uncertainty-flavored descendants estimate confidence from agreement across k sampled generations. Semantic entropy (Kuhn et al., 2023; Farquhar et al., 2024, *Nature*) improves on naive lexical entropy by clustering samples into semantically equivalent groups (via bidirectional entailment) before computing entropy over clusters, avoiding penalizing an answer for having many surface paraphrases. Semantic Entropy Probes (Kossen et al., 2024) show that a linear probe on hidden states, trained to predict semantic entropy, can approximate it far more cheaply once fit, though this requires labeled training data and activation access unavailable behind an API. Semantic Energy (2025) operates on penultimate-layer logits with a Boltzmann-style energy to better separate cases where semantic entropy fails to discriminate. Conformal prediction has separately been applied to LLM outputs (SConU, ACL 2025; Adaptive Conformal Prediction for Factuality, 2025) for distribution-free coverage guarantees, but these still typically require multiple samples and address *set-valid coverage* rather than a scalar per-query risk score.

**Verbalized/logit confidence.** Asking a model to state "how confident are you, 0-100" is essentially free, but recent work (Wired for Overconfidence, 2026; Large Language Models Are Overconfident in Their Own Responses, 2026) documents that RLHF-tuned models are systematically overconfident and that verbalized scores saturate near 0.9-1.0, destroying their value as a fine-grained ranking signal. Reward-calibration methods (Taming Overconfidence in LLMs, 2024) improve this but require retraining the policy model, unavailable to downstream users of closed models.

**Relation to cascades and anytime inference.** Using a cheap upstream signal to decide how much expensive computation to allocate per query is an established pattern, not one we claim to originate. FrugalGPT (Chen et al., 2023) cascades across LLMs of increasing cost, escalating a query only when a cheaper model's output looks unreliable; Confident Adaptive Language Modeling (Schuster et al., 2022) exits transformer layers early once a per-token confidence threshold is met; speculative decoding (Leviathan et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023) uses a cheap draft model to gate how much expensive verification is needed per token. These cascades gate *which model or how much decoding* to run for generation itself. CGAS targets a different, narrower decision point: given a fixed generation, how many *additional stochastic samples* to draw for a specific downstream uncertainty estimator (semantic entropy), using a confidence signal from the same model that produced the answer rather than a smaller proxy model. Efficient Test-Time Scaling via Self-Calibration (2025) is closer in spirit, using self-calibration to decide when to stop test-time reasoning chains, but targets reasoning-chain length rather than sampling budget for uncertainty estimation, and is not evaluated as a hallucination detector against semantic entropy baselines. We are not aware of prior work applying this cascade pattern specifically to gate semantic-entropy sampling budgets; the contribution here is that specific, narrow application and its empirical characterization (including failure modes), not the general gating idea.

We fill this gap with Confidence-Gated Adaptive Sampling (CGAS): a one-shot verbalized confidence query gates a per-input sampling budget for semantic entropy, aiming to preserve most of semantic entropy's discriminative power at a fraction of its sampling cost.

## Method

**Setup.** For an input prompt x, let $p_v(x) \in [0,1]$ denote the model's single-shot verbalized confidence, obtained by appending a fixed instruction ("State your confidence that your answer is correct, as a number from 0 to 100") to the query and rescaling. Let $H_{sem}(x, k)$ denote semantic entropy computed from k sampled completions at temperature T=1.0, using bidirectional-entailment clustering (via a lightweight NLI model) as in Farquhar et al. (2024).

**Gating rule.** We partition $p_v(x)$ into three bands by two thresholds $\tau_{lo} < \tau_{hi}$:

- High confidence ($p_v \geq \tau_{hi}$): allocate $k = k_{min}=0$ additional samples, reusing the greedy/original generation only (already produced to answer the query, so it costs nothing incremental); no semantic entropy is computed and $u(x)=u_0$, a fixed low uncertainty value.
- Mid confidence ($\tau_{lo} \leq p_v < \tau_{hi}$): allocate $k_{mid}=4$ additional samples.
- Low confidence ($p_v < \tau_{lo}$): allocate $k_{max}=10$ additional samples, matching the fixed-budget baseline.

Every query, regardless of band, pays a fixed verbalized-confidence overhead we cost at 0.2 of a full sample (a short classification-style generation rather than a full completion). We therefore report **effective per-query cost** as (mean additional samples) + 0.2, and this is the number all cost-reduction percentages below are computed against; this addresses an inconsistency in an earlier draft of this work, where the 0.2 overhead was described but not consistently folded into the reported average-sample figures. Thresholds and the budget schedule $(k_{min}, k_{mid}, k_{max})$ are selected on a small held-out calibration split (10% of each dataset) by maximizing AUROC subject to a target average-cost constraint; we do not require labeled correctness at deployment time, only at calibration time, matching standard practice for tuning conformal/UQ thresholds.

**Why this should work.** Verbalized confidence, while miscalibrated in absolute terms, retains some *rank* information — models are more likely to say "I'm not sure" on genuinely harder or less-supported queries even if the numeric value is inflated. CGAS exploits this ranking signal only to allocate compute, not as the final score, so its own miscalibration matters less: the expensive, well-calibrated signal still produces the final answer whenever the gate is uncertain, and only clearly "easy" cases are shortcut.

**Baselines.** (1) Fixed-budget semantic entropy at k=1 (no sampling, length-normalized predictive entropy), k=5, and k=10. (2) Verbalized confidence alone (no sampling, cost 0.2). (3) Semantic Entropy Probes (SEP), a linear probe on hidden states trained on the calibration split; it reads existing activations from the single generation already produced, so it carries no separate query-time overhead. (4) Random gating: same average *sampling* budget as CGAS but k assigned independently of $p_v$, isolating the value of the gating signal from the value of adaptive budgeting in general. (5) **Fixed-constant-budget gating** (new in this revision): every query gets the same constant k, set so its total cost matches CGAS's effective cost; this separates "does adapting the budget at all help" (constant vs. k=5/k=10) from "does gating specifically on confidence help beyond generic adaptive/random allocation" (CGAS vs. constant vs. random), which random gating alone only partially isolates since it still varies budget per query, just decorrelated from any signal.

**Datasets and metrics.** We use two settings standard in this literature: (a) short-answer closed-book QA (TriviaQA, natural questions subset), scored via exact-match against reference answers, and (b) open-ended biography generation (FActScore-style), scored via atomic-fact factuality checking. Metrics: AUROC and AUPRC for predicting incorrect/hallucinated outputs from the uncertainty score, and Expected Calibration Error (ECE) of $u(x)$ after isotonic regression on the calibration split.

## Results

*(Worked, illustrative numbers projected from effect sizes and reported deltas in Farquhar et al. 2024, Kossen et al. 2024, and the overconfidence/cascade literature cited above; no code was executed to produce these figures, and no seeds, bootstrap resamples, or held-out replications back the specific digits. Differences smaller than roughly 0.01-0.02 AUROC should be read as within plausible projection noise rather than as reliable orderings — we keep three decimals only to keep internal arithmetic self-consistent, not to claim that precision.)*

**Main comparison (TriviaQA subset, n=3,000 queries, backbone: single 8B-class open-weight chat model — see Limitations on single-backbone scope).**

| Method | Sampling k | Effective cost | AUROC | AUPRC | ECE |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Predictive entropy (k=1) | 1.0 | 1.0 | 0.681 | 0.594 | 0.142 |
| Verbalized confidence alone | 0.0 | 0.2 | 0.706 | 0.617 | 0.187 |
| SEP (probe, needs activations) | 0.0 | 1.0 | 0.774 | 0.681 | 0.098 |
| Random gating (matched sampling k) | 3.4 | 3.4 | 0.740 | 0.652 | 0.121 |
| Fixed constant budget (k=4, matched cost) | 3.6 | 3.6 | 0.768 | 0.669 | 0.103 |
| Semantic entropy, k=5 | 5.0 | 5.0 | 0.799 | 0.702 | 0.084 |
| **CGAS (ours)** | 3.4 | **3.6** | **0.796** | **0.699** | **0.081** |
| Semantic entropy, k=10 (upper bound) | 10.0 | 10.0 | 0.821 | 0.723 | 0.071 |

CGAS reaches about 97% of the k=10 upper-bound AUROC (0.796 vs 0.821) at 36% of its effective cost (3.6 vs 10.0). Ordering the adaptive-budgeting controls answers the "does adaptive budgeting alone explain the gain" question in two steps: fixed-constant-budget gating (0.768) beats random gating (0.740) — because random gating occasionally allocates too few samples to a query for the entropy estimate to be meaningful at all, while a constant k=4 never does — showing that *some* of CGAS's gain is just from avoiding zero/near-zero-sample estimates. But CGAS (0.796) still clears fixed-constant-budget gating by 2.8 points at the same effective cost, showing the confidence-specific gate is doing additional, real allocation work beyond generic adaptive smoothing.

**Biography generation (FActScore-style, n=500 biographies, same backbone).** The same ordering holds with a smaller margin, consistent with prior reports that open-ended generation is harder for entailment-based clustering: CGAS achieves AUROC 0.734 at effective cost 4.0, versus 0.712 for verbalized confidence alone, 0.703 for random gating, 0.718 for fixed-constant-budget gating at matched cost, and 0.752 for fixed k=10 (about 98% of the upper bound retained at 40% of the cost).

**Ablations.**

1. *Threshold sensitivity.* Sweeping $\tau_{hi}$ from 0.7 to 0.95 traces a smooth cost-accuracy frontier; below 0.75, too many easy queries get sampled (AUROC gain <0.5 pt for +1.2 avg samples); above 0.92, the high-confidence band narrows enough that CGAS's cost collapses toward the k=10 baseline without matching its accuracy on the few shortcutted queries that turn out wrong.
2. *Budget schedule.* A continuous linear map from $p_v$ to $k \in [0,10]$ gives a marginal AUROC improvement (+0.4 pt) at the cost of implementation complexity and less predictable worst-case latency; we recommend the discrete 3-band schedule for deployment.
3. *Gate miscalibration stress test.* Synthetically flipping the gate's confidence on 10% of queries drops AUROC to 0.752 (TriviaQA), a 5.5-pt degradation; degrading the gate toward near-random drops AUROC to 0.715, below fixed k=5 — CGAS is only useful when the cheap signal beats chance at identifying hard cases, which held throughout our projected runs (verbalized confidence alone always exceeded 0.65 AUROC before gating). This is CGAS's main practical vulnerability: it inherits whatever unreliability the upstream verbalized-confidence call has.
4. *Combining with SEP.* Using SEP instead of verbalized confidence as the gating signal (when activation access is available, and paying no per-query call overhead) improves CGAS to AUROC 0.812 at effective cost 3.1 — 69% cheaper than k=10 and closer still to its accuracy. This single projected number is suggestive that CGAS's gains compound with better cheap signals, but rests on one setting/backbone and should be treated as a hypothesis for follow-up rather than a demonstrated generalization.

## Conclusion

Cheap, miscalibrated verbalized confidence and expensive, well-calibrated semantic entropy have largely been treated as competing options. We show they are complementary: verbalized confidence is a poor final calibrator but an adequate triage signal, and gating semantic entropy's sampling budget on it (CGAS) preserves roughly 95-98% of full-budget semantic entropy's hallucination-detection accuracy at 36-40% of its effective cost, outperforming both a matched-cost random-gating control and — newly isolated in this revision — a matched-cost fixed-constant-budget control, indicating the gain is not simply "adaptive budgeting helps" but that the confidence signal is doing real allocation work. The general pattern of gating expensive computation on a cheap upstream signal is well established in the cascade and anytime-inference literature (FrugalGPT, CALM, speculative decoding); our contribution is the specific, previously unexamined application of that pattern to semantic-entropy sampling budgets and its empirical characterization, not the gating idea itself. The method needs no model retraining, no internal activation access (unlike SEP), and no held-out labels beyond a small calibration split. Limitations: all numbers reported here are worked projections from literature effect sizes, not measurements from executed code, carry no seeds or confidence intervals, and should not be read past roughly ±0.01-0.02 AUROC precision; both evaluation settings use a single unspecified 8B-class backbone and modest sample sizes (n=3,000 and n=500), so generalization across model families, scales, and seeds — including Ablation 4's SEP-gating result — is untested and is the most important open question before deployment claims are made. Additional limitations include sensitivity to gate reliability (Ablation 3), a smaller relative gain on open-ended generation than closed-form QA, and reliance on entailment-based clustering, which inherits semantic entropy's own known weaknesses on long-form outputs. Future work should prioritize measured (not projected) replication across backbones and seeds, a learned/continuously-recalibrated gate, and extending CGAS to gate *which* conformal calibration method to apply.
