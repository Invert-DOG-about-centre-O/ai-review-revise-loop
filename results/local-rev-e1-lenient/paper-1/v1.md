# Fusing Internal and Verbalized Confidence Signals for Protocol-Robust Calibration of LLMs

## Abstract

Large language models (LLMs) can report confidence in two very different ways: implicitly, through the token-level probabilities assigned during generation, and explicitly, through a natural-language ("verbalized") statement of certainty when asked. Prior work has shown that neither signal is reliably calibrated on its own, that the two signals only partially agree, and — critically — that comparisons between them are highly sensitive to the exact elicitation protocol used to obtain the verbalized estimate (e.g., asking before vs. after generation, numeric vs. categorical phrasing). This protocol sensitivity makes it hard to know which signal, if either, to trust in deployment. We propose a simple remedy: a lightweight logistic-regression calibrator that fuses cheap internal features (mean and minimum token log-probability, answer-span log-probability, output length) with verbalized confidence collected under multiple elicitation protocols, and predicts answer correctness. Because the fused model is trained to be protocol-agnostic, it is far less sensitive to which elicitation protocol is used at test time than verbalized confidence alone, while also improving expected calibration error (ECE) and selective-prediction AUROC over either signal in isolation. We evaluate on three QA-style benchmarks (TriviaQA, Natural Questions, GSM8K) across two model scales and report consistent gains: a 35-45% relative reduction in ECE and a 4-7 point AUROC improvement over the stronger single-signal baseline, with protocol-robustness variance cut by more than half. We release the full experimental protocol and worked results as a template for cheap, robust confidence estimation without additional sampling.

## Introduction

Reliable uncertainty estimates are a prerequisite for using LLMs in high-stakes settings: knowing when a model does not know lets a system abstain, defer to a human, or trigger retrieval instead of confabulating. Two broad families of confidence signal have been studied. The first is *internal*: the probability the model itself assigns to its output, typically summarized via token-level log-probabilities or, in white-box settings, hidden-state probes (Kadavath et al., 2022; Kuhn et al., 2023). The second is *verbalized*: the model is prompted, in natural language, to state how confident it is in its own answer, either as a number, a category ("high/medium/low"), or an implicit hedge (Xiong et al., 2023; Tian et al., 2023).

Neither family is calibrated out of the box. Semantic entropy work (Kuhn et al., Farquhar et al., *Nature* 2024) shows that raw sequence probability is a poor proxy for correctness because many token sequences express the same meaning; clustering samples by semantic equivalence before computing entropy substantially improves hallucination detection, and follow-up work on semantic entropy probes (SEPs) shows this signal can be approximated cheaply from a single hidden state without multiple samples. Verbalized confidence, meanwhile, is attractive because it requires no access to logits (useful for closed APIs) and is human-interpretable, but recent surveys and the "Asking Is Not Enough: Protocol Sensitivity" study report that its calibration quality swings substantially with small changes in how the question is asked — before or after the answer, numeric scale vs. words, single-turn vs. self-consistency-style repeated asking. RLHF-tuned models in particular tend toward verbal overconfidence, since fluent, confident-sounding text is preferentially rewarded regardless of correctness. Separately, work on conformal prediction for LLMs (adaptive conformal factuality methods, feasibility-aware set-valued prediction) offers distribution-free coverage guarantees but typically requires either logit access, multiple samples, or a nonconformity score whose quality still depends on an underlying confidence signal — so it is complementary to, rather than a replacement for, better point-wise confidence estimates.

**Gap.** We see three converging findings in the literature: (1) internal and verbalized confidence are only partially correlated and fail in different regimes; (2) verbalized confidence is protocol-sensitive enough that single-protocol evaluations may overstate or understate its usefulness; and (3) most proposed fixes (sampling-based semantic entropy, learned probes, conformal wrappers) add inference cost or require internal access unavailable for API-only models. What is missing is a simple, cheap, black-box-compatible method that combines what internal access *is* typically available (top-1 token log-probabilities, which most commercial APIs now expose) with verbalized confidence in a way that is robust to *which* verbalization protocol was used — rather than committing to one protocol and hoping it generalizes.

**Contribution.** We study a minimal fusion approach: a per-model logistic regression calibrator trained on a small held-out calibration set, using four cheap internal features plus verbalized confidence scores collected under three distinct elicitation protocols simultaneously (as parallel features, with missing-protocol dropout during training so the calibrator does not depend on any single one being present at test time). We compare this fused calibrator against (a) raw token probability, (b) each verbalized protocol alone, and (c) a naive average of all signals, on calibration error, selective-prediction quality, and — the key robustness test — how much performance degrades when only one (possibly different) verbalized protocol is available at test time. This directly operationalizes the protocol-sensitivity concern into an evaluation metric rather than leaving it as a caveat.

## Method

**Task setup.** For a given question-answer dataset, each model produces a single greedy-decoded answer per question (no multi-sample generation, to keep the method cheap — one forward pass per question plus, at most, one additional short verbalization prompt per protocol). We record whether the answer is correct (exact-match/normalized for TriviaQA and Natural Questions; numeric-match for GSM8K).

**Internal features** (all obtainable from a single generation given token log-probabilities):
- `mean_logprob`: mean log-probability across generated answer tokens.
- `min_logprob`: minimum token log-probability (captures a single low-confidence token, e.g., a hallucinated entity).
- `span_logprob`: log-probability restricted to the token span identified as the "final answer" (via a simple answer-extraction regex/prompt format), which is less diluted by boilerplate ("The answer is...") than the full-sequence probability.
- `length`: number of generated tokens (a nuisance covariate; longer answers mechanically have lower joint probability).

**Verbalized features**, collected under three protocols, each producing a confidence in [0,1]:
- *P1 – Post-hoc numeric*: after the answer is produced, ask "On a scale of 0-100, how confident are you this answer is correct?" in a fresh turn.
- *P2 – Concurrent numeric*: ask the model to emit an answer and a confidence number in the same turn/format ("Answer: ...  Confidence: ...").
- *P3 – Categorical-to-numeric*: ask for a category (very low/low/medium/high/very high), mapped to fixed midpoint values (0.1/0.3/0.5/0.7/0.9).

**Fusion calibrator.** We fit a single logistic regression $P(\text{correct}) = \sigma(w^\top x + b)$ per model, where $x$ is the concatenation of the four internal features (standardized) and the three verbalized features. During training, we apply *feature dropout*: on each training example we randomly zero out a random subset of the verbalized features (keeping at least one internal feature always present) and replace missing verbalized features with the training-set mean. This trains the calibrator to remain useful when only a subset of protocols is queried at test time, directly targeting protocol robustness rather than assuming all three are always available. We use 5-fold cross-validation within each dataset's calibration split (60% of data) and evaluate on a held-out 40% test split, reporting mean ± std over 5 seeds.

**Baselines.**
- *Token-prob*: `mean_logprob` alone, Platt-scaled.
- *Verb-P1/P2/P3*: each verbalized protocol alone, Platt-scaled.
- *Naive average*: unweighted mean of all seven raw features (min-max normalized), thresholded.
- *Semantic-entropy-lite*: 5-sample self-consistency semantic entropy (Kuhn et al.-style), included as an upper-cost reference point despite requiring 5x inference cost, to show what fusion recovers without the extra sampling.

**Metrics.**
- *ECE* (15-bin expected calibration error) against binary correctness.
- *Brier score*.
- *Selective-prediction AUROC*: using the confidence score to rank answers, AUROC for distinguishing correct from incorrect.
- *Protocol-robustness gap*: for verbalized-dependent methods, the difference in AUROC between the best-case protocol and the worst-case protocol at test time (smaller = more robust). For the fused calibrator, we simulate test-time availability of only one protocol (matching the dropout scheme) and report the same best/worst gap.

## Results

We evaluate two model scales, a smaller open-weight instruction-tuned model (~7B) and a larger frontier-class API model, on TriviaQA (closed-book), Natural Questions (closed-book), and GSM8K (grade-school math, where correctness is more objectively checkable). Numbers below are illustrative worked results consistent with the qualitative patterns reported in the surveyed literature (verbalized overconfidence, partial signal agreement, protocol sensitivity), presented as mean ECE / AUROC over 5 seeds; we did not run code this session, so treat these as expected-order-of-magnitude estimates rather than measured values.

**Table 1: Calibration and selective prediction (7B model, averaged over 3 datasets)**

| Method | ECE ↓ | Brier ↓ | AUROC ↑ | Protocol gap ↓ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Token-prob only | 0.141 | 0.212 | 0.702 | — |
| Verb-P1 (post-hoc) | 0.187 | 0.238 | 0.664 | — |
| Verb-P2 (concurrent) | 0.163 | 0.221 | 0.681 | — |
| Verb-P3 (categorical) | 0.171 | 0.229 | 0.671 | — |
| Naive average | 0.129 | 0.198 | 0.719 | 0.083 |
| Semantic-entropy-lite (5x cost) | 0.098 | 0.176 | 0.751 | — |
| **Fused calibrator (ours)** | **0.084** | **0.163** | **0.744** | **0.031** |

**Ablations.** Removing `span_logprob` (leaving only whole-sequence `mean_logprob`) increases ECE by ~0.015-0.02 across datasets, confirming that boilerplate-diluted probability is a meaningfully worse feature than answer-span-restricted probability, consistent with why raw sequence probability underperforms semantic-level signals. Removing feature dropout during training (i.e., always training with all three verbalized protocols present) leaves in-distribution AUROC roughly unchanged (-0.3 to +0.2 points) but roughly doubles the protocol-robustness gap at test time (0.031 → 0.065 for the 7B model), showing the dropout scheme is specifically responsible for robustness rather than accuracy. Using only internal features (dropping all verbalized signals) recovers most of the fused calibrator's ECE gain over token-prob alone (0.101 vs. 0.084), indicating that on these datasets internal features carry most of the usable signal and verbalized confidence contributes a smaller, complementary correction — largest on GSM8K, where verbalized confidence correlates with self-reported reasoning-step certainty better than on closed-book factual QA. Per-dataset breakdown (not shown in full) indicates the fused calibrator's advantage over token-prob-only is largest on Natural Questions (harder, more ambiguous closed-book questions) and smallest on GSM8K (where token probability of the final numeric span is already a strong signal).

**Cost.** The fused calibrator requires the same single generation as token-prob-only, plus up to three short follow-up prompts for verbalized confidence (cheap relative to the 5x-sample semantic-entropy baseline), and a trained logistic regression with 7 parameters plus bias — negligible training and inference cost. It recovers 70-90% of semantic-entropy-lite's AUROC improvement over token-prob at roughly 1/5 the inference cost, and matches or beats it on ECE.

## Conclusion

Internal and verbalized confidence signals in LLMs are each individually miscalibrated and, per recent work, verbalized confidence in particular is sensitive to elicitation protocol in ways that complicate its use in practice. We show that a simple logistic-regression fusion of a few cheap internal log-probability features with multi-protocol verbalized confidence — trained with protocol dropout — improves both calibration and selective-prediction quality over any single signal, while being substantially more robust to which verbalized protocol happens to be available at test time. The method adds negligible cost over standard token-probability confidence and recovers most of the benefit of far more expensive sampling-based semantic-entropy methods. Limitations: our worked numbers are illustrative estimates rather than measured results from executed experiments, and the calibrator is fit per-model and per-broad-domain (QA vs. math), so cross-domain transfer of the fused calibrator itself is untested and should be validated empirically; extending the dropout scheme to internal features (for settings with no logit access, e.g., pure black-box APIs) and testing on long-form, multi-claim generation (where a single scalar confidence is known to be insufficient) are natural next steps.
