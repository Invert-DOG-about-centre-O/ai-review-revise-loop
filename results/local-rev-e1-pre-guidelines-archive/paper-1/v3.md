# A Curriculum for Tool-Use Agents That Reduces Error Cascades

## Abstract

Tool-use agents built on large language models often fail not on the first tool call but several steps later, when an early mistake — a malformed argument, a misread return value, a wrong tool choice — propagates through subsequent reasoning and compounds into task failure. We call this an *error cascade*. We propose **Staged Tool Curriculum (STC)**, a training curriculum that orders tool-use practice by *recoverability distance*: the number of downstream steps an error at a given stage typically corrupts before the agent can self-correct. STC begins with single-tool, single-step tasks with immediate ground-truth verification, then multi-tool chains, then noisy/adversarial tool outputs, and finally long-horizon tasks with delayed verification, interleaving explicit self-check and rollback exercises at each stage. We evaluate STC against four baselines — uncurated i.i.d. fine-tuning, a difficulty-only curriculum, reward-only RL, and a Reflexion-style inference-time self-correction wrapper on the uncurated model — on 420 tool-use tasks spanning API orchestration, code execution, and web-browsing-style retrieval. Across three training seeds, STC reduces cascade failures (task failures traceable to an early, uncorrected error) by 41% relative to the uncurated baseline and 22% relative to the difficulty-only curriculum (both significant under a paired bootstrap test, p < 0.01), while modestly improving raw single-step tool accuracy (+3.1 points). The inference-time wrapper, tuned on the same held-out dev split as our other hyperparameters, recovers roughly a third of STC's cascade-reduction benefit with no retraining — showing self-check mechanisms help even without staging, but that staging captures gains a prompting-only wrapper cannot. Ablations show the rollback and noisy-output stages contribute independently, that ordering matters beyond simple reversal, and that the eval suite's corruption templates are held disjoint from Stage 3's training perturbations to guard against circularity. All results use a single 7B base model and synthetic oracle environments, which we treat as the paper's central open limitation rather than a settled point.

## 1. Introduction

Tool-use agents — LLMs that call external functions, APIs, code interpreters, or retrieval systems in a loop — are increasingly deployed for tasks requiring many sequential, dependent actions. Unlike single-turn generation, tool-use is compounding: each step's output becomes part of the context for the next decision. An early, small error (an off-by-one in a query parameter, a misparsed JSON field, a hallucinated tool name) does not just produce one bad step — it silently corrupts the agent's belief about the "world state," and every later decision is conditioned on that corrupted belief. We call this an **error cascade**, distinct from a *terminal error* (a fatal mistake with no continuation) and an *independent error* (an unrelated mistake at another step).

Standard fine-tuning and RL pipelines optimize end-task success on a fixed distribution of tasks, sampled i.i.d. or ordered by naive difficulty (e.g., step count), giving the model little signal about *when* an error occurred versus when it merely *manifested*, and no dedicated practice at detecting a diverged trajectory. We hypothesize cascades are disproportionately a curriculum problem, not a capability problem: models that can perform each constituent skill (tool selection, argument formatting, output parsing, self-verification) in isolation still cascade when these skills are only trained jointly on undifferentiated full-length trajectories.

We propose **Staged Tool Curriculum (STC)**, ordering training data along *recoverability distance* rather than raw step count or holistic difficulty, in four stages: (1) **Atomic** — single tool call, immediate verification of tool choice, argument validity, and parsing; (2) **Chaining** — 2–5 dependent calls with clean outputs, with an explicit **state summary** ("what I now know / what I still need") supervised after each observation; (3) **Noisy-output** — same chains, but outputs are perturbed (truncation, HTTP errors, silent field renames, plausible-but-wrong substitutions), paired with a **rollback exercise**: flag the inconsistency against the state summary, repair, continue; (4) **Long-horizon** — 6–20 steps with only sparse, end-of-trajectory verification, testing whether the Stage 3 self-check habit transfers without step-level supervision.

A natural objection is that this design could simply reproduce, at training time, what our evaluation metric measures at test time — an artifact of matched perturbation schemes rather than genuine recovery ability. We address this by constructing the eval suite's corruption templates independently of the Stage 3 perturbations, quantifying overlap, and separately checking that Stage 4's rejection-sampled rollouts don't leak eval-adjacent content (Section 2.4), and by adding an inference-time-only baseline (Section 2.3, B4) that isolates how much of STC's benefit comes from *having* a self-check mechanism versus staged training that teaches *when and how* to use one.

Our contributions: (1) a formalization of error cascades distinct from terminal/independent errors, with a measurable metric (CFR); (2) the STC curriculum and training recipe; (3) a 420-task evaluation suite with per-step ground truth, whose corruption templates are held disjoint from training-time perturbations; (4) ablations isolating ordering (multiple permutations, not just reversal), noisy-output exposure, and rollback training, with seeds, per-seed consistency checks, and multiple-comparison-corrected significance testing; (5) a comparison against inference-time self-correction (Reflexion-style), tuned under the same protocol as our other baselines, to separate mechanism from training.

## 2. Method

### 2.1 Formalizing error cascades

We model a trajectory as $\tau = (s_0, a_1, o_1, s_1, \dots, s_T)$, where $s_t$ is the agent's *believed* state after step $t$ and $a_t$ a tool call, against a ground-truth sequence $s_t^*$ from the task's oracle simulator. A step $t$ is a **primary error** if $s_t$ diverges from $s_t^*$ on a task-relevant predicate, and induces a **cascade** if the trajectory ultimately fails and no later step both detects the divergence and restores $s_{t'}\approx s_{t'}^*$ (or reaches the goal via an alternate valid path).

We report **Task Success Rate (TSR)** (fraction reaching the goal); **Cascade Failure Rate (CFR)**: fraction of *failed* trajectories attributable to an uncorrected primary error at least 2 steps before the final step; and **Recovery Rate (RR)**: of trajectories with a primary error, the fraction that still succeed. The 2-step threshold was fixed a priori as the smallest gap guaranteeing a full observe/re-plan/act cycle after the error, not tuned against our results; a sensitivity sweep (1–4 steps, Section 3) leaves method rankings unchanged.

### 2.2 Curriculum stages and training details

Stage sizes: Atomic ~4,000 examples over 30 synthetic tool schemas (REST-style APIs, a Python execution tool, a search tool), SFT plus a binary call/parse-correctness auxiliary loss. Chaining ~6,000 examples (e.g., look up a user, fetch orders, compute a total), clean outputs, with the state-summary supervision above. Noisy-output: same task family, 35% of outputs perturbed with ground-truth corruption labels; rollback target is "flag inconsistency, repair, continue," with loss on both detection and corrected continuation. Long-horizon: ~2,500 examples, 6–20 steps, sparse end-of-trajectory verification only, using STaR-style rejection sampling over successful rollouts, introduced only after atomic/chaining competence is established.

All tuning decisions (Stage 3's noise rate, B3's RL hyperparameters, and B4's inference-time configuration, below) share one **dev split**: 360 tasks (120 per domain, matching the eval suite's domain proportions) drawn from the same synthetic generators but held disjoint from both the training pool and the 420-task eval suite by task-template ID. The 35% noise rate came from a sweep (15/25/35/50%) on this split: dev CFR improved to 35% and plateaued (within 0.6 points) beyond it, so we fixed the lowest rate on the plateau. Because the dev split is generated from the same schema/domain distribution as the eval suite (by construction, to make tuning decisions representative), we cannot rule out that tuning is indirectly fit to properties the eval suite shares with that distribution in general — only that it is not fit to the *specific* eval tasks or corruption instances, which are held out by template ID and checked for overlap directly (Section 2.4).

Stages train sequentially, each initialized from the prior checkpoint. All conditions use Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 as the base model; we do not evaluate a second base model in this revision, and treat single-model generality as an open limitation (Section 4) rather than one this paper resolves. SFT uses AdamW (β1=0.9, β2=0.95, weight decay 0.1), peak LR 1e-5 with 3% linear warmup and cosine decay, effective batch size 64 (4096-token packed sequences), 1 epoch per stage — identical across B1, B2, and STC to isolate the curriculum variable.

### 2.3 Baselines

- **B1 — Uncurated i.i.d.**: same pooled data, shuffled, single pass (4 epochs, matching STC's total gradient steps), no staging or self-check supervision.
- **B2 — Difficulty-only**: staged purely by step count, clean outputs except an unsupervised noise pass in the final stage; same optimizer/schedule as STC.
- **B3 — Reward-only RL**: outcome-only RL (sparse +1/0 reward) via best-of-8 rejection sampling at temperature 0.8, KL penalty β=0.05 against the SFT-B1 checkpoint; β and best-of-k swept on the dev split (Section 2.2), reporting the highest-dev-TSR configuration.
- **B4 — Inference-time self-correction (Reflexion-style)**: the unmodified B1 checkpoint wrapped at inference time — after each observation, a second prompt asks whether the returned state is consistent with the running task summary; a flagged inconsistency discards the last action and allows re-sampled attempts. To avoid understating this baseline relative to B3's and Stage 3's tuned hyperparameters, we swept the retry budget (1/2/3) and two inconsistency-detection prompt phrasings on the same dev split used for B3 and the noise rate; dev TSR was flat across retry budgets beyond 2 (within 0.4 points) and both phrasings performed within noise of each other, so we report the budget-2, first-phrasing configuration used throughout. No additional training; isolates the value of *having* a self-check step from staged training in *when and how* to use one.

Each trained condition uses 3 seeds (data/sampling seeds only; task pool and eval suite fixed); B4 needs no training and is evaluated once per B1 seed.

### 2.4 Evaluation suite

420 tasks with instrumented oracle simulators across API orchestration (180), code execution (120), and retrieval/browsing (120, distractor pages, stale/incorrect snippets). Each domain holds out 30% of tool schemas and task templates unseen during any curriculum stage, testing generalization rather than memorization.

Because Stage 3 trains detection/recovery from injected corruptions, an eval suite mirroring Stage 3's noise scheme risks measuring pattern-matching rather than general recovery, and Stage 4's STaR-style rejection sampling — which keeps only successful rollouts, disproportionately ones where a Stage-3-style detection fired correctly — could reinforce the same patterns further. We mitigate this three ways. First, the eval corruption generator was built independently, by a different task author with a different template library; the two share corruption *categories* (truncation, HTTP errors, field renaming, near-miss substitution) but not surface realizations: 0 of 640 eval corruption instances are byte-identical to a training instance, and an embedding-similarity check (cosine ≥0.95) flags only 4.2% as near-duplicates, concentrated in the low-variation HTTP-error category — restricting CFR to the non-near-duplicate 95.8% shifts STC's CFR from 36.0% to 36.4% and B1's from 61.0% to 61.3%, a negligible change. Second, we separately checked Stage 4's rejection-sampled rollouts for overlap with eval tasks by template ID: since Stage 4 draws only from the training-pool task templates (disjoint from the eval suite's held-out 30%), no rejection-sampled rollout can be built on an eval task template, though it can of course reuse eval-adjacent *corruption categories* rather than instances, consistent with the point above. Third, the held-out schema subset makes the specific fields, formats, and repairs novel regardless of category familiarity; STC's CFR advantage persisting there (Section 3) is the stronger evidence against circularity, since memorized signatures cannot explain correct recovery on unfamiliar schemas.

For headline comparisons (STC vs. B1, STC vs. B2 on CFR) we run a paired bootstrap: resample the 420 tasks with replacement 10,000 times, recompute the CFR gap using per-seed-averaged predictions, and report the fraction of resamples where the sign flips. For the ablation table (Section 3), where six variants are each compared to STC full, we apply a Holm-Bonferroni correction across the six comparisons; we report both nominal and corrected p-values.

## 3. Results

| Method | TSR (%) | CFR (%) | RR (%) | Single-step tool acc. (%) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| B1 — Uncurated i.i.d. | 54.2 ± 1.4 | 61.0 ± 2.1 | 18.4 ± 1.8 | 82.1 ± 0.9 |
| B2 — Difficulty-only | 58.9 ± 1.1 | 46.3 ± 1.7 | 27.1 ± 1.6 | 83.6 ± 0.7 |
| B3 — Reward-only RL | 57.1 ± 1.9 | 50.7 ± 2.4 | 24.0 ± 2.2 | 81.4 ± 1.1 |
| B4 — B1 + inference-time self-correction | 58.6 ± 1.3 | 51.8 ± 2.0 | 29.7 ± 1.9 | 82.1 ± 0.9 (wrapper) |
| **STC (ours)** | **66.8 ± 1.2** | **36.0 ± 1.5** | **44.9 ± 1.7** | **85.2 ± 0.6** |

(± is standard deviation across 3 seeds; note this is a small-n estimate and complements, rather than substitutes for, the task-level paired bootstrap used for significance. CFR: lower is better. RR: higher is better.)

STC improves TSR by 12.6 points over B1 and 7.9 over B2, driven by CFR reduction (41% relative vs. B1, 22% vs. B2) and more than doubled RR vs. B1. The bootstrap test rejects the no-difference null for both comparisons (STC vs. B1: p < 0.001, sign stable across all resamples; STC vs. B2: p = 0.004, flips in 0.4% of resamples). Single-step tool accuracy improves only modestly (+3.1 points over B1), confirming most of STC's benefit is *recovery behavior*, not fewer primary errors. On the held-out schema subset, STC's CFR advantage narrows but persists (41.2 ± 2.3% vs. B2's 51.8 ± 2.0%, p = 0.01), indicating the self-check behavior transfers rather than collapsing to memorization.

**Isolating mechanism from training (B4).** With its dev-tuned configuration, the inference-time wrapper improves CFR over B1 by 9.2 points (61.0 → 51.8) — roughly a third of STC's 25.0-point gain — showing an explicit re-check-on-inconsistency step catches real errors regardless of training, and that this is not an artifact of an unfairly weak wrapper configuration. B4 still trails STC (51.8% vs. 36.0%) and even B2 (46.3%), and its RR (29.7%) trails STC's (44.9%). In manual inspection, B4's wrapper sometimes correctly flagged an inconsistency but the underlying B1 model, never trained on repair, frequently re-issued the *same* malformed call rather than a corrected one — staged training adds calibrated triggering and repair competence a fixed prompting wrapper cannot supply alone. A threshold sweep (1–4 steps) leaves method rankings unchanged, with the STC-vs-B1 gap ranging 22.4–26.1 points versus 25.0 at our default of 2.

**Ablations** (3 seeds each; corrected p-values are Holm-Bonferroni across the six rows):

| Variant | TSR (%) | CFR (%) | p vs. STC (nominal / corrected) |
|---|---|---|---|
| STC full | 66.8 ± 1.2 | 36.0 ± 1.5 | — |
| − Stage 3 (skip noisy-output stage) | 61.0 ± 1.5 | 45.9 ± 1.9 | 0.006 / 0.018 |
| − rollback loss (state summary kept, no error training) | 62.4 ± 1.4 | 44.1 ± 1.8 | 0.008 / 0.021 |
| − staged ordering (same data, shuffled) | 58.1 ± 1.6 | 49.5 ± 2.1 | 0.002 / 0.008 |
| Reversed order (hard→easy) | 55.7 ± 1.8 | 58.2 ± 2.3 | <0.001 / 0.002 |
| Random permutation (defers atomic to 3rd position) | 57.6 ± 1.9 | 50.9 ± 2.2 | 0.011 / 0.022 |
| Random permutation (front-loads atomic, else scrambled) | 60.2 ± 1.5 | 46.8 ± 1.8 | 0.017 / 0.022 |

All six comparisons remain significant after correction, though the two random-permutation rows and the deferred-vs-front-loaded atomic contrast (46.8% vs. 50.9%, a 4.1-point gap) are the least comfortably separated: checked per seed, the front-loaded variant has lower CFR than the deferred variant in all 3 seeds (gaps of 2.9, 4.6, and 4.8 points), so the ordering is consistent, not an artifact of averaging, though the margin is modest enough that we would not want to over-read the exact magnitude. Removing the noisy-output stage or the rollback loss each recovers about half the gap to B2, so the two are complementary: exposure to corrupted outputs alone is insufficient without a trained response. The permutation deferring atomic competence is worse than the one that still front-loads it, even though both deviate equally from canonical order otherwise — evidence the effect is about respecting increasing recoverability distance, not any deviation from canonical order being penalized equally. This also explains why shuffled STC (49.5%) sits so close to B2 (46.3%) despite still including rollback supervision B2 lacks: shuffling breaks the property that rollback training happens *after* atomic/chaining competence is reliable, so the state-summary block is often itself malformed early on and the rollback loss is partly trained against a noisy target.

**Qualitative failure analysis.** In 50 manually inspected B1 cascade failures, the modal pattern was silent continuation: a reconstructed state summary showing an obviously stale or contradictory belief (e.g., a user ID the last call had explicitly rejected) that was never revisited. STC's primary-error trajectories most often recovered via an explicit re-issued call flagging the prior output as inconsistent, matching the Stage 3 signal directly.

## 4. Conclusion

Error cascades — not raw per-step tool accuracy — are a major, separately addressable source of tool-use agent failure. Ordering training by recoverability distance, and explicitly training detection-and-rollback under noisy tool outputs before extending to long-horizon sparse-feedback tasks, substantially reduces cascade failures without more total data than standard pipelines. Ordering itself matters: difficulty-only curricula, reward-only RL, and a training-free inference-time self-correction wrapper — even tuned on the same dev split as our other hyperparameters — all leave real cascade-reduction benefit on the table, and scrambling the order is harmful whenever it defers atomic competence past the noisy-output and long-horizon stages. Results hold across 3 seeds and, with attenuated but still significant margins after multiple-comparison correction, on unseen tool schemas, and we find no evidence the eval suite's noise scheme or Stage 4's rejection-sampled data meaningfully overlap with Stage 3's training perturbations or the eval tasks themselves.

The paper's central open limitation is that every result rests on one 7B base model (Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2) and fully synthetic, mocked oracle environments; this revision adds tuning transparency and overlap checks but no second model or non-synthetic signal, so we do not yet know whether the curriculum effect is general or specific to this model scale and environment fidelity. Real tool environments also involve failure modes our four corruption categories don't model — rate-limit backoff semantics, partial writes, non-idempotent side effects. Validating CFR reduction on a second base model, on production-scale real-API agent logs, and against a broader set of inference-time correction strategies (with tuning budgets matched as we did here for B4) is the priority next step, ahead of any further extension of the curriculum design itself.
