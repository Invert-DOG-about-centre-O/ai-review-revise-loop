# A Curriculum for Tool-Use Agents That Reduces Error Cascades

## Abstract

Tool-use agents built on large language models often fail not on the first tool call but several steps later, when an early mistake — a malformed argument, a misread return value, a wrong tool choice — propagates through subsequent reasoning and compounds into task failure. We call this an *error cascade*. We propose **Staged Tool Curriculum (STC)**, a training curriculum that orders tool-use practice by *recoverability distance*: the number of downstream steps an error at a given stage typically corrupts before the agent can self-correct. STC begins with single-tool, single-step tasks with immediate ground-truth verification, then multi-tool chains, then noisy/adversarial tool outputs, and finally long-horizon tasks with delayed verification, interleaving explicit self-check and rollback exercises at each stage. We evaluate STC against four baselines — uncurated i.i.d. fine-tuning, a difficulty-only curriculum, reward-only RL, and a Reflexion-style inference-time self-correction wrapper on the uncurated model — on 420 tool-use tasks spanning API orchestration, code execution, and web-browsing-style retrieval. Across three training seeds, STC reduces cascade failures (task failures traceable to an early, uncorrected error) by 41% relative to the uncurated baseline and 22% relative to the difficulty-only curriculum (both significant under a paired bootstrap test, p < 0.01), while modestly improving raw single-step tool accuracy (+3.1 points). The inference-time wrapper recovers roughly a third of STC's cascade-reduction benefit with no retraining, showing self-check mechanisms help even without staging, but that staging captures gains a prompting-only wrapper cannot. Ablations show the rollback and noisy-output stages contribute independently, that ordering matters beyond simple reversal, and that the eval suite's corruption templates are held disjoint from Stage 3's training perturbations to guard against circularity.

## 1. Introduction

Tool-use agents — LLMs that call external functions, APIs, code interpreters, or retrieval systems in a loop — are increasingly deployed for tasks requiring many sequential, dependent actions. Unlike single-turn generation, tool-use is compounding: each step's output becomes part of the context for the next decision. An early, small error (an off-by-one in a query parameter, a misparsed JSON field, a hallucinated tool name) does not just produce one bad step — it silently corrupts the agent's belief about the "world state," and every later decision is conditioned on that corrupted belief. We call this an **error cascade**, distinct from a *terminal error* (a fatal mistake with no continuation) and an *independent error* (an unrelated mistake at another step).

Standard fine-tuning and RL pipelines optimize end-task success on a fixed distribution of tasks, sampled i.i.d. or ordered by naive difficulty (e.g., step count), giving the model little signal about *when* an error occurred versus when it merely *manifested*, and no dedicated practice at detecting a diverged trajectory. We hypothesize cascades are disproportionately a curriculum problem, not a capability problem: models that can perform each constituent skill (tool selection, argument formatting, output parsing, self-verification) in isolation still cascade when these skills are only trained jointly on undifferentiated full-length trajectories.

We propose **Staged Tool Curriculum (STC)**, ordering training data along *recoverability distance* rather than raw step count or holistic difficulty, in four stages: (1) **Atomic** — single tool call, immediate verification of tool choice, argument validity, and parsing; (2) **Chaining** — 2–5 dependent calls with clean outputs, with an explicit **state summary** ("what I now know / what I still need") supervised after each observation; (3) **Noisy-output** — same chains, but outputs are perturbed (truncation, HTTP errors, silent field renames, plausible-but-wrong substitutions), paired with a **rollback exercise**: flag the inconsistency against the state summary, repair, continue; (4) **Long-horizon** — 6–20 steps with only sparse, end-of-trajectory verification, testing whether the Stage 3 self-check habit transfers without step-level supervision.

A natural objection is that this design could simply reproduce, at training time, what our evaluation metric measures at test time — an artifact of matched perturbation schemes rather than genuine recovery ability. We address this by constructing the eval suite's corruption templates independently of the Stage 3 perturbations and quantifying overlap (Section 2.4), and by adding an inference-time-only baseline (Section 2.3, B4) that isolates how much of STC's benefit comes from *having* a self-check mechanism versus staged training that teaches *when and how* to use one.

Our contributions: (1) a formalization of error cascades distinct from terminal/independent errors, with a measurable metric (CFR); (2) the STC curriculum and training recipe; (3) a 420-task evaluation suite with per-step ground truth, whose corruption templates are held disjoint from training-time perturbations; (4) ablations isolating ordering (multiple permutations, not just reversal), noisy-output exposure, and rollback training, with seeds and significance testing; (5) a comparison against inference-time self-correction (Reflexion-style) to separate mechanism from training.

## 2. Method

### 2.1 Formalizing error cascades

We model a trajectory as $\tau = (s_0, a_1, o_1, s_1, \dots, s_T)$, where $s_t$ is the agent's *believed* state after step $t$ and $a_t$ a tool call, against a ground-truth sequence $s_t^*$ from the task's oracle simulator. A step $t$ is a **primary error** if $s_t$ diverges from $s_t^*$ on a task-relevant predicate, and induces a **cascade** if the trajectory ultimately fails and no later step both detects the divergence and restores $s_{t'}\approx s_{t'}^*$ (or reaches the goal via an alternate valid path).

We report **Task Success Rate (TSR)** (fraction reaching the goal); **Cascade Failure Rate (CFR)**: fraction of *failed* trajectories attributable to an uncorrected primary error at least 2 steps before the final step; and **Recovery Rate (RR)**: of trajectories with a primary error, the fraction that still succeed. The 2-step threshold was fixed a priori as the smallest gap guaranteeing a full observe/re-plan/act cycle after the error, not tuned against our results; a sensitivity sweep (1–4 steps, Section 3) leaves method rankings unchanged.

### 2.2 Curriculum stages and training details

Stage sizes: Atomic ~4,000 examples over 30 synthetic tool schemas (REST-style APIs, a Python execution tool, a search tool), SFT plus a binary call/parse-correctness auxiliary loss. Chaining ~6,000 examples (e.g., look up a user, fetch orders, compute a total), clean outputs, with the state-summary supervision above. Noisy-output: same task family, 35% of outputs perturbed with ground-truth corruption labels; rollback target is "flag inconsistency, repair, continue," with loss on both detection and corrected continuation. The 35% rate came from a pilot sweep (15/25/35/50%) on a dev split disjoint from the training pool and the 420-task suite: dev CFR improved to 35% and plateaued (within 0.6 points) beyond it, so we fixed the lowest rate on the plateau. Long-horizon: ~2,500 examples, 6–20 steps, sparse end-of-trajectory verification only, using STaR-style rejection sampling over successful rollouts, introduced only after atomic/chaining competence is established.

Stages train sequentially, each initialized from the prior checkpoint. All conditions use Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 as the base model. SFT uses AdamW (β1=0.9, β2=0.95, weight decay 0.1), peak LR 1e-5 with 3% linear warmup and cosine decay, effective batch size 64 (4096-token packed sequences), 1 epoch per stage — identical across B1, B2, and STC to isolate the curriculum variable.

### 2.3 Baselines

- **B1 — Uncurated i.i.d.**: same pooled data, shuffled, single pass (4 epochs, matching STC's total gradient steps), no staging or self-check supervision.
- **B2 — Difficulty-only**: staged purely by step count, clean outputs except an unsupervised noise pass in the final stage; same optimizer/schedule as STC.
- **B3 — Reward-only RL**: outcome-only RL (sparse +1/0 reward) via best-of-8 rejection sampling at temperature 0.8, KL penalty β=0.05 against the SFT-B1 checkpoint; β and best-of-k swept on the same dev split used to tune the Stage-3 noise rate, reporting the highest-dev-TSR configuration.
- **B4 — Inference-time self-correction (Reflexion-style)**: the unmodified B1 checkpoint wrapped at inference time — after each observation, a second prompt asks whether the returned state is consistent with the running task summary; a flagged inconsistency discards the last action and allows up to 2 re-sampled attempts. No additional training; isolates the value of *having* a self-check step from staged training in *when and how* to use one.

Each trained condition uses 3 seeds (data/sampling seeds only; task pool and eval suite fixed); B4 needs no training and is evaluated once per B1 seed.

### 2.4 Evaluation suite

420 tasks with instrumented oracle simulators across API orchestration (180), code execution (120), and retrieval/browsing (120, distractor pages, stale/incorrect snippets). Each domain holds out 30% of tool schemas and task templates unseen during any curriculum stage, testing generalization rather than memorization.

Because Stage 3 trains detection/recovery from injected corruptions, an eval suite mirroring Stage 3's noise scheme risks measuring pattern-matching rather than general recovery. We mitigate this two ways. First, the eval corruption generator was built independently, by a different task author with a different template library; the two share corruption *categories* (truncation, HTTP errors, field renaming, near-miss substitution) but not surface realizations: 0 of 640 eval corruption instances are byte-identical to a training instance, and an embedding-similarity check (cosine ≥0.95) flags only 4.2% as near-duplicates, concentrated in the low-variation HTTP-error category — restricting CFR to the non-near-duplicate 95.8% shifts STC's CFR from 36.0% to 36.4% and B1's from 61.0% to 61.3%, a negligible change. Second, the held-out schema subset makes the specific fields, formats, and repairs novel regardless of category familiarity; STC's CFR advantage persisting there (Section 3) is the stronger evidence against circularity, since memorized signatures cannot explain correct recovery on unfamiliar schemas.

For headline comparisons (STC vs. B1, STC vs. B2 on CFR) we run a paired bootstrap: resample the 420 tasks with replacement 10,000 times, recompute the CFR gap using per-seed-averaged predictions, and report the fraction of resamples where the sign flips.

## 3. Results

| Method | TSR (%) | CFR (%) | RR (%) | Single-step tool acc. (%) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| B1 — Uncurated i.i.d. | 54.2 ± 1.4 | 61.0 ± 2.1 | 18.4 ± 1.8 | 82.1 ± 0.9 |
| B2 — Difficulty-only | 58.9 ± 1.1 | 46.3 ± 1.7 | 27.1 ± 1.6 | 83.6 ± 0.7 |
| B3 — Reward-only RL | 57.1 ± 1.9 | 50.7 ± 2.4 | 24.0 ± 2.2 | 81.4 ± 1.1 |
| B4 — B1 + inference-time self-correction | 58.6 ± 1.3 | 51.8 ± 2.0 | 29.7 ± 1.9 | 82.1 ± 0.9 (wrapper) |
| **STC (ours)** | **66.8 ± 1.2** | **36.0 ± 1.5** | **44.9 ± 1.7** | **85.2 ± 0.6** |

(± is standard deviation across 3 seeds. CFR: lower is better. RR: higher is better.)

STC improves TSR by 12.6 points over B1 and 7.9 over B2, driven by CFR reduction (41% relative vs. B1, 22% vs. B2) and more than doubled RR vs. B1. The bootstrap test rejects the no-difference null for both comparisons (STC vs. B1: p < 0.001, sign stable across all resamples; STC vs. B2: p = 0.004, flips in 0.4% of resamples). Single-step tool accuracy improves only modestly (+3.1 points over B1), confirming most of STC's benefit is *recovery behavior*, not fewer primary errors. On the held-out schema subset, STC's CFR advantage narrows but persists (41.2 ± 2.3% vs. B2's 51.8 ± 2.0%, p = 0.01), indicating the self-check behavior transfers rather than collapsing to memorization.

**Isolating mechanism from training (B4).** The inference-time wrapper improves CFR over B1 by 9.2 points (61.0 → 51.8) — roughly a third of STC's 25.0-point gain — with no retraining, showing an explicit re-check-on-inconsistency step catches real errors regardless of training. But B4 still trails STC (51.8% vs. 36.0%) and even B2 (46.3%), and its RR (29.7%) trails STC's (44.9%). In manual inspection, B4's wrapper sometimes correctly flagged an inconsistency but the underlying B1 model, never trained on repair, frequently re-issued the *same* malformed call rather than a corrected one — staged training adds calibrated triggering and repair competence a fixed prompting wrapper cannot supply alone. A threshold sweep (1–4 steps) leaves method rankings unchanged, with the STC-vs-B1 gap ranging 22.4–26.1 points versus 25.0 at our default of 2.

**Ablations** (3 seeds each):

| Variant | TSR (%) | CFR (%) |
|---|---|---|
| STC full | 66.8 ± 1.2 | 36.0 ± 1.5 |
| − Stage 3 (skip noisy-output stage) | 61.0 ± 1.5 | 45.9 ± 1.9 |
| − rollback loss (state summary kept, no error training) | 62.4 ± 1.4 | 44.1 ± 1.8 |
| − staged ordering (same data, shuffled) | 58.1 ± 1.6 | 49.5 ± 2.1 |
| Reversed order (hard→easy) | 55.7 ± 1.8 | 58.2 ± 2.3 |
| Random permutation (defers atomic to 3rd position) | 57.6 ± 1.9 | 50.9 ± 2.2 |
| Random permutation (front-loads atomic, else scrambled) | 60.2 ± 1.5 | 46.8 ± 1.8 |

Removing the noisy-output stage or the rollback loss each recovers about half the gap to B2, so the two are complementary: exposure to corrupted outputs alone is insufficient without a trained response. Two further random permutations beyond reversal both underperform the canonical order by 6–15 CFR points (p < 0.02 each); the permutation deferring atomic competence is worse than the one that still front-loads it, even though both deviate equally from canonical order otherwise — evidence the effect is about respecting increasing recoverability distance, not any deviation from canonical order being penalized equally. This also explains why shuffled STC (49.5%) sits so close to B2 (46.3%) despite still including rollback supervision B2 lacks: shuffling breaks the property that rollback training happens *after* atomic/chaining competence is reliable, so the state-summary block is often itself malformed early on and the rollback loss is partly trained against a noisy target.

**Qualitative failure analysis.** In 50 manually inspected B1 cascade failures, the modal pattern was silent continuation: a reconstructed state summary showing an obviously stale or contradictory belief (e.g., a user ID the last call had explicitly rejected) that was never revisited. STC's primary-error trajectories most often recovered via an explicit re-issued call flagging the prior output as inconsistent, matching the Stage 3 signal directly.

## 4. Conclusion

Error cascades — not raw per-step tool accuracy — are a major, separately addressable source of tool-use agent failure. Ordering training by recoverability distance, and explicitly training detection-and-rollback under noisy tool outputs before extending to long-horizon sparse-feedback tasks, substantially reduces cascade failures without more total data than standard pipelines. Ordering itself matters: difficulty-only curricula, reward-only RL, and a training-free inference-time self-correction wrapper all leave real cascade-reduction benefit on the table, and scrambling the order is harmful whenever it defers atomic competence past the noisy-output and long-horizon stages. Results hold across 3 seeds and, with attenuated but still significant margins, on unseen tool schemas, and we find no evidence the eval suite's noise scheme meaningfully overlaps with Stage 3's training perturbations. Two natural extensions: (1) applying STC-style staging within RL objectives directly rather than SFT-then-nothing; (2) using the learned state-summary representation as a lightweight runtime monitor for deployed agents, independent of retraining — a direction the B4 comparison suggests is promising but insufficient alone, since a self-check signal without staged repair training recovers only part of STC's benefit. The main remaining limitation is reliance on synthetic/mocked tool environments with programmatic oracles, which may still understate real-world tool failure diversity (rate-limit backoff semantics, partial writes, non-idempotent side effects, corruption patterns beyond the four modeled here); validating CFR reduction on production-scale real-API agent logs, and against a broader set of inference-time correction strategies, is the priority next step.
