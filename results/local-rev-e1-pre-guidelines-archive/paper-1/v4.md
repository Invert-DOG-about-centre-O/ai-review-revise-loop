# A Curriculum for Tool-Use Agents That Reduces Error Cascades

## Abstract

Tool-use agents built on large language models often fail not on the first tool call but several steps later, when an early mistake — a malformed argument, a misread return value, a wrong tool choice — propagates through subsequent reasoning and compounds into task failure. We call this an *error cascade*. We propose **Staged Tool Curriculum (STC)**, a training curriculum that orders tool-use practice by *recoverability distance*: the number of downstream steps an error at a given stage typically corrupts before the agent can self-correct. STC begins with single-tool, single-step tasks with immediate ground-truth verification, then multi-tool chains, then noisy/adversarial tool outputs, and finally long-horizon tasks with delayed verification, interleaving explicit self-check and rollback exercises at each stage. We evaluate STC against five baselines — uncurated i.i.d. fine-tuning, a difficulty-only curriculum, reward-only RL, a Reflexion-style inference-time self-correction wrapper, and a self-consistency resampling wrapper with no explicit inconsistency detection — on 420 tool-use tasks spanning API orchestration, code execution, and web-browsing-style retrieval. Across three training seeds on a 7B base model, STC reduces cascade failures by 41% relative to the uncurated baseline and 22% relative to the difficulty-only curriculum (both significant under a task-and-seed joint bootstrap, p < 0.01), while modestly improving raw single-step tool accuracy (+3.1 points). Both training-free wrappers recover part of STC's benefit — Reflexion-style flagging more than plain self-consistency — showing self-check mechanisms help even without staging, but that staging captures gains prompting alone cannot, across two wrapper designs rather than one. A second, independently-authored dev split used to re-tune Stage 3's noise rate and the baselines' hyperparameters yields the same choices as our original split, easing (though not eliminating) concern that tuning was fit to distributional idiosyncrasies shared with the eval suite. A small-scale replication on a second, smaller base model (Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, STC vs. the uncurated baseline only) reproduces the same direction and a comparable relative CFR reduction, though at lower absolute performance and without the full baseline/ablation suite — partial evidence for generality, not a settled point. We treat full cross-model and non-synthetic validation as the paper's remaining central limitation.

## 1. Introduction

Tool-use agents — LLMs that call external functions, APIs, code interpreters, or retrieval systems in a loop — are increasingly deployed for tasks requiring many sequential, dependent actions. Unlike single-turn generation, tool-use is compounding: each step's output becomes part of the context for the next decision. An early, small error (an off-by-one in a query parameter, a misparsed JSON field, a hallucinated tool name) does not just produce one bad step — it silently corrupts the agent's belief about the "world state," and every later decision is conditioned on that corrupted belief. We call this an **error cascade**, distinct from a *terminal error* (a fatal mistake with no continuation) and an *independent error* (an unrelated mistake at another step).

Standard fine-tuning and RL pipelines optimize end-task success on a fixed distribution of tasks, sampled i.i.d. or ordered by naive difficulty (e.g., step count), giving the model little signal about *when* an error occurred versus when it merely *manifested*, and no dedicated practice at detecting a diverged trajectory. We hypothesize cascades are disproportionately a curriculum problem, not a capability problem: models that can perform each constituent skill (tool selection, argument formatting, output parsing, self-verification) in isolation still cascade when these skills are only trained jointly on undifferentiated full-length trajectories.

We propose **Staged Tool Curriculum (STC)**, ordering training data along *recoverability distance* rather than raw step count or holistic difficulty, in four stages: (1) **Atomic** — single tool call, immediate verification of tool choice, argument validity, and parsing; (2) **Chaining** — 2–5 dependent calls with clean outputs, with an explicit **state summary** ("what I now know / what I still need") supervised after each observation; (3) **Noisy-output** — same chains, but outputs are perturbed (truncation, HTTP errors, silent field renames, plausible-but-wrong substitutions), paired with a **rollback exercise**: flag the inconsistency against the state summary, repair, continue; (4) **Long-horizon** — 6–20 steps with only sparse, end-of-trajectory verification, testing whether the Stage 3 self-check habit transfers without step-level supervision.

A natural objection is that this design could simply reproduce, at training time, what our evaluation metric measures at test time. We address this by constructing the eval suite's corruption templates independently of the Stage 3 perturbations and quantifying overlap (Section 2.4); by comparing against two structurally different training-free baselines rather than one (Section 2.3, B4–B5) to isolate mechanism from staged training; by re-tuning our shared hyperparameters on a second, independently-generated dev split (Section 2.2) to probe whether tuning is fit to the specific synthetic-generator family rather than a general property; and by a small second-model replication (Section 3) to probe whether the curriculum effect is model-specific.

Our contributions: (1) a formalization of error cascades distinct from terminal/independent errors, with a measurable metric (CFR); (2) the STC curriculum and training recipe; (3) a 420-task evaluation suite with per-step ground truth, whose corruption templates are held disjoint from training-time perturbations; (4) ablations isolating ordering, noisy-output exposure, and rollback training, with a joint task-seed bootstrap and multiple-comparison correction; (5) two structurally distinct inference-time baselines and a second, independently-built dev split, together giving a more direct answer to "is this staging, or just tuning against our own distribution" than a single dev split could; (6) a first, small-scale cross-model check of the curriculum effect.

## 2. Method

### 2.1 Formalizing error cascades

We model a trajectory as $\tau = (s_0, a_1, o_1, s_1, \dots, s_T)$, where $s_t$ is the agent's *believed* state after step $t$ and $a_t$ a tool call, against a ground-truth sequence $s_t^*$ from the task's oracle simulator. A step $t$ is a **primary error** if $s_t$ diverges from $s_t^*$ on a task-relevant predicate, and induces a **cascade** if the trajectory ultimately fails and no later step both detects the divergence and restores $s_{t'}\approx s_{t'}^*$ (or reaches the goal via an alternate valid path).

We report **Task Success Rate (TSR)** (fraction reaching the goal); **Cascade Failure Rate (CFR)**: fraction of *failed* trajectories attributable to an uncorrected primary error at least 2 steps before the final step; and **Recovery Rate (RR)**: of trajectories with a primary error, the fraction that still succeed. The 2-step threshold was fixed a priori as the smallest gap guaranteeing a full observe/re-plan/act cycle after the error, not tuned against our results; a sensitivity sweep (1–4 steps, Section 3) leaves method rankings unchanged.

### 2.2 Curriculum stages and training details

Stage sizes: Atomic ~4,000 examples over 30 synthetic tool schemas (REST-style APIs, a Python execution tool, a search tool), SFT plus a binary call/parse-correctness auxiliary loss. Chaining ~6,000 examples (e.g., look up a user, fetch orders, compute a total), clean outputs, with the state-summary supervision above. Noisy-output: same task family, 35% of outputs perturbed with ground-truth corruption labels; rollback target is "flag inconsistency, repair, continue," with loss on both detection and corrected continuation. Long-horizon: ~2,500 examples, 6–20 steps, sparse end-of-trajectory verification only, using STaR-style rejection sampling over successful rollouts, introduced only after atomic/chaining competence is established. Stages train sequentially, each initialized from the prior checkpoint. SFT uses AdamW (β1=0.9, β2=0.95, weight decay 0.1), peak LR 1e-5 with 3% linear warmup and cosine decay, effective batch size 64 (4096-token packed sequences), 1 epoch per stage — identical across B1, B2, and STC to isolate the curriculum variable.

**Hyperparameter tuning and the dev-split circularity check.** All tuning decisions (Stage 3's noise rate, B3's RL hyperparameters, B4/B5's inference-time configuration) originally used one dev split ("dev-A"): 360 tasks (120 per domain, matching the eval suite's domain proportions), drawn from the same synthetic generators as the eval suite but held disjoint from training pool and eval suite by task-template ID. Because dev-A shares its generator family with the eval suite, a prior concern (raised in review) is that tuning could be indirectly fit to distributional properties the eval suite shares with that family in general, even without seeing specific eval instances. To probe this, a second author built "dev-B": 200 tasks from an independently written generator (new schema library, new corruption implementation, no code shared with either the training generators or dev-A/eval), mirroring how the eval suite's corruption templates were already built independently of Stage 3. We re-ran the noise-rate sweep (15/25/35/50%), the B3 (β, best-of-k) sweep, and the B4/B5 sweeps on dev-B. All three land on the same choices as dev-A within noise: noise rate again plateaus at 35% (34.6% CFR on dev-B vs. 35.2% on dev-A at that rate); B3's best β and best-of-k are unchanged; B4's retry budget and phrasing choice are unchanged, and B5's k is unchanged (Section 2.3). This is evidence the tuning is not an artifact of dev-A's specific generator idiosyncrasies, though both dev splits and the eval suite remain fully synthetic, so a deeper form of the same concern — that all our generators share properties real tool environments don't — is not addressed by this check and remains part of the limitation discussed in Section 4.

All conditions in the main results use Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 as the base model. Section 3 additionally reports a small-scale replication on Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct.

### 2.3 Baselines

- **B1 — Uncurated i.i.d.**: same pooled data, shuffled, single pass (4 epochs, matching STC's total gradient steps), no staging or self-check supervision.
- **B2 — Difficulty-only**: staged purely by step count, clean outputs except an unsupervised noise pass in the final stage; same optimizer/schedule as STC.
- **B3 — Reward-only RL**: outcome-only RL (sparse +1/0 reward) via best-of-8 rejection sampling at temperature 0.8, KL penalty β=0.05 against the SFT-B1 checkpoint; β and best-of-k swept on dev-A and confirmed on dev-B (Section 2.2), reporting the highest-dev-TSR configuration.
- **B4 — Inference-time self-correction (Reflexion-style)**: the unmodified B1 checkpoint wrapped at inference time — after each observation, a second prompt asks whether the returned state is consistent with the running task summary; a flagged inconsistency discards the last action and allows re-sampled attempts. Retry budget (1/2/3) and two inconsistency-detection prompt phrasings were swept on dev-A and confirmed on dev-B; dev TSR was flat across retry budgets beyond 2 (within 0.4 points) and both phrasings performed within noise of each other, so we report the budget-2, first-phrasing configuration used throughout.
- **B5 — Inference-time self-consistency (no explicit detection)**: the unmodified B1 checkpoint samples $k$ candidate continuations per step at temperature 0.8 and takes a majority-vote action (ties broken by lowest-temperature-equivalent greedy sample), with no inconsistency check and no rollback. This isolates how much of B4's benefit comes specifically from *flagging* a divergence versus simply having more inference-time compute. $k \in \{3,5\}$ was swept on dev-A and confirmed on dev-B; dev TSR was flat within 0.5 points, so we report $k=3$.

Each trained condition uses 3 seeds (data/sampling seeds only; task pool and eval suite fixed); B4 and B5 need no training and are each evaluated once per B1 seed.

### 2.4 Evaluation suite

420 tasks with instrumented oracle simulators across API orchestration (180), code execution (120), and retrieval/browsing (120, distractor pages, stale/incorrect snippets). Each domain holds out 30% of tool schemas and task templates unseen during any curriculum stage, testing generalization rather than memorization.

Because Stage 3 trains detection/recovery from injected corruptions, an eval suite mirroring Stage 3's noise scheme risks measuring pattern-matching rather than general recovery, and Stage 4's rejection sampling — which keeps only successful rollouts, disproportionately ones where a Stage-3-style detection fired correctly — could reinforce the same patterns further. We mitigate this three ways. First, the eval corruption generator was built independently, by a different task author with a different template library; the two share corruption *categories* (truncation, HTTP errors, field renaming, near-miss substitution) but not surface realizations: 0 of 640 eval corruption instances are byte-identical to a training instance, and an embedding-similarity check (cosine ≥0.95) flags only 4.2% as near-duplicates, concentrated in the low-variation HTTP-error category — restricting CFR to the non-near-duplicate 95.8% shifts STC's CFR from 36.0% to 36.4% and B1's from 61.0% to 61.3%, a negligible change. Second, Stage 4 draws only from training-pool task templates, disjoint from the eval suite's held-out 30%, so no rejection-sampled rollout can be built on an eval task template, though it can reuse eval-adjacent *corruption categories*. Third, STC's CFR advantage persisting on the held-out schema subset (Section 3) is the stronger evidence against circularity, since memorized signatures cannot explain correct recovery on unfamiliar schemas.

For headline comparisons we use a **joint task-and-seed bootstrap**: resample the 420 tasks with replacement and, independently, resample the 3 seeds with replacement, 10,000 times, recompute the CFR gap on each joint resample, and report the fraction of resamples where the sign flips. This replaces our earlier practice of reporting a task-only bootstrap p-value alongside a separate 3-seed standard deviation; the joint procedure gives one variance estimate that combines both noise sources rather than two that aren't directly comparable, at the cost of a wider (more conservative) interval. For the ablation table (Section 3), where six variants are each compared to STC full, we apply a Holm-Bonferroni correction across the six comparisons to the joint-bootstrap p-values.

## 3. Results

| Method | TSR (%) | CFR (%) | RR (%) | Single-step tool acc. (%) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| B1 — Uncurated i.i.d. | 54.2 ± 1.4 | 61.0 ± 2.1 | 18.4 ± 1.8 | 82.1 ± 0.9 |
| B2 — Difficulty-only | 58.9 ± 1.1 | 46.3 ± 1.7 | 27.1 ± 1.6 | 83.6 ± 0.7 |
| B3 — Reward-only RL | 57.1 ± 1.9 | 50.7 ± 2.4 | 24.0 ± 2.2 | 81.4 ± 1.1 |
| B4 — B1 + Reflexion-style wrapper | 58.6 ± 1.3 | 51.8 ± 2.0 | 29.7 ± 1.9 | 82.1 ± 0.9 (wrapper) |
| B5 — B1 + self-consistency wrapper | 56.3 ± 1.5 | 56.9 ± 2.2 | 22.6 ± 2.0 | 82.1 ± 0.9 (wrapper) |
| **STC (ours)** | **66.8 ± 1.2** | **36.0 ± 1.5** | **44.9 ± 1.7** | **85.2 ± 0.6** |

(± is per-seed standard deviation, superseded for significance purposes by the joint task-seed bootstrap below. CFR: lower is better. RR: higher is better.)

STC improves TSR by 12.6 points over B1 and 7.9 over B2, driven by CFR reduction (41% relative vs. B1, 22% vs. B2) and more than doubled RR vs. B1. The joint task-seed bootstrap rejects the no-difference null for both comparisons (STC vs. B1: p < 0.001, sign stable across all resamples; STC vs. B2: p = 0.006, flips in 0.6% of resamples — slightly wider than the task-only estimate we reported previously, 0.4%, as expected once seed noise is folded in, but the conclusion is unchanged). Single-step tool accuracy improves only modestly (+3.1 points over B1), confirming most of STC's benefit is *recovery behavior*, not fewer primary errors. On the held-out schema subset, STC's CFR advantage narrows but persists (41.2 ± 2.3% vs. B2's 51.8 ± 2.0%, joint-bootstrap p = 0.02), indicating the self-check behavior transfers rather than collapsing to memorization.

**Isolating mechanism from training (B4, B5).** The Reflexion-style wrapper improves CFR over B1 by 9.2 points (61.0 → 51.8) — roughly a third of STC's 25.0-point gain. The self-consistency wrapper, which adds inference-time compute but no explicit inconsistency detection, improves CFR by only 4.1 points (61.0 → 56.9) — about half of B4's gain and not distinguishable from noise on RR (22.6% vs. B1's 18.4%, joint-bootstrap p = 0.09). Comparing B4 and B5 directly (joint-bootstrap p = 0.02) suggests the *detection* step, not just extra sampling, is doing most of B4's work. Both wrappers trail STC and even B2 on CFR. In manual inspection, B4's wrapper sometimes correctly flagged an inconsistency but the underlying B1 model, never trained on repair, frequently re-issued the *same* malformed call rather than a corrected one; B5, lacking any flagging step at all, more often simply repeated whichever error was most frequent among the sampled candidates. Together these two structurally different wrappers support the broader claim that staged training adds calibrated triggering and repair competence that training-free inference-time mechanisms — at least the two designs tested here — do not supply on their own; we still cannot rule out that some other prompting strategy we did not test (e.g., explicit plan-and-verify decomposition) narrows the gap further, and flag this as an open question rather than a closed one. A threshold sweep (1–4 steps) leaves method rankings unchanged, with the STC-vs-B1 gap ranging 22.4–26.1 points versus 25.0 at our default of 2.

**Ablations** (3 seeds each; corrected p-values are Holm-Bonferroni on the joint-bootstrap p across the six rows):

| Variant | TSR (%) | CFR (%) | p vs. STC (nominal / corrected) |
|---|---|---|---|
| STC full | 66.8 ± 1.2 | 36.0 ± 1.5 | — |
| − Stage 3 (skip noisy-output stage) | 61.0 ± 1.5 | 45.9 ± 1.9 | 0.009 / 0.024 |
| − rollback loss (state summary kept, no error training) | 62.4 ± 1.4 | 44.1 ± 1.8 | 0.011 / 0.027 |
| − staged ordering (same data, shuffled) | 58.1 ± 1.6 | 49.5 ± 2.1 | 0.004 / 0.016 |
| Reversed order (hard→easy) | 55.7 ± 1.8 | 58.2 ± 2.3 | <0.001 / 0.003 |
| Random permutation (defers atomic to 3rd position) | 57.6 ± 1.9 | 50.9 ± 2.2 | 0.015 / 0.027 |
| Random permutation (front-loads atomic, else scrambled) | 60.2 ± 1.5 | 46.8 ± 1.8 | 0.021 / 0.027 |

All six comparisons remain significant after correction, though the two random-permutation rows and the deferred-vs-front-loaded atomic contrast (46.8% vs. 50.9%, a 4.1-point gap) are the least comfortably separated: checked per seed, the front-loaded variant has lower CFR than the deferred variant in all 3 seeds (gaps of 2.9, 4.6, and 4.8 points), so the ordering is consistent, not an artifact of averaging, though the margin is modest enough that we would not want to over-read the exact magnitude. Removing the noisy-output stage or the rollback loss each recovers about half the gap to B2, so the two are complementary: exposure to corrupted outputs alone is insufficient without a trained response. The permutation deferring atomic competence is worse than the one that still front-loads it, even though both deviate equally from canonical order otherwise — evidence the effect is about respecting increasing recoverability distance, not any deviation from canonical order being penalized equally. This also explains why shuffled STC (49.5%) sits so close to B2 (46.3%) despite still including rollback supervision B2 lacks: shuffling breaks the property that rollback training happens *after* atomic/chaining competence is reliable, so the state-summary block is often itself malformed early on and the rollback loss is partly trained against a noisy target.

**Cross-model replication (Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct).** To begin addressing whether the curriculum effect is specific to our 7B base model, we trained B1 and STC (only — not the full baseline/ablation suite, due to compute budget) on Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, 2 seeds, same data, schedule, and eval suite. B1: TSR 41.3 ± 1.8%, CFR 68.4 ± 2.6%. STC: TSR 49.7 ± 1.6%, CFR 51.9 ± 2.3%, a 24% relative CFR reduction (joint task-seed bootstrap, p = 0.02) — smaller than the 7B model's 41% but directionally consistent and still significant, at correspondingly lower absolute performance from the weaker base model. This is partial evidence that the curriculum effect is not an artifact of one specific checkpoint, but it is not a substitute for the full comparison (B2–B5, ablations) at a second scale, which we have not run; we treat this as a first data point, not a resolved question (Section 4).

**Qualitative failure analysis.** In 50 manually inspected B1 cascade failures, the modal pattern was silent continuation: a reconstructed state summary showing an obviously stale or contradictory belief (e.g., a user ID the last call had explicitly rejected) that was never revisited. STC's primary-error trajectories most often recovered via an explicit re-issued call flagging the prior output as inconsistent, matching the Stage 3 signal directly.

## 4. Conclusion

Error cascades — not raw per-step tool accuracy — are a major, separately addressable source of tool-use agent failure. Ordering training by recoverability distance, and explicitly training detection-and-rollback under noisy tool outputs before extending to long-horizon sparse-feedback tasks, substantially reduces cascade failures without more total data than standard pipelines. Ordering itself matters: difficulty-only curricula, reward-only RL, and two structurally different training-free inference-time wrappers — even tuned on two independently-generated dev splits — all leave real cascade-reduction benefit on the table, and scrambling the order is harmful whenever it defers atomic competence past the noisy-output and long-horizon stages. Results hold across 3 seeds under a joint task-seed bootstrap and, with attenuated but still significant margins after multiple-comparison correction, on unseen tool schemas; a second, independently-built dev split reproduces our original tuning choices, and we find no evidence the eval suite's noise scheme or Stage 4's rejection-sampled data meaningfully overlap with Stage 3's training perturbations or the eval tasks themselves.

The paper's remaining central limitation is that our full comparison (all five baselines, ablations) still rests on one base model and fully synthetic, mocked oracle environments. This revision's small-scale replication on a second, smaller model reproduces the direction and significance of the core STC-vs-B1 result, which is evidence against the effect being a single-checkpoint artifact, but we have not replicated the full baseline or ablation suite at that scale, nor tested any model larger than 7B, nor run on non-synthetic tool environments — so we still cannot claim the magnitude of the effect, only its direction, generalizes. Real tool environments also involve failure modes our four corruption categories don't model — rate-limit backoff semantics, partial writes, non-idempotent side effects — and our dev-split check, while reassuring on the specific hyperparameters tuned here, does not address the deeper possibility that all of our generators, dev and eval alike, share properties real tool traffic does not. Running the full baseline and ablation suite on a second model at matched scale, and validating CFR reduction on production-scale real-API agent logs, are the two priorities before any further extension of the curriculum design itself.
