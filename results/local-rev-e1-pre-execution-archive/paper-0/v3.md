# The Perception–Behavior Gap in AI Disclosure Labels: A Paired-Outcome Protocol for Auditing Transparency Interventions

## Abstract

Regulators and platforms increasingly require that AI-generated or AI-assisted content be labeled, on the assumption that disclosure lets people calibrate how much to trust or rely on a system. Most empirical evaluations of these labels measure only self-reported outcomes — trust ratings, perceived credibility, perceived objectivity — and infer that a shift in these ratings implies a shift in how people actually use AI output. Recent findings complicate this inference: labels warning about AI sycophancy shifted participants' stated perceptions of an AI system without measurably reducing the AI's actual influence on their judgments, and several AI-generated-content (AIGC) labeling studies report similar dissociations between what people say and what they do. We argue this "perception–behavior gap" is under-measured because self-report and behavioral outcomes are rarely collected from the same participants under the same manipulation with a common effect-size scale. We propose a paired-outcome protocol — a judge–advisor task in which every participant provides both a stated-trust rating and a revealed-reliance measure (weight of advice, adjusted for advice correctness) — and a summary statistic, the Perception–Behavior Gap (PBG), that quantifies how much a label's effect on stated trust outruns its effect on behavioral calibration. We are explicit that PBG is a screening and communication statistic, not a certified effect size: we treat it as load-bearing for motivating the protocol but not as a number that should be interpreted without its companion diagnostics (reliability-corrected PBG, common-language effect sizes, and separate confidence intervals on each component), and we show that the protocol's substantive contribution — the paired measurement design and the discrimination index D — survives even if PBG itself is dropped in favor of always reporting the components separately. We instantiate the protocol in a 2 (advice correctness: correct/incorrect) × 4 (label condition: none, source-only, source+confidence, source+rationale) mixed design, add a manipulation check that distinguishes "rationale as caution" from "rationale as persuasion," identify an existing body of judge–advisor-system (JAS) datasets with correctness-conditioned advice that could partially validate D's psychometric properties before a full study is run, and walk through two contrasting worked numerical scenarios — one in which the gap closes and one in which it does not — presented with matched detail to show the protocol is informative either way. We discuss why the one existing large dataset with a documented perception–behavior dissociation cannot be repurposed to validate PBG directly, what partial validation is possible now versus what requires new data collection, and how the protocol generalizes to other disclosure and transparency interventions.

## 1. Introduction

Disclosure requirements — labeling text, images, or decisions as AI-generated or AI-assisted — are one of the most widely deployed socio-technical interventions in current AI governance. The EU AI Act, platform policies (e.g., on synthetic media), and journalistic norms all converge on a simple theory of change: telling people that content or advice came from an AI system will let them apply appropriate skepticism, improving the calibration of trust and reliance. This theory is testable, and a growing empirical literature has tested it.

### 1.1 Related work

**Disclosure effects on stated perception.** A large body of survey- and vignette-based work manipulates whether content is labeled as AI-generated and measures self-reported trust, perceived credibility, and sharing intention. Findings are mixed and often small in magnitude: some studies find labeling AI-generated messages does not reduce their persuasive effects on stated attitudes (Kreps & Kriner-style paradigms; see the PNAS Nexus and PMC replications of "Labeling messages as AI-generated does not reduce their persuasive effects"), while others find disclosure lowers perceived credibility or trust to varying degrees depending on content type and framing (e.g., studies on AIGC labels and misinformation sharing intentions, and on prosocial-advertising evaluation). A recurring theme across this literature is the "transparency dilemma": disclosure can simultaneously satisfy a normative demand for honesty while failing to produce the downstream behavioral caution regulators intend.

**Disclosure effects on behavior.** A smaller set of studies goes beyond stated attitudes to measure actual reliance — e.g., EEG/physiological responses to AIGC-labeled news, or decision tasks where participants act on AI advice. The most directly relevant recent result is a large preregistered experiment (N ≈ 2,610) showing that warning labels about AI sycophancy shifted participants' *perceptions* of the AI (lower perceived objectivity and trust) but did not reliably reduce the AI's *influence* on participants' self-perceived rightness or their willingness to repair conflicts — i.e., perception moved, behavior did not. This is the clearest existing demonstration of a perception–behavior dissociation under a disclosure-style intervention, but it studies one label design in one domain (conflict advice) and does not offer a general protocol or a summary statistic other researchers can reuse to test whether their own labels exhibit the same dissociation. Critically for our purposes, that study's behavioral outcome (self-perceived rightness / willingness to repair) is not conditioned on independently-verifiable advice correctness the way our discrimination index requires (§2.1, §1.2 below elaborates why this blocks direct reanalysis).

**Trust calibration frameworks.** Separately, the human-AI trust literature (e.g., work on calibrated trust in LLM hallucinations, the Trust Calibration Maturity Model, and studies on how uncertainty granularity shapes verification behavior) has developed the general normative target — trust and reliance should track actual system reliability — but these frameworks are typically applied to confidence scores and uncertainty communication, not to disclosure/source labels specifically, and they mostly evaluate calibration via behavioral measures alone (e.g., weight-of-advice, accept/reject decisions) without jointly collecting the stated-perception measures that disclosure-policy debates actually care about.

**Classic judge–advisor-system (JAS) studies.** A separate, older literature on advice-taking (e.g., studies of advisor-accuracy effects on weight of advice in general-knowledge and quantity-estimation tasks) already manipulates advisor correctness within participant and reports WOA conditioned on whether the advisor was right or wrong. These studies were not designed around AI disclosure and do not collect a matched stated-trust measure, so they cannot answer our question either — but as we discuss in §1.2, they are a plausible lower-cost source for partially validating the psychometric behavior of a correctness-conditioned discrimination index like D, independent of whether the labels we propose ever get tested on them.

**The gap.** No existing protocol we found collects (a) a stated-perception outcome and (b) an advice-correctness-conditioned behavioral outcome from the *same* participants under the *same* label manipulation, on a scale that lets researchers directly compare how much a label moves each. This matters practically: a label that only moves stated trust is a communication intervention, not a calibration intervention, and policy built on stated-trust evidence alone risks declaring success (or failure) on the wrong outcome. It also matters for label *design*: it is plausible that some label content (e.g., a rationale explaining *why* the AI's output should be checked) closes the gap where a bare source tag does not, but this is untested because the relevant comparison has not been made within one design.

### 1.2 Why existing datasets cannot substitute for new data collection — and what partial validation is possible now

A natural question — one we take seriously rather than deferring — is whether the PBG statistic could be computed retroactively on the sycophancy-warning-label dataset (ref. 1) or another cited dataset, rather than only on newly collected data. We do not think this is currently possible, for a structural reason rather than a data-access reason: computing our discrimination index D requires that the *same items* be answerable with independently verifiable ground truth and that the AI's advice be experimentally scripted to be correct on some trials and incorrect on others, stratified within participant. The sycophancy study's behavioral outcome is self-perceived rightness and conflict-repair willingness in interpersonal-conflict vignettes, a domain chosen specifically because there is no ground truth to be right or wrong about — that is the point of the vignette design, not an oversight. Similarly, most AIGC-labeling studies (refs. 3–6, 8) measure sharing intention or perceived credibility of whole messages rather than eliciting a pre-advice judgment and a post-advice revision on items with known correct answers. Reanalysis is therefore not simply a matter of recomputing a new statistic on old columns; it would require a dataset that manipulated advice correctness by design and also collected a matched stated-trust measure — a combination none of the disclosure-labeling datasets we reviewed has.

That said, "no dataset can validate PBG" is a stronger claim than "no dataset can validate PBG end-to-end," and the two should not be conflated. PBG has two separable ingredients: (a) the discrimination index D, which requires only correctness-conditioned advice and no label manipulation at all, and (b) the pairing of D with a stated-trust measure under a label manipulation, which is the part genuinely absent from every dataset we found. Ingredient (a) already exists in the classic JAS literature: studies of advice-taking in general-knowledge and estimation tasks (refs. 15–17) manipulate advisor accuracy within participant and report WOA separately for correct and incorrect advice, which is exactly the input D needs. These studies do not manipulate disclosure labels and were not designed to test our protocol's central question, so they cannot substitute for running the full study — but where item-level or condition-level summary statistics are available in their published reports, they could be used now, before any new data collection, to obtain an external, independent estimate of D's test-retest reliability, its censoring rate, and its sensitivity to the regression-to-the-mean pathology discussed in §2.1. That would let us check, ahead of committing to N ≈ 1,050–1,200 participants, whether D behaves the way our power calculation (§2.5) assumes it does. We treat this as a concrete, low-cost next step (§4) rather than something we can complete within this note, since it requires locating and re-extracting statistics from specific published studies rather than reasoning about the design in the abstract.

### 1.3 Contribution

We contribute (1) a paired-outcome experimental protocol for auditing disclosure/transparency labels that collects stated-perception and advice-correctness-conditioned behavioral measures from the same participants; (2) a summary statistic, the Perception–Behavior Gap (PBG), together with an explicit treatment of the measurement assumptions it requires and does not yet satisfy, and an explicit argument for why the protocol's value does not depend on PBG surviving as a single number (§2.3); and (3) a fully worked instantiation of the protocol (task, conditions, manipulation checks, sample-size and power calculation, and two contrasting hypothetical result patterns presented with matched detail) that other researchers can adapt directly, along with a concrete, low-cost partial-validation path (§1.2) that does not require us to execute the full study in this note.

## 2. Method

### 2.1 Task

We use a judge–advisor system (JAS) task, standard in the advice-taking literature. On each trial, a participant first gives an independent initial estimate or classification (e.g., "Is this news headline likely true or false?" or a numeric quantity-estimation item), then sees an AI system's suggestion, then gives a final answer. The gap between initial and final answer, expressed as a fraction of the gap between initial answer and the AI's suggestion, is the **Weight of Advice (WOA)** — the standard continuous behavioral reliance measure. For binary/classification items we use the analogous **Switch Rate** (probability of moving to the AI's suggested label) as a secondary behavioral outcome.

Critically, the ground truth of each item is known to the experimenter, and AI advice is scripted to be **correct** on half of trials and **incorrect** on the other half (unbeknownst to participants), stratified within participant. This lets us compute, per participant per condition, a **behavioral discrimination index**:

  D = WOA(correct-advice trials) − WOA(incorrect-advice trials)

D is the key behavioral calibration outcome: a well-calibrated participant relies more on the AI when it is right than when it is wrong (D > 0); a participant who relies on the AI regardless of correctness has D ≈ 0.

WOA-based measures have known pathologies that we build into the analysis plan rather than treating as an afterthought. WOA is undefined when initial and AI-suggested answers coincide (those trials are dropped, not imputed as 0), is censored at 0 and 1 for participants who never move or who fully adopt the advice, and can be inflated by regression-to-the-mean when initial estimates are extreme. Because these pathologies could differentially affect the correct- and incorrect-advice subsets and thereby bias D, we will (a) report the proportion of censored and undefined trials per condition, (b) repeat all analyses with a rank-based, censoring-robust alternative to raw WOA (the participant-level Spearman correlation between advice-agreement and advice-correctness, rescaled to run in parallel with WOA-based D), and (c) additionally report an AUC-style discrimination metric — treating "moved toward advice" as a binary event and advice correctness as the true class, then computing the area under the resulting ROC curve — since this class of metric is standard in the trust-calibration literature we cite (refs. 9–11) and does not depend on the linear, uncensored WOA scale the way D does. We treat D as the primary, pre-registered outcome for power purposes (§2.5) but report all three so that the substantive conclusion about which label closes the gap does not rest on one metric's idiosyncrasies.

Immediately after each block, participants also give the **stated-perception outcome**: a 7-point trust rating ("How much do you trust this AI system's suggestions?") and a 7-point perceived-accuracy rating, averaged into a single perception score P.

**Manipulation check: distinguishing "caution" from "persuasion."** A rationale-bearing label could in principle move D for two different reasons that a bare comparison of ΔP and ΔD cannot distinguish: the rationale could prompt genuine verification behavior (caution), or it could simply add credible-sounding text that increases trust without changing how the advice is processed (persuasion) — and these two mechanisms predict different, but potentially confusable, patterns of null ΔD. To separate them, we add two process measures collected on every trial, not only in the Source+Rationale arm: (i) a **binary self-report probe** immediately after the final answer — "Did seeing this label make you more likely to double-check the AI's suggestion, or more likely to accept it?" (double-check / accept / no effect) — and (ii) a **behavioral proxy for verification effort**, time spent on the advice screen before submitting a final answer, logged unobtrusively. If Source+Rationale produces a null ΔD alongside participants reporting increased "accept" responses and no increase in time-on-advice, that supports the persuasion account. If it produces a null ΔD alongside increased "double-check" responses and increased time-on-advice, that supports a caution-attempted-but-ineffective account — i.e., participants tried to verify but the rationale did not give them anything actionable to verify with. These are different findings with different design implications (rewrite the rationale's content vs. abandon rationale-style labels altogether), and the protocol as specified now has the process data needed to tell them apart rather than leaving the mechanism ambiguous post hoc.

### 2.2 Design

2 (advice correctness: correct / incorrect; within-participant, item-level) × 4 (label condition: **No-label**, **Source-only** ["AI-generated suggestion"], **Source+Confidence** [source tag plus a numeric model confidence score], **Source+Rationale** [source tag plus a one-sentence, item-specific reason to double-check, e.g., "this topic is one where the model is often overconfident"]) mixed design, with label condition manipulated between-participants to avoid carry-over/demand effects from seeing multiple label styles, and advice correctness manipulated within-participant to allow the discrimination index D to be computed per participant.

We add a **placebo label** arm (a content-free label of identical visual salience, e.g., "Suggestion #{item number}") to separate the effect of *any* salient annotation from the effect of AI-source disclosure specifically — this is the primary baseline against which No-label and the three real label conditions are compared, since a bare No-label vs. Source-only contrast confounds "labeling" with "adding a visually salient UI element."

### 2.3 Outcomes and the Perception–Behavior Gap statistic

For each label condition c, relative to the placebo baseline, we compute two standardized effect sizes:
- ΔP(c): standardized effect of condition c on stated perception P.
- ΔD(c): standardized effect of condition c on behavioral discrimination D.

We define **PBG(c) = ΔP(c) − ΔD(c)**. PBG(c) ≈ 0 indicates a label whose effect on stated trust is matched by an equivalent effect on behavioral calibration ("what people say tracks what they do"). PBG(c) ≫ 0 indicates a label that moves stated trust substantially more than it moves calibrated reliance — the dissociation pattern reported for sycophancy warning labels. PBG(c) < 0 (moving behavior more than stated perception) is possible but not predicted by prior work and would itself be a notable finding.

**Commensurability of ΔP and ΔD.** Subtracting two Cohen's-d values computed on different outcome distributions is only meaningful if both are, in some defensible sense, on the same scale, and we do not think this can simply be asserted. Cohen's d standardizes by each outcome's own pooled SD, so PBG implicitly assumes that "one SD of movement in trust rating" and "one SD of movement in behavioral discrimination" represent comparable degrees of intervention effect — an assumption that could fail if, for example, the perception composite has substantially lower measurement reliability than D (unreliability inflates SD and shrinks d, which would mechanically *understate* ΔP relative to ΔD, biasing PBG toward zero, or the reverse if D is the noisier measure). We therefore treat raw PBG as a first-pass, easily communicated statistic, but recommend three companion checks before over-interpreting any single PBG value: (i) report the split-half or test-retest reliability of both P and D in the actual sample and, where they differ materially, report a reliability-corrected PBG using disattenuated effect sizes; (ii) report each ΔP and ΔD alongside its own confidence interval rather than only the difference, so readers can see whether PBG is driven by a real ΔD ≈ 0 or merely by a wide, uninformative ΔD interval; and (iii) as a scale-free robustness check, report the rank-based common-language variant of each effect (probability that a randomly drawn treated participant exceeds a randomly drawn placebo participant on P, and separately on D) so the comparison does not depend on the SD-standardization step at all. None of this resolves the commensurability question in the abstract — no single number can certify that a d on a Likert composite and a d on a WOA difference are "the same kind of unit" — but it makes the assumption visible and testable in any actual dataset rather than silently embedded in a single subtraction.

**Is PBG load-bearing?** Given that we cannot certify commensurability in advance, it is fair to ask whether the paper's contribution survives if PBG itself were dropped in favor of always reporting ΔP, ΔD, their reliability-corrected versions, and the common-language effects separately, rather than collapsing them into one subtraction. We think the answer is yes for the protocol's scientific content and no for its communicability. The scientific content — whether a label's effect on stated trust is or is not matched by its effect on behavioral discrimination — is fully carried by the triad (ΔP, ΔD, and their reliability-corrected/rank-based companions); nothing about detecting or characterizing the perception–behavior dissociation requires computing their difference. What PBG adds is a single, sortable, plottable number that lets a regulator or a meta-analyst compare label designs, or compare across studies, without holding two interval estimates in their head at once — the same reason single-number summaries (e.g., NNT in medicine, or a raw effect-size difference in meta-analysis) persist despite known aggregation caveats. We therefore report PBG as a headline, screening-level statistic in every table below, but we do not treat it as a substitute for the triad, and we would consider a replication or extension of this protocol successful even if it concluded that PBG should be retired in favor of always reporting the triad — that would still be a direct empirical answer to a question this note can only pose.

### 2.4 Baselines and ablations

- **Baselines**: No-label (standard control in the disclosure literature) and Placebo-label (isolates salience from content, addressed in §2.2).
- **Ablation 1 — label content**: Source-only vs. Source+Confidence vs. Source+Rationale isolates which *component* of a rich label (bare disclosure, numeric confidence, or qualitative rationale) drives any closing of the gap.
- **Ablation 2 — stakes**: a between-participants replication arm using high-stakes item framing (e.g., medical/financial advice items) vs. the low-stakes default (trivia/news-headline items), since prior disclosure work finds effects vary by content domain and perceived risk.
- **Ablation 3 — AI-literacy covariate**: a brief 6-item AI-literacy scale collected pre-task, entered as a covariate, since trust-calibration work finds literacy and prior AI exposure moderate reliance independent of any label.
- **Ablation 4 — rationale specificity**: within the Source+Rationale arm only, generic rationale ("AI can make mistakes") vs. item-specific rationale (varies per item), to test whether specificity — not just the presence of a rationale — drives calibration gains, cross-tabulated against the manipulation-check probe in §2.1 to see whether specificity operates by increasing reported "double-check" responses rather than merely reported trust.

### 2.5 Sample size and power

Powering on D (the harder-to-move outcome) rather than P: assuming a target detectable between-condition difference in D of d = 0.35 (a conservative estimate given that WOA effects in the advice-taking literature are typically small-to-medium), two-tailed α = .05, power = .90, a two-group comparison requires n ≈ 172 per arm (G*Power-style normal approximation). With 5 arms (No-label, Placebo, Source-only, Source+Confidence, Source+Rationale) plus a stakes-replication arm, total N ≈ 1,050–1,200, in line with sample sizes used in the sycophancy-label study (N ≈ 2,610 across more cells) and other large disclosure experiments in this literature. Because the AUC-style and rank-based companion metrics in §2.1 are less efficient than the parametric WOA difference for a fixed N, we note that a study powered on D at d = 0.35 will have somewhat lower power for the companion metrics; researchers prioritizing metric-robustness over strict power efficiency should inflate n per arm by roughly 15–20%. As noted in §1.2, this d = 0.35 target and the assumed censoring/reliability rates it depends on are themselves currently un-validated for D specifically; re-extracting condition-level statistics from existing correctness-conditioned JAS datasets (refs. 15–17) before data collection begins would let researchers replace this literature-based guess with an empirical prior, and is the cheapest single thing that could be done to de-risk the full-N study.

## 3. Results

We did not execute this protocol. Rather than present a single worked table calibrated to reproduce our own predicted pattern — which would let the numbers simply restate the hypothesis they are meant to test — we walk through two contrasting hypothetical scenarios, matched in the level of numeric and narrative detail we give each, to show that the PBG statistic and the protocol's other diagnostics (§2.1, §2.3) are informative regardless of which one materializes. Neither scenario is privileged as more likely a priori: both are equally consistent with the literature surveyed in §1.1, which documents both persistent dissociations (refs. 1, 7) and content-dependent closing of disclosure effects (refs. 3–4), and nothing in that literature currently lets us assign higher prior probability to one pattern over the other. We label both explicitly as hypothetical; no numbers below are data.

**Scenario A — the gap closes for rationale-bearing labels.** Source-only labeling reproduces the classic dissociation: stated trust rises (ΔP = 0.31) with little change in discrimination (ΔD = 0.06, n.s.), giving a large PBG (0.25). Source+Confidence partially closes the gap in D (0.14) but ΔP rises even more (0.44), so PBG stays large (0.30) — consistent with confidence numbers being over-trusted rather than used diagnostically. Source+Rationale is the condition where ΔD (0.33) approaches ΔP (0.39), bringing PBG down to a non-significant residual (0.06). The manipulation-check probe (§2.1) would show, under this scenario, an increase in "double-check" self-reports and time-on-advice specifically in the Source+Rationale arm, consistent with the ΔD movement reflecting attempted-and-successful verification rather than a measurement artifact.

| Condition | ΔP (d) | ΔD (d) | PBG | Reliability-corrected PBG (illustrative) |
|---|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| Source-only | 0.31 | 0.06 | 0.25 | 0.22 |
| Source+Confidence | 0.44 | 0.14 | 0.30 | 0.27 |
| Source+Rationale | 0.39 | 0.33 | 0.06 | 0.09 |

**Scenario B — no tested label closes the gap.** Suppose rationale content does not translate into better discrimination — e.g., because participants treat the rationale as more persuasive "extra text from the AI" rather than as a cue to verify, a failure mode discussed in the prosocial-advertising and transparency-dilemma literature (refs. 4, 7). Source-only and Source+Confidence pattern as in Scenario A (ΔP = 0.29 / 0.41, ΔD = 0.08 / 0.11, PBG = 0.21 / 0.30), but Source+Rationale now also fails to close the gap: ΔD remains small (0.10, n.s.) alongside a large ΔP (0.42), yielding PBG ≈ 0.32, the largest of the three conditions rather than the smallest. The manipulation-check probe distinguishes two sub-cases of this pattern that a bare ΔP/ΔD comparison could not: if participants increasingly report "accept" rather than "double-check" and show no increase in time-on-advice, that supports the persuasion account above; if instead participants report increased "double-check" and increased time-on-advice despite the null ΔD, that indicates attempted-but-ineffective verification — participants tried to act on the rationale but it gave them nothing diagnostic to act on. Reliability-corrected PBG under this scenario (0.19 / 0.27 / 0.29, respectively) stays close to raw PBG throughout, since nothing in this pattern implies a reliability gap between P and D. This would falsify our stated design intuition that item-specific rationale should help, but it would not falsify the protocol or the PBG statistic: it would be a substantively important finding in its own right — that no tested label design delivers more than a communication effect, and that rationale-bearing labels can inflate stated trust without inflating calibration, a worse outcome for policy than a label that does nothing. The value of running the protocol does not depend on Scenario A being the one that occurs; a null or reversed pattern is just as reportable and just as actionable, since it would tell regulators that none of the tested label designs deliver more than a communication effect, which is itself the paper's central concern in §1.

|---|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| Source-only | 0.29 | 0.08 | 0.21 | 0.19 |
| Source+Confidence | 0.41 | 0.11 | 0.30 | 0.27 |
| Source+Rationale | 0.42 | 0.10 | 0.32 | 0.29 |

**Ablation 4 (rationale specificity).** Under Scenario A, item-specific rationale is expected to outperform generic rationale on ΔD (e.g., 0.33 vs. 0.12), with the manipulation-check probe showing more "double-check" responses in the item-specific sub-arm. Under Scenario B, both generic and item-specific rationale show similarly muted ΔD (e.g., 0.10 vs. 0.09) — and if the manipulation-check probe also shows similarly muted "double-check" rates in both, that would point toward rationale content per se being ineffective (not just our specific wording), whereas a difference in probe responses without a corresponding difference in ΔD would point toward the attempted-but-ineffective-verification account instead. This is the concrete case where the process measures added in §2.1, rather than ΔP/ΔD alone, are what let the study distinguish "rationale doesn't work" from "our rationale wording doesn't work."

## 4. Conclusion

Disclosure and transparency labels are usually evaluated on whether they change what people *say* about an AI system, not on whether they change how people actually *use* it — and the one large study that measured a related construct found the two can diverge, though its vignette-based, no-ground-truth design cannot be repurposed to validate a correctness-conditioned discrimination statistic directly (§1.2). We formalize the general concern as the Perception–Behavior Gap and propose a paired-outcome protocol and a PBG statistic that let researchers detect, quantify, and compare this divergence across label designs within one study. We have tried to be explicit about where this statistic is on solid ground and where it is not: PBG as a raw difference of two Cohen's-d values assumes a commensurability between stated-perception and behavioral-discrimination effect sizes that is not automatically true, we specify companion diagnostics (reliability correction, rank-based common-language effects, reporting both ΔP and ΔD with their own intervals) that any real application of the protocol should run before treating a single PBG number as conclusive, and we argue explicitly (§2.3) that the protocol's scientific content survives even if PBG itself is eventually retired in favor of always reporting the triad. We also worked through two opposite hypothetical outcomes, presented with matched detail rather than favoring the confirming pattern, to show the protocol does not require its own hypothesis to be confirmed in order to produce a useful result — a null or reversed pattern is diagnostic too, and the added manipulation check (§2.1) ensures such a null result would not be mechanistically ambiguous. We see five immediate next steps: (i) before any new data collection, re-extract condition-level WOA statistics from existing correctness-conditioned JAS datasets (refs. 15–17) to obtain an external estimate of D's reliability and censoring behavior, replacing the literature-based guess in §2.5 with an empirical one; (ii) run the protocol as specified here (§2.5 gives the power calculation) as a preregistered study, reporting D alongside its AUC-style and rank-based companions and the manipulation-check probe rather than D alone; (iii) use that dataset to empirically test, for the first time, whether ΔP and ΔD effect sizes are in fact commensurable once reliability is accounted for, and whether PBG should be retained as a headline statistic or replaced by the triad it summarizes; (iv) extend the behavioral outcome beyond WOA/switch-rate to downstream task accuracy, to test whether closing PBG also improves objective decision quality, not just discrimination; and (v) apply the same paired-outcome logic to other socio-technical interventions currently evaluated on self-report alone (e.g., algorithmic-decision explanations, chatbot uncertainty phrasing), since the perception–behavior dissociation we discuss is unlikely to be specific to AI-source labels.
