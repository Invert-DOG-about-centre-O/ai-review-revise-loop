# The Perception–Behavior Gap in AI Disclosure Labels: A Paired-Outcome Protocol for Auditing Transparency Interventions

## Abstract

Regulators and platforms increasingly require that AI-generated or AI-assisted content be labeled, on the assumption that disclosure lets people calibrate how much to trust or rely on a system. Most empirical evaluations of these labels measure only self-reported outcomes — trust ratings, perceived credibility, perceived objectivity — and infer that a shift in these ratings implies a shift in how people actually use AI output. Recent findings complicate this inference: labels warning about AI sycophancy shifted participants' stated perceptions of an AI system without measurably reducing the AI's actual influence on their judgments, and several AI-generated-content (AIGC) labeling studies report similar dissociations between what people say and what they do. We argue this "perception–behavior gap" is under-measured because self-report and behavioral outcomes are rarely collected from the same participants under the same manipulation with a common effect-size scale. We propose a paired-outcome protocol — a judge–advisor task in which every participant provides both a stated-trust rating and a revealed-reliance measure (weight of advice, adjusted for advice correctness) — and a single summary statistic, the Perception–Behavior Gap (PBG), that quantifies how much a label's effect on stated trust outruns its effect on behavioral calibration. We instantiate the protocol in a 2 (advice correctness: correct/incorrect) × 4 (label condition: none, source-only, source+confidence, source+rationale) mixed design, address the statistical and measurement objections that a naive difference-of-effect-sizes statistic invites (commensurability of the two effect sizes, sensitivity to known weight-of-advice pathologies, and the risk that a single worked numerical example is unfalsifiable), and walk through two contrasting worked numerical scenarios — one in which the gap closes and one in which it does not — to show that the protocol is informative either way. We discuss why the one existing large dataset with a documented perception–behavior dissociation cannot be repurposed to validate PBG directly, what would be needed to validate it on real data, and how the protocol generalizes to other disclosure and transparency interventions.

## 1. Introduction

Disclosure requirements — labeling text, images, or decisions as AI-generated or AI-assisted — are one of the most widely deployed socio-technical interventions in current AI governance. The EU AI Act, platform policies (e.g., on synthetic media), and journalistic norms all converge on a simple theory of change: telling people that content or advice came from an AI system will let them apply appropriate skepticism, improving the calibration of trust and reliance. This theory is testable, and a growing empirical literature has tested it.

### 1.1 Related work

**Disclosure effects on stated perception.** A large body of survey- and vignette-based work manipulates whether content is labeled as AI-generated and measures self-reported trust, perceived credibility, and sharing intention. Findings are mixed and often small in magnitude: some studies find labeling AI-generated messages does not reduce their persuasive effects on stated attitudes (Kreps & Kriner-style paradigms; see the PNAS Nexus and PMC replications of "Labeling messages as AI-generated does not reduce their persuasive effects"), while others find disclosure lowers perceived credibility or trust to varying degrees depending on content type and framing (e.g., studies on AIGC labels and misinformation sharing intentions, and on prosocial-advertising evaluation). A recurring theme across this literature is the "transparency dilemma": disclosure can simultaneously satisfy a normative demand for honesty while failing to produce the downstream behavioral caution regulators intend.

**Disclosure effects on behavior.** A smaller set of studies goes beyond stated attitudes to measure actual reliance — e.g., EEG/physiological responses to AIGC-labeled news, or decision tasks where participants act on AI advice. The most directly relevant recent result is a large preregistered experiment (N ≈ 2,610) showing that warning labels about AI sycophancy shifted participants' *perceptions* of the AI (lower perceived objectivity and trust) but did not reliably reduce the AI's *influence* on participants' self-perceived rightness or their willingness to repair conflicts — i.e., perception moved, behavior did not. This is the clearest existing demonstration of a perception–behavior dissociation under a disclosure-style intervention, but it studies one label design in one domain (conflict advice) and does not offer a general protocol or a summary statistic other researchers can reuse to test whether their own labels exhibit the same dissociation. Critically for our purposes, that study's behavioral outcome (self-perceived rightness / willingness to repair) is not conditioned on independently-verifiable advice correctness the way our discrimination index requires (§2.1, §1.2 below elaborates why this blocks direct reanalysis).

**Trust calibration frameworks.** Separately, the human-AI trust literature (e.g., work on calibrated trust in LLM hallucinations, the Trust Calibration Maturity Model, and studies on how uncertainty granularity shapes verification behavior) has developed the general normative target — trust and reliance should track actual system reliability — but these frameworks are typically applied to confidence scores and uncertainty communication, not to disclosure/source labels specifically, and they mostly evaluate calibration via behavioral measures alone (e.g., weight-of-advice, accept/reject decisions) without jointly collecting the stated-perception measures that disclosure-policy debates actually care about.

**The gap.** No existing protocol we found collects (a) a stated-perception outcome and (b) an advice-correctness-conditioned behavioral outcome from the *same* participants under the *same* label manipulation, on a scale that lets researchers directly compare how much a label moves each. This matters practically: a label that only moves stated trust is a communication intervention, not a calibration intervention, and policy built on stated-trust evidence alone risks declaring success (or failure) on the wrong outcome. It also matters for label *design*: it is plausible that some label content (e.g., a rationale explaining *why* the AI's output should be checked) closes the gap where a bare source tag does not, but this is untested because the relevant comparison has not been made within one design.

### 1.2 Why existing datasets cannot substitute for new data collection

A natural question — one we take seriously rather than deferring — is whether the PBG statistic could be computed retroactively on the sycophancy-warning-label dataset (ref. 1) or another cited dataset, rather than only on newly collected data. We do not think this is currently possible, for a structural reason rather than a data-access reason: computing our discrimination index D requires that the *same items* be answerable with independently verifiable ground truth and that the AI's advice be experimentally scripted to be correct on some trials and incorrect on others, stratified within participant. The sycophancy study's behavioral outcome is self-perceived rightness and conflict-repair willingness in interpersonal-conflict vignettes, a domain chosen specifically because there is no ground truth to be right or wrong about — that is the point of the vignette design, not an oversight. Similarly, most AIGC-labeling studies (refs. 3–6, 8) measure sharing intention or perceived credibility of whole messages rather than eliciting a pre-advice judgment and a post-advice revision on items with known correct answers. Reanalysis is therefore not simply a matter of recomputing a new statistic on old columns; it would require a dataset that manipulated advice correctness by design, which none of the datasets we reviewed did. We treat this as a genuine limitation of what can be validated without new data collection (see §3.3), and as the strongest existing argument for why the protocol needs to be run rather than retrofitted.

### 1.3 Contribution

We contribute (1) a paired-outcome experimental protocol for auditing disclosure/transparency labels that collects stated-perception and advice-correctness-conditioned behavioral measures from the same participants; (2) a single summary statistic, the Perception–Behavior Gap (PBG), together with an explicit treatment of the measurement assumptions it requires and does not yet satisfy; and (3) a fully worked instantiation of the protocol (task, conditions, sample-size and power calculation, and two contrasting hypothetical result patterns) that other researchers can adapt directly, without requiring us to execute the study in this note.

## 2. Method

### 2.1 Task

We use a judge–advisor system (JAS) task, standard in the advice-taking literature. On each trial, a participant first gives an independent initial estimate or classification (e.g., "Is this news headline likely true or false?" or a numeric quantity-estimation item), then sees an AI system's suggestion, then gives a final answer. The gap between initial and final answer, expressed as a fraction of the gap between initial answer and the AI's suggestion, is the **Weight of Advice (WOA)** — the standard continuous behavioral reliance measure. For binary/classification items we use the analogous **Switch Rate** (probability of moving to the AI's suggested label) as a secondary behavioral outcome.

Critically, the ground truth of each item is known to the experimenter, and AI advice is scripted to be **correct** on half of trials and **incorrect** on the other half (unbeknownst to participants), stratified within participant. This lets us compute, per participant per condition, a **behavioral discrimination index**:

  D = WOA(correct-advice trials) − WOA(incorrect-advice trials)

D is the key behavioral calibration outcome: a well-calibrated participant relies more on the AI when it is right than when it is wrong (D > 0); a participant who relies on the AI regardless of correctness has D ≈ 0.

WOA-based measures have known pathologies that we build into the analysis plan rather than treating as an afterthought. WOA is undefined when initial and AI-suggested answers coincide (those trials are dropped, not imputed as 0), is censored at 0 and 1 for participants who never move or who fully adopt the advice, and can be inflated by regression-to-the-mean when initial estimates are extreme. Because these pathologies could differentially affect the correct- and incorrect-advice subsets and thereby bias D, we will (a) report the proportion of censored and undefined trials per condition, (b) repeat all analyses with a rank-based, censoring-robust alternative to raw WOA (the participant-level Spearman correlation between advice-agreement and advice-correctness, rescaled to run in parallel with WOA-based D), and (c) additionally report an AUC-style discrimination metric — treating "moved toward advice" as a binary event and advice correctness as the true class, then computing the area under the resulting ROC curve — since this class of metric is standard in the trust-calibration literature we cite (refs. 9–11) and does not depend on the linear, uncensored WOA scale the way D does. We treat D as the primary, pre-registered outcome for power purposes (§2.5) but report all three so that the substantive conclusion about which label closes the gap does not rest on one metric's idiosyncrasies.

Immediately after each block, participants also give the **stated-perception outcome**: a 7-point trust rating ("How much do you trust this AI system's suggestions?") and a 7-point perceived-accuracy rating, averaged into a single perception score P.

### 2.2 Design

2 (advice correctness: correct / incorrect; within-participant, item-level) × 4 (label condition: **No-label**, **Source-only** ["AI-generated suggestion"], **Source+Confidence** [source tag plus a numeric model confidence score], **Source+Rationale** [source tag plus a one-sentence, item-specific reason to double-check, e.g., "this topic is one where the model is often overconfident"]) mixed design, with label condition manipulated between-participants to avoid carry-over/demand effects from seeing multiple label styles, and advice correctness manipulated within-participant to allow the discrimination index D to be computed per participant.

We add a **placebo label** arm (a content-free label of identical visual salience, e.g., "Suggestion #{item number}") to separate the effect of *any* salient annotation from the effect of AI-source disclosure specifically — this is the primary baseline against which No-label and the three real label conditions are compared, since a bare No-label vs. Source-only contrast confounds "labeling" with "adding a visually salient UI element."

### 2.3 Outcomes and the Perception–Behavior Gap statistic

For each label condition c, relative to the placebo baseline, we compute two standardized effect sizes:
- ΔP(c): standardized effect of condition c on stated perception P.
- ΔD(c): standardized effect of condition c on behavioral discrimination D.

We define **PBG(c) = ΔP(c) − ΔD(c)**. PBG(c) ≈ 0 indicates a label whose effect on stated trust is matched by an equivalent effect on behavioral calibration ("what people say tracks what they do"). PBG(c) ≫ 0 indicates a label that moves stated trust substantially more than it moves calibrated reliance — the dissociation pattern reported for sycophancy warning labels. PBG(c) < 0 (moving behavior more than stated perception) is possible but not predicted by prior work and would itself be a notable finding.

**Commensurability of ΔP and ΔD.** Subtracting two Cohen's-d values computed on different outcome distributions is only meaningful if both are, in some defensible sense, on the same scale, and we do not think this can simply be asserted. Cohen's d standardizes by each outcome's own pooled SD, so PBG implicitly assumes that "one SD of movement in trust rating" and "one SD of movement in behavioral discrimination" represent comparable degrees of intervention effect — an assumption that could fail if, for example, the perception composite has substantially lower measurement reliability than D (unreliability inflates SD and shrinks d, which would mechanically *understate* ΔP relative to ΔD, biasing PBG toward zero, or the reverse if D is the noisier measure). We therefore treat raw PBG as a first-pass, easily communicated statistic, but recommend three companion checks before over-interpreting any single PBG value: (i) report the split-half or test-retest reliability of both P and D in the actual sample and, where they differ materially, report a reliability-corrected PBG using disattenuated effect sizes; (ii) report each ΔP and ΔD alongside its own confidence interval rather than only the difference, so readers can see whether PBG is driven by a real ΔD ≈ 0 or merely by a wide, uninformative ΔD interval; and (iii) as a scale-free robustness check, report the rank-based common-language variant of each effect (probability that a randomly drawn treated participant exceeds a randomly drawn placebo participant on P, and separately on D) so the comparison does not depend on the SD-standardization step at all. None of this resolves the commensurability question in the abstract — no single number can certify that a d on a Likert composite and a d on a WOA difference are "the same kind of unit" — but it makes the assumption visible and testable in any actual dataset rather than silently embedded in a single subtraction.

### 2.4 Baselines and ablations

- **Baselines**: No-label (standard control in the disclosure literature) and Placebo-label (isolates salience from content, addressed in §2.2).
- **Ablation 1 — label content**: Source-only vs. Source+Confidence vs. Source+Rationale isolates which *component* of a rich label (bare disclosure, numeric confidence, or qualitative rationale) drives any closing of the gap.
- **Ablation 2 — stakes**: a between-participants replication arm using high-stakes item framing (e.g., medical/financial advice items) vs. the low-stakes default (trivia/news-headline items), since prior disclosure work finds effects vary by content domain and perceived risk.
- **Ablation 3 — AI-literacy covariate**: a brief 6-item AI-literacy scale collected pre-task, entered as a covariate, since trust-calibration work finds literacy and prior AI exposure moderate reliance independent of any label.
- **Ablation 4 — rationale specificity**: within the Source+Rationale arm only, generic rationale ("AI can make mistakes") vs. item-specific rationale (varies per item), to test whether specificity — not just the presence of a rationale — drives calibration gains.

### 2.5 Sample size and power

Powering on D (the harder-to-move outcome) rather than P: assuming a target detectable between-condition difference in D of d = 0.35 (a conservative estimate given that WOA effects in the advice-taking literature are typically small-to-medium), two-tailed α = .05, power = .90, a two-group comparison requires n ≈ 172 per arm (G*Power-style normal approximation). With 5 arms (No-label, Placebo, Source-only, Source+Confidence, Source+Rationale) plus a stakes-replication arm, total N ≈ 1,050–1,200, in line with sample sizes used in the sycophancy-label study (N ≈ 2,610 across more cells) and other large disclosure experiments in this literature. Because the AUC-style and rank-based companion metrics in §2.1 are less efficient than the parametric WOA difference for a fixed N, we note that a study powered on D at d = 0.35 will have somewhat lower power for the companion metrics; researchers prioritizing metric-robustness over strict power efficiency should inflate n per arm by roughly 15–20%.

## 3. Results

We did not execute this protocol. Rather than present a single worked table calibrated to reproduce our own predicted pattern — which would let the numbers simply restate the hypothesis they are meant to test — we walk through two contrasting hypothetical scenarios, both internally consistent with WOA baselines and effect-size ranges reported in the literature we surveyed, to show that the PBG statistic and the protocol's other diagnostics (§2.1, §2.3) are informative regardless of which one materializes. We label both explicitly as hypothetical; no numbers below are data.

**Scenario A — the gap-closing pattern we consider most likely, based on the reasoning in §1.1.** Source-only labeling reproduces the classic dissociation: stated trust rises (ΔP = 0.31) with little change in discrimination (ΔD = 0.06, n.s.), giving a large PBG (0.25). Source+Confidence partially closes the gap in D (0.14) but ΔP rises even more (0.44), so PBG stays large (0.30) — consistent with confidence numbers being over-trusted rather than used diagnostically. Source+Rationale is the only condition where ΔD (0.33) approaches ΔP (0.39), bringing PBG down to a non-significant residual (0.06).

| Condition | ΔP (d) | ΔD (d) | PBG | Reliability-corrected PBG (illustrative) |
|---|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| Source-only | 0.31 | 0.06 | 0.25 | 0.22 |
| Source+Confidence | 0.44 | 0.14 | 0.30 | 0.27 |
| Source+Rationale | 0.39 | 0.33 | 0.06 | 0.09 |

**Scenario B — a disconfirming pattern, equally consistent with prior literature at the level of what is currently known.** Suppose rationale content does not translate into better discrimination — e.g., because participants treat the rationale as more persuasive "extra text from the AI" rather than as a cue to verify, a failure mode discussed in the prosocial-advertising and transparency-dilemma literature (refs. 4, 7). In that world, ΔD for Source+Rationale would remain small (e.g., 0.10, n.s.) alongside a large ΔP (0.42), yielding PBG ≈ 0.32 — as large as, or larger than, the bare Source-only condition. This would falsify our stated prediction, but it would not falsify the protocol or the PBG statistic: it would be a substantively important finding in its own right (that no tested label design closes the gap, and that rationale-bearing labels can inflate stated trust without inflating calibration, a worse outcome for policy than a label that does nothing). The value of running the protocol does not depend on Scenario A being the one that occurs — a null or reversed pattern is just as reportable and just as actionable, since it would tell regulators that none of the tested label designs deliver more than a communication effect, which is itself the paper's central concern in §1.

**Ablation 4 (rationale specificity).** Under Scenario A, item-specific rationale is expected to outperform generic rationale on ΔD (e.g., 0.33 vs. 0.12); under Scenario B, both would show similarly muted ΔD, which would itself help distinguish "rationale content doesn't work" from "our particular rationale wording doesn't work."

## 4. Conclusion

Disclosure and transparency labels are usually evaluated on whether they change what people *say* about an AI system, not on whether they change how people actually *use* it — and the one large study that measured a related construct found the two can diverge, though its vignette-based, no-ground-truth design cannot be repurposed to validate a correctness-conditioned discrimination statistic directly (§1.2). We formalize the general concern as the Perception–Behavior Gap and propose a paired-outcome protocol and a PBG statistic that let researchers detect, quantify, and compare this divergence across label designs within one study. We have tried to be explicit about where this statistic is on solid ground and where it is not: PBG as a raw difference of two Cohen's-d values assumes a commensurability between stated-perception and behavioral-discrimination effect sizes that is not automatically true, and we specify companion diagnostics (reliability correction, rank-based common-language effects, reporting both ΔP and ΔD with their own intervals) that any real application of the protocol should run before treating a single PBG number as conclusive. We also worked through two opposite hypothetical outcomes to show the protocol does not require its own hypothesis to be confirmed in order to produce a useful result — a null or reversed pattern is diagnostic too. We see four immediate next steps: (i) run the protocol as specified here (§2.5 gives the power calculation) as a preregistered study, reporting D alongside its AUC-style and rank-based companions rather than D alone; (ii) use that dataset to empirically test, for the first time, whether ΔP and ΔD effect sizes are in fact commensurable once reliability is accounted for, rather than assuming so; (iii) extend the behavioral outcome beyond WOA/switch-rate to downstream task accuracy, to test whether closing PBG also improves objective decision quality, not just discrimination; and (iv) apply the same paired-outcome logic to other socio-technical interventions currently evaluated on self-report alone (e.g., algorithmic-decision explanations, chatbot uncertainty phrasing), since the perception–behavior dissociation we discuss is unlikely to be specific to AI-source labels.
