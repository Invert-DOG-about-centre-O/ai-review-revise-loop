# The Perception–Behavior Gap in AI Disclosure Labels: A Paired-Outcome Protocol for Auditing Transparency Interventions

## Abstract

Regulators and platforms increasingly require that AI-generated or AI-assisted content be labeled, on the assumption that disclosure lets people calibrate how much to trust or rely on a system. Most empirical evaluations of these labels measure only self-reported outcomes — trust ratings, perceived credibility, perceived objectivity — and infer that a shift in these ratings implies a shift in how people actually use AI output. Recent findings complicate this inference: labels warning about AI sycophancy shifted participants' stated perceptions of an AI system without measurably reducing the AI's actual influence on their judgments, and several AI-generated-content (AIGC) labeling studies report similar dissociations between what people say and what they do. We argue this "perception–behavior gap" is under-measured because self-report and behavioral outcomes are rarely collected from the same participants under the same manipulation with a common effect-size scale. We propose a paired-outcome protocol — a judge–advisor task in which every participant provides both a stated-trust rating and a revealed-reliance measure (weight of advice, adjusted for advice correctness) — and a single summary statistic, the Perception–Behavior Gap (PBG), that quantifies how much a label's effect on stated trust outruns its effect on behavioral calibration. We instantiate the protocol in a 2 (advice correctness: correct/incorrect) × 4 (label condition: none, source-only, source+confidence, source+rationale) mixed design and report worked, pre-registered-style results (not from an executed study) illustrating the expected pattern: all three label conditions raise stated trust relative to no-label, but only source+rationale produces a statistically and practically meaningful increase in behavioral discrimination between correct and incorrect advice. We discuss how the protocol generalizes to other disclosure and transparency interventions and lay out concrete baselines, ablations, and power calculations for researchers who wish to run it.

## 1. Introduction

Disclosure requirements — labeling text, images, or decisions as AI-generated or AI-assisted — are one of the most widely deployed socio-technical interventions in current AI governance. The EU AI Act, platform policies (e.g., on synthetic media), and journalistic norms all converge on a simple theory of change: telling people that content or advice came from an AI system will let them apply appropriate skepticism, improving the calibration of trust and reliance. This theory is testable, and a growing empirical literature has tested it.

### 1.1 Related work

**Disclosure effects on stated perception.** A large body of survey- and vignette-based work manipulates whether content is labeled as AI-generated and measures self-reported trust, perceived credibility, and sharing intention. Findings are mixed and often small in magnitude: some studies find labeling AI-generated messages does not reduce their persuasive effects on stated attitudes (Kreps & Kriner-style paradigms; see the PNAS Nexus and PMC replications of "Labeling messages as AI-generated does not reduce their persuasive effects"), while others find disclosure lowers perceived credibility or trust to varying degrees depending on content type and framing (e.g., studies on AIGC labels and misinformation sharing intentions, and on prosocial-advertising evaluation). A recurring theme across this literature is the "transparency dilemma": disclosure can simultaneously satisfy a normative demand for honesty while failing to produce the downstream behavioral caution regulators intend.

**Disclosure effects on behavior.** A smaller set of studies goes beyond stated attitudes to measure actual reliance — e.g., EEG/physiological responses to AIGC-labeled news, or decision tasks where participants act on AI advice. The most directly relevant recent result is a large preregistered experiment (N ≈ 2,610) showing that warning labels about AI sycophancy shifted participants' *perceptions* of the AI (lower perceived objectivity and trust) but did not reliably reduce the AI's *influence* on participants' self-perceived rightness or their willingness to repair conflicts — i.e., perception moved, behavior did not. This is the clearest existing demonstration of a perception–behavior dissociation under a disclosure-style intervention, but it studies one label design in one domain (conflict advice) and does not offer a general protocol or a summary statistic other researchers can reuse to test whether their own labels exhibit the same dissociation.

**Trust calibration frameworks.** Separately, the human-AI trust literature (e.g., work on calibrated trust in LLM hallucinations, the Trust Calibration Maturity Model, and studies on how uncertainty granularity shapes verification behavior) has developed the general normative target — trust and reliance should track actual system reliability — but these frameworks are typically applied to confidence scores and uncertainty communication, not to disclosure/source labels specifically, and they mostly evaluate calibration via behavioral measures alone (e.g., weight-of-advice, accept/reject decisions) without jointly collecting the stated-perception measures that disclosure-policy debates actually care about.

**The gap.** No existing protocol we found collects (a) a stated-perception outcome and (b) an advice-correctness-conditioned behavioral outcome from the *same* participants under the *same* label manipulation, on a common scale that lets researchers directly compare how much a label moves each. This matters practically: a label that only moves stated trust is a communication intervention, not a calibration intervention, and policy built on stated-trust evidence alone risks declaring success (or failure) on the wrong outcome. It also matters for label *design*: it is plausible that some label content (e.g., a rationale explaining *why* the AI's output should be checked) closes the gap where a bare source tag does not, but this is untested because the relevant comparison has not been made within one design.

### 1.2 Contribution

We contribute (1) a paired-outcome experimental protocol for auditing disclosure/transparency labels that collects stated-perception and advice-correctness-conditioned behavioral measures from the same participants; (2) a single summary statistic, the Perception–Behavior Gap (PBG), for quantifying and comparing across label designs how much a label's effect on stated trust outruns its effect on behavioral discrimination; and (3) a fully worked instantiation of the protocol (task, conditions, sample-size and power calculation, and plausible result numbers) that other researchers can adapt directly, without requiring us to execute the study in this note.

## 2. Method

### 2.1 Task

We use a judge–advisor system (JAS) task, standard in the advice-taking literature. On each trial, a participant first gives an independent initial estimate or classification (e.g., "Is this news headline likely true or false?" or a numeric quantity-estimation item), then sees an AI system's suggestion, then gives a final answer. The gap between initial and final answer, expressed as a fraction of the gap between initial answer and the AI's suggestion, is the **Weight of Advice (WOA)** — the standard continuous behavioral reliance measure. For binary/classification items we use the analogous **Switch Rate** (probability of moving to the AI's suggested label) as a secondary behavioral outcome.

Critically, the ground truth of each item is known to the experimenter, and AI advice is scripted to be **correct** on half of trials and **incorrect** on the other half (unbeknownst to participants), stratified within participant. This lets us compute, per participant per condition, a **behavioral discrimination index**:

  D = WOA(correct-advice trials) − WOA(incorrect-advice trials)

D is the key behavioral calibration outcome: a well-calibrated participant relies more on the AI when it is right than when it is wrong (D > 0); a participant who relies on the AI regardless of correctness has D ≈ 0.

Immediately after each block, participants also give the **stated-perception outcome**: a 7-point trust rating ("How much do you trust this AI system's suggestions?") and a 7-point perceived-accuracy rating, averaged into a single perception score P.

### 2.2 Design

2 (advice correctness: correct / incorrect; within-participant, item-level) × 4 (label condition: **No-label**, **Source-only** ["AI-generated suggestion"], **Source+Confidence** [source tag plus a numeric model confidence score], **Source+Rationale** [source tag plus a one-sentence, item-specific reason to double-check, e.g., "this topic is one where the model is often overconfident"]) mixed design, with label condition manipulated between-participants to avoid carry-over/demand effects from seeing multiple label styles, and advice correctness manipulated within-participant to allow the discrimination index D to be computed per participant.

We add a **placebo label** arm (a content-free label of identical visual salience, e.g., "Suggestion #{item number}") to separate the effect of *any* salient annotation from the effect of AI-source disclosure specifically — this is the primary baseline against which No-label and the three real label conditions are compared, since a bare No-label vs. Source-only contrast confounds "labeling" with "adding a visually salient UI element."

### 2.3 Outcomes and the Perception–Behavior Gap statistic

For each label condition c, relative to the placebo baseline, we compute two standardized effect sizes on the same Cohen's-d scale:
- ΔP(c): standardized effect of condition c on stated perception P.
- ΔD(c): standardized effect of condition c on behavioral discrimination D.

We define **PBG(c) = ΔP(c) − ΔD(c)**. PBG(c) ≈ 0 indicates a label whose effect on stated trust is matched by an equivalent effect on behavioral calibration ("what people say tracks what they do"). PBG(c) ≫ 0 indicates a label that moves stated trust substantially more than it moves calibrated reliance — the dissociation pattern reported for sycophancy warning labels. PBG(c) < 0 (moving behavior more than stated perception) is possible but not predicted by prior work and would itself be a notable finding.

### 2.4 Baselines and ablations

- **Baselines**: No-label (standard control in the disclosure literature) and Placebo-label (isolates salience from content, addressed in §2.2).
- **Ablation 1 — label content**: Source-only vs. Source+Confidence vs. Source+Rationale isolates which *component* of a rich label (bare disclosure, numeric confidence, or qualitative rationale) drives any closing of the gap.
- **Ablation 2 — stakes**: a between-participants replication arm using high-stakes item framing (e.g., medical/financial advice items) vs. the low-stakes default (trivia/news-headline items), since prior disclosure work finds effects vary by content domain and perceived risk.
- **Ablation 3 — AI-literacy covariate**: a brief 6-item AI-literacy scale collected pre-task, entered as a covariate, since trust-calibration work finds literacy and prior AI exposure moderate reliance independent of any label.
- **Ablation 4 — rationale specificity**: within the Source+Rationale arm only, generic rationale ("AI can make mistakes") vs. item-specific rationale (varies per item), to test whether specificity — not just the presence of a rationale — drives calibration gains.

### 2.5 Sample size and power

Powering on D (the harder-to-move outcome) rather than P: assuming a target detectable between-condition difference in D of d = 0.35 (a conservative estimate given that WOA effects in the advice-taking literature are typically small-to-medium), two-tailed α = .05, power = .90, a two-group comparison requires n ≈ 172 per arm (G*Power-style normal approximation). With 5 arms (No-label, Placebo, Source-only, Source+Confidence, Source+Rationale) plus a stakes-replication arm, total N ≈ 1,050–1,200, in line with sample sizes used in the sycophancy-label study (N ≈ 2,610 across more cells) and other large disclosure experiments in this literature.

## 3. Results

We did not execute this protocol; the numbers below are worked, illustrative values consistent with effect sizes reported in the disclosure and trust-calibration literature we surveyed (small ΔD, small-to-medium ΔP, WOA baseline ≈ 0.4–0.5), presented to show what the analysis and PBG statistic would look like and to make our predictions falsifiable.

**Table 1. Stated perception (P) and behavioral discrimination (D) by label condition, relative to placebo baseline.**

| Condition        | ΔP (trust, d) | ΔD (discrimination, d) | PBG = ΔP − ΔD | 95% CI on PBG |
|-------------------|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| No-label vs. Placebo   | 0.03 (ns) | 0.02 (ns) | 0.01  | [−0.15, 0.17] |
| Source-only       | 0.31**    | 0.06 (ns) | 0.25** | [0.10, 0.40] |
| Source+Confidence | 0.44**    | 0.14*     | 0.30** | [0.15, 0.45] |
| Source+Rationale  | 0.39**    | 0.33**    | 0.06 (ns) | [−0.08, 0.20] |

*p<.05, **p<.01 (illustrative). D computed as WOA(correct) − WOA(incorrect); positive ΔD means the label increased the gap in reliance between correct and incorrect advice, i.e., improved calibration.

**Table 2. Raw WOA by advice correctness and condition (illustrative means, SD in parentheses).**

| Condition | WOA \| correct | WOA \| incorrect | D |
|---|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| Placebo | 0.47 (0.21) | 0.44 (0.22) | 0.03 |
| Source-only | 0.45 (0.20) | 0.40 (0.21) | 0.05 |
| Source+Confidence | 0.49 (0.19) | 0.38 (0.20) | 0.11 |
| Source+Rationale | 0.52 (0.18) | 0.30 (0.19) | 0.22 |

**Interpretation of the worked pattern.** Source-only labeling produces the classic dissociation: a clear, statistically robust bump in stated trust (ΔP = 0.31) with essentially no change in behavioral calibration (ΔD = 0.06, n.s.), giving a large PBG (0.25) — replicating, in a general protocol, the qualitative pattern reported for sycophancy warning labels. Adding a numeric confidence score partially closes the gap (ΔD rises to 0.14) but stated trust rises even more (ΔP = 0.44), so PBG remains large — consistent with concerns in the trust-calibration literature that bare confidence numbers are often over-trusted rather than used diagnostically. Only Source+Rationale yields a ΔD large enough to bring PBG down to a non-significant residual (0.06), i.e., its effect on stated trust is now matched by a roughly commensurate effect on behavioral discrimination. The stakes ablation (not tabulated) would be expected, based on the domain-sensitivity found in prior AIGC-label studies, to show larger ΔP shifts but similarly muted ΔD shifts in the high-stakes arm, widening PBG further for Source-only and Source+Confidence.

**Ablation 4 (rationale specificity, illustrative).** Item-specific rationale is predicted to outperform generic rationale on ΔD (e.g., 0.33 vs. 0.12), suggesting the gap-closing effect of Source+Rationale in Table 1 is driven by information content per item rather than merely the presence of extra text — a distinction that a bare "labels help" claim would miss.

## 4. Conclusion

Disclosure and transparency labels are usually evaluated on whether they change what people *say* about an AI system, not on whether they change how people actually *use* it — and the one large study that measured both found they can diverge. We formalize this as the Perception–Behavior Gap and propose a paired-outcome protocol and a single PBG statistic that let researchers detect, quantify, and compare this divergence across label designs within one study, rather than inferring it indirectly across separate literatures. Our worked results illustrate a falsifiable, actionable prediction: bare source disclosure and numeric confidence scores are likely to move stated trust more than they move calibrated behavior, while rationale-bearing labels — especially item-specific ones — are the more promising candidate for closing the gap. We see three immediate next steps: (i) run the protocol as specified here (§2.5 gives the power calculation) as a preregistered study; (ii) extend the behavioral outcome beyond WOA/switch-rate to downstream task accuracy, to test whether closing PBG also improves objective decision quality, not just discrimination; and (iii) apply the same paired-outcome logic to other socio-technical interventions currently evaluated on self-report alone (e.g., algorithmic-decision explanations, chatbot uncertainty phrasing), since the perception–behavior dissociation we discuss is unlikely to be specific to AI-source labels.
