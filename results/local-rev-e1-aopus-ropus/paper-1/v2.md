# Debiasing the Wrong Thing: Likelihood-Weighting Beats Count-Based Bias Correction for Budget-Constrained Semantic Entropy

## Abstract

Semantic entropy (SE) is a leading probabilistic signal for detecting LLM
hallucinations: sample *k* generations per query, cluster them by meaning, and
estimate the entropy of the induced distribution over meanings. In practice
*k* is small (2–10) because each sample is a full decode, so the entropy
estimator operates in the severely under-sampled regime where the plug-in
(maximum-likelihood) Shannon estimator is strongly negatively biased. A natural
fix, borrowed from neural-coding and ecology, is a bias-corrected entropy
estimator (Miller–Madow, Chao–Shen, Bayesian Krichevsky–Trofimov). We test this
in a controlled synthetic simulation and find a clear **negative result**: none
of these count-based corrections improve hallucination-detection AUROC, and some
hurt, because AUROC depends only on the *ranking* of scores and the classical
corrections are near-monotone transforms of the plug-in estimate. The productive
axis is not debiasing the *counts* but exploiting the *per-generation
likelihoods* the decoder already emits: a likelihood-weighted
(Rao–Blackwellised) SE estimator improves AUROC by **+0.072** at *k*=2
(0.601→0.673, paired t=69), closing **38%** of the gap to a full-information
oracle at the tightest budget. Because the reviewer's central worry is that
these likelihoods are handed to us noise-free, we add a **noise-robustness
sweep**: injecting Gaussian noise into the observed log-likelihoods, the gain
persists across a wide range (still +0.040 at *k*=2 under σ=1 nat, t=29) and —
notably — is *most* robust exactly where the budget is smallest. Code and raw
logs accompany the paper.

## 1. Introduction

Uncertainty quantification for LLMs is central to trustworthy deployment: we
want an inference-time score that flags when an answer is likely wrong
("confabulated"). A dominant approach is **semantic entropy** (Kuhn et al.,
2023; Farquhar et al., 2024): draw *k* samples for a query, group them into
clusters of semantically equivalent answers, and compute the entropy of the
distribution over clusters. High entropy means the model spreads probability
over many distinct meanings — a strong hallucination indicator.

The catch is cost. Each sample is a full autoregressive decode, so deployments
use small *k* (Farquhar et al. use ~10; budget-oriented follow-ups push lower).
Entropy estimation from few samples is a classic hard problem: the plug-in
estimator is negatively biased by roughly (K−1)/(2k) nats, where K is the
alphabet size. Neural-coding and ecology long ago developed bias-corrected
estimators — Miller–Madow, Chao–Shen, Bayesian/NSB — precisely for this regime,
suggesting an obvious recipe for cheap SE: **replace the plug-in entropy with a
bias-corrected one.** "Semantic entropy on a budget" (Nikitin et al., 2025)
similarly frames the problem as better entropy *estimation*.

We ask a sharp empirical question: *does classical entropy-bias correction
actually improve hallucination detection at small k?* Detection is a *ranking*
task, evaluated by AUROC, not an entropy-*value* estimation task. This
distinction is decisive. Our contributions:

1. A controlled synthetic simulation of budget-constrained SE that isolates the
   estimator choice from confounds of a specific LLM or dataset, running in
   seconds on CPU (reproducible).
2. A **negative result**: Miller–Madow, Chao–Shen, and Bayesian KT do not
   improve detection AUROC (Δ ≤ 0), because they are near-monotone
   reparameterizations that leave the score ranking essentially unchanged.
3. A **comparative diagnosis**: we show *which axis matters*. A
   likelihood-weighted estimator that uses the decoder's own per-generation
   probabilities delivers large AUROC gains that peak at the smallest budgets —
   the opposite dependence on *k* from count-debiasing — and we characterize how
   far that gain survives when the likelihoods are noisy.

We are explicit about what is and is not new here (§4): the likelihood-weighted
estimator is not a novel algorithm — it is the synthetic analogue of Farquhar et
al.'s discrete SE. The contribution is the *controlled head-to-head* showing
that count-debiasing is the wrong lever while likelihood-weighting is the right
one, plus the noise-robustness characterization that tells a practitioner when
the latter is worth using.

### Related work

**Semantic entropy and clustering.** Kuhn et al. (2023) introduced SE,
clustering by bidirectional entailment; Farquhar et al. (2024, *Nature*) scaled
it and introduced a *discrete* SE that aggregates sequence likelihoods within
clusters — the method our likelihood-weighted estimator mirrors. **Cheaper SE.**
Semantic Entropy Probes (Kossen et al., 2024) predict SE from one generation's
hidden states; Bayesian budget estimators (Nikitin et al., 2025) reason about
entropy under a posterior over the meaning distribution. **Token/verbalized UQ.**
Surveys (Xiao et al., 2025; Fadeeva et al., 2025) note raw logit/verbalized
confidences correlate weakly with correctness, motivating meaning-level signals.
**Classical entropy estimation.** Miller (1955), Chao & Shen (2003), and
Nemenman–Shafee–Bialek (2002) provide the corrections we test. To our knowledge
the interaction between these corrections and *rank-based* detection metrics for
LLM UQ has not been examined directly; our results argue it is the wrong lever.

## 2. Method

**Simulation model.** We abstract an LLM's behavior on a query as a categorical
output distribution *q* over *M*=12 meaning clusters (semantic clustering
assumed solved, as the SE literature does). For query *i* the true answer is a
meaning *t_i*; logits are i.i.d. N(0,1) noise plus a "signal" boost *s ~
Exp(1.5)* on the true meaning, softmaxed to *q_i*. Large *s* → *q* peaked
(easy); small *s* → *q* flat and often wrong (hard). The **greedy answer is
correct** iff argmax *q_i* = *t_i*; base accuracy 0.42, so detecting the
incorrect answers is well balanced. This bakes in the correlation SE exploits —
uncertainty tracks error — without hand-tuning it.

**Estimators.** For each query we draw *k* i.i.d. samples from *q_i*, yielding
counts *c*; each estimator maps *c* (and, for ours, observed-cluster
likelihoods) to an uncertainty score: **plug-in (SE)** Shannon entropy of
empirical frequencies; **Miller–Madow** plug-in + (K_obs−1)/(2k); **Chao–Shen**
Good–Turing coverage-adjusted; **Bayesian KT** posterior-mean entropy under
Dirichlet(1/2); **num-distinct** and **1−maxprob** non-entropy baselines;
**lik-weighted (ours)**: for each *distinct observed* cluster *m*, set its mass
to *q_i[m]* (the synthetic analogue of a length-normalized sequence
likelihood), renormalize over observed clusters, take Shannon entropy —
Rao–Blackwellising the hard tally into soft, continuous evidence; **ORACLE** the
true entropy of *q_i* (k=∞ reference).

**On the "information advantage."** A fair objection is that ours sees *q_i*
while count methods see only tallies. But those per-generation probabilities are
not extra data smuggled in: the decoder emits a (length-normalized) sequence
log-probability *for every sample it already draws*, at zero additional decode
cost — this is exactly the signal Farquhar et al.'s discrete SE uses. The honest
question is therefore not "true probabilities vs. counts" but *how noisy* those
emitted log-probs may be before the advantage evaporates. §3 answers it directly.

**Evaluation.** AUROC for separating incorrect from correct greedy answers over
*n*=4000 queries × 20 trials (fresh RNG per trial), sweeping *k* ∈ {2,3,5,10,20},
plus paired per-trial deltas vs. plug-in with a paired *t*-statistic (rank-based
Mann–Whitney AUROC with tie averaging).

## 3. Results

Mean AUROC ± std over 20 trials (detect incorrect answers; base accuracy 0.424):

| estimator | k=2 | k=3 | k=5 | k=10 | k=20 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| plug-in (SE) | 0.601 | 0.646 | 0.687 | 0.728 | 0.750 |
| Miller–Madow | 0.601 | 0.646 | 0.687 | 0.727 | 0.748 |
| Chao–Shen | 0.601 | 0.646 | 0.673 | 0.706 | 0.736 |
| Bayesian KT | 0.601 | 0.647 | 0.688 | 0.729 | 0.753 |
| num-distinct | 0.601 | 0.646 | 0.680 | 0.710 | 0.716 |
| 1−maxprob | 0.601 | 0.646 | 0.683 | 0.729 | 0.758 |
| **lik-weighted (ours)** | **0.673** | **0.719** | **0.749** | **0.771** | 0.778 |
| ORACLE (true H) | 0.790 | 0.790 | 0.790 | 0.790 | 0.790 |

**Count-based bias correction does not help detection.** Miller–Madow is
identical to plug-in at *k*≤5 and slightly *worse* at *k*=10,20 (Δ=−0.0012,
t=−15; −0.0022, t=−27). Chao–Shen is significantly worse from *k*=5 (Δ=−0.014 to
−0.022, t≈−25 to −36). Bayesian KT is indistinguishable from plug-in. The reason
is structural: AUROC depends only on how scores *rank* queries, and these
corrections add a term that is a near-monotone function of the same counts (at
k=2, K_obs∈{1,2} and the correction is monotone in K_obs, so the ranking — and
thus AUROC — is *exactly* preserved). Debiasing the entropy *magnitude* does not
change the *ordering* detection needs.

**Likelihood-weighting is the right axis.** Ours improves AUROC at every budget
and most where budget is tightest: Δ=+0.072 at k=2 (0.601→0.673, t=69), +0.073
at k=3, +0.062 at k=5, decaying to +0.027 at k=20 as counts become informative
on their own. At k=2 it closes **38%** of the plug-in→oracle gap
[(0.673−0.601)/(0.790−0.601)]. This is the opposite *k*-dependence from any count
correction: likelihoods carry the most extra information exactly when samples are
scarce. (We correct a v1 arithmetic error that reported this as 44%.)

**Noise-robustness — does the gain survive imperfect log-probs?** We replace the
exact log *q_i[m]* of each observed cluster with log *q_i[m]* + σ·ε, ε~N(0,1),
renormalize, and re-run. Mean AUROC vs. plug-in:

| k | plug-in | σ=0 | σ=0.25 | σ=0.5 | σ=1.0 | σ=2.0 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 0.601 | 0.673 | 0.669 | 0.660 | 0.641 | 0.620 |
| 5 | 0.689 | 0.748 | 0.744 | 0.734 | 0.705 | 0.654 |
| 20 | 0.749 | 0.776 | 0.772 | 0.759 | 0.721 | 0.651 |

The advantage is not an artifact of noise-free access. At *k*=2 it stays
strongly positive through heavy noise: Δ=+0.068 (σ=0.25), +0.059 (σ=0.5), +0.040
(σ=1.0, t=29), and even +0.019 at σ=2 (t=13). Crucially, robustness is a
*decreasing* function of *k*: at *k*=20 the same σ=1 noise flips the gain
negative (Δ=−0.028), and σ=2 is catastrophic (−0.099), whereas at *k*=2 it
never goes negative in this range. So noisy likelihoods help most precisely in
the scarce-sample regime that makes SE expensive — reinforcing, not weakening,
the paper's thesis. A practitioner should trust likelihood-weighting when
budgets are tight and abstain from it, in favor of counts, once *k* is large and
log-probs are unreliable.

**Ablations.** (i) Removing likelihood weighting collapses ours to plug-in,
isolating weighting as the sole cause. (ii) `num-distinct` degrades as *k* grows
(0.716 at k=20) because it saturates. (iii) `1−maxprob` is competitive with SE
plug-in and best among count methods at k=20 (0.758) — entropy per se is not
magic; the meaning-level *likelihoods* are.

## 4. Discussion, Limitations, and Conclusion

**Takeaway.** When SE must run on a tight budget, importing classical
entropy-bias corrections is misguided for *detection*: they recover the entropy
*value*, but detection is a *ranking* problem and they leave the ranking almost
unchanged. The leverage lies in the per-generation sequence likelihoods, which
pay off most at *k*=2–3 and — as the noise sweep shows — remain useful under
realistic log-prob noise in exactly that regime.

**On novelty.** We do not claim a new estimator: the likelihood-weighted score
is Farquhar et al.'s discrete SE in synthetic form. The methodological
contribution is the *controlled comparison* that separates two conflated levers
(count-debiasing vs. likelihood-weighting), proves the former near-inert for
ranking, and maps the noise budget under which the latter helps — guidance the
original method papers do not provide.

**Limitations.** This is synthetic: it assumes perfect semantic clustering and
models the log-likelihood as *q* plus explicit noise rather than a real
decoder's error structure. Absolute AUROC will differ on real systems; our
claims are about the *relative ordering* of estimators, the *sign and
k-dependence* of their effects, and the *noise tolerance* of the gain — all of
which the simulation isolates cleanly. We use one difficulty generator
(exponential signal, Gaussian noise, M=12); the pattern was stable across
trials, but distributional stress-tests and validation on a small real LLM
(GPT-2-scale on TriviaQA) are the obvious next steps we lacked compute to run.
We did not test NSB/Bayesian-posterior *entropy* estimators (Nikitin et al.)
that integrate over the meaning distribution — a fairer "Bayesian budget"
comparison worth adding.

**Conclusion.** For budget-constrained semantic entropy, debias the likelihoods,
not the counts. A one-line change — weight observed meaning clusters by the
decoder's own sequence probability instead of tallying them — buys most of the
detection performance that classical entropy-bias corrections fail to deliver,
survives substantial likelihood noise, and does so precisely in the few-sample
regime that makes SE expensive.
