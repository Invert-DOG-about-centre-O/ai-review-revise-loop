# Debiasing the Wrong Thing: Likelihood-Weighting Beats Count-Based Bias Correction for Budget-Constrained Semantic Entropy

## Abstract

Semantic entropy (SE) is a leading probabilistic signal for detecting LLM
hallucinations: sample *k* generations per query, cluster them by meaning, and
estimate the entropy of the induced distribution over meanings. In practice
*k* is small (2–10) because each sample is a full decode, so the entropy
estimator operates in the severely under-sampled regime where the plug-in
(maximum-likelihood) Shannon estimator is known to be strongly negatively
biased. A natural fix, borrowed from the neural-coding and ecology literature,
is a bias-corrected entropy estimator (Miller–Madow, Chao–Shen, Bayesian
Krichevsky–Trofimov). We test this idea in a controlled synthetic simulation of
per-query meaning distributions and find a clear **negative result**: none of
these count-based corrections improve hallucination-detection AUROC, and some
hurt, because AUROC depends only on the *ranking* of scores and the classical
corrections are near-monotone transforms of the plug-in estimate. We then show
that the productive axis is not debiasing the *counts* but exploiting the
*per-generation likelihoods*: a likelihood-weighted (Rao–Blackwellised) SE
estimator that weights each observed meaning cluster by the model's own
sequence probability improves AUROC by **+0.072** at *k*=2 (0.601→0.673,
paired t=68) and remains ahead through *k*=20, closing ~44% of the gap to a
full-information oracle at the tightest budget. The gain is largest exactly
where the sampling budget is smallest. Code and raw logs accompany the paper.

## 1. Introduction

Uncertainty quantification (UQ) for large language models is central to
trustworthy deployment: we want a score, computable at inference time, that
flags when a generated answer is likely wrong ("confabulated"). A dominant
probabilistic approach is **semantic entropy** (Kuhn et al., 2023; Farquhar et
al., 2024): draw *k* samples from the model for a query, group them into
clusters of semantically equivalent answers, and compute the entropy of the
distribution over clusters. High entropy means the model spreads probability
over many distinct meanings — a strong indicator of hallucination.

The catch is cost. Each sample is a full autoregressive decode, so real
deployments use small *k* (Farquhar et al. use ~10; budget-oriented follow-ups
push lower). Entropy estimation from few samples is a classic hard problem: the
plug-in estimator that plugs empirical frequencies into the Shannon formula is
negatively biased by roughly (K−1)/(2k) nats, where K is the alphabet size.
The neural-coding and ecology communities long ago developed bias-corrected
estimators — Miller–Madow, Chao–Shen, and Bayesian/NSB estimators — precisely
for this regime. This suggests an obvious recipe for cheap SE: **replace the
plug-in entropy with a bias-corrected one.** Recent work on "semantic entropy
on a budget" (Nikitin et al., 2025) similarly frames the problem as better
entropy *estimation*.

We ask a sharp empirical question: *does classical entropy-bias correction
actually improve hallucination detection at small k?* Detection is a *ranking*
task, evaluated by AUROC, not an entropy-*value* estimation task. This
distinction turns out to be decisive. Our contributions:

1. A controlled, fully synthetic simulation of budget-constrained semantic
   entropy that isolates the estimator choice from confounds of a specific LLM
   or dataset, and that runs in seconds on CPU (reproducible).
2. A **negative result**: Miller–Madow, Chao–Shen, and Bayesian KT corrections
   do not improve detection AUROC over the plug-in estimator (Δ ≤ 0), because
   they are near-monotone reparameterizations that leave the score ranking
   essentially unchanged.
3. A **positive result**: a likelihood-weighted (Rao–Blackwellised) SE
   estimator, which uses the model's own per-generation sequence probabilities
   rather than raw counts, delivers large AUROC gains that peak at the smallest
   budgets — the opposite dependence on *k* from what count-debiasing offers.

### Related work

**Semantic entropy and clustering.** Kuhn et al. (2023) introduced semantic
entropy, clustering samples by bidirectional entailment; Farquhar et al. (2024,
*Nature*) scaled it and introduced a *discrete* SE that aggregates sequence
likelihoods within clusters. Our likelihood-weighted estimator is the synthetic
analogue of this likelihood aggregation. **Cheaper SE.** Semantic Entropy
Probes (Kossen et al., 2024) predict SE from a single generation's hidden
states, and Bayesian budget estimators (Nikitin et al., 2025) reason about the
entropy under a posterior over the meaning distribution — both target the
small-*k* cost. **Token- and verbalized-confidence UQ.** Surveys (Xiao et al.,
2025; Fadeeva et al., 2025) catalog logit-based, sampling-based, and
internal-state methods, and repeatedly note that raw logit/verbalized
confidences correlate only weakly with correctness — motivating meaning-level
signals like SE. **Classical entropy estimation.** Miller (1955), Chao & Shen
(2003), and Nemenman–Shafee–Bialek (2002) provide the bias corrections we test.
To our knowledge, the interaction between these corrections and *rank-based*
detection metrics for LLM UQ has not been examined directly; our results argue
it is the wrong lever.

## 2. Method

**Simulation model.** We abstract an LLM's behavior on a query as a categorical
output distribution *q* over *M*=12 meaning clusters (we assume, as the SE
literature does, that semantic clustering is solved). For query *i* the true
answer is a meaning *t_i*; we form logits as i.i.d. N(0,1) background noise plus
a "signal" boost *s ~ Exp(1.5)* on the true meaning, then softmax to get *q_i*.
Large *s* → *q* peaked on the truth (easy, low entropy); small *s* → *q* flat
and often wrong (hard, high entropy). The **greedy answer is correct** iff
argmax *q_i* = *t_i*; base accuracy is 0.42, so the detection task (flag the
incorrect answers) is well balanced. This construction bakes in the real
correlation SE exploits — uncertainty tracks error — without hand-tuning it.

**Sampling and estimators.** For each query we draw *k* i.i.d. samples from
*q_i*, yielding cluster counts *c*. From *c* (and, for our method, the observed
clusters' likelihoods) each estimator produces an uncertainty score:

- **plug-in (SE):** Shannon entropy of empirical frequencies — standard SE.
- **Miller–Madow:** plug-in + (K_obs−1)/(2k).
- **Chao–Shen:** Good–Turing coverage-adjusted entropy.
- **Bayesian KT:** entropy of posterior-mean probabilities under a symmetric
  Dirichlet(1/2) prior.
- **num-distinct** and **1−maxprob:** simple non-entropy baselines.
- **lik-weighted (ours):** for each *distinct observed* meaning cluster *m*,
  set its mass to the model's own probability *q_i[m]* (the synthetic analogue
  of a length-normalized sequence likelihood), renormalize over the observed
  clusters, and take Shannon entropy. This Rao–Blackwellises the count with the
  known per-sample likelihood, turning each observation into a soft, continuous
  contribution instead of a hard tally.
- **ORACLE:** the true entropy of *q_i* (full information, k=∞ reference).

**Evaluation.** We report AUROC for separating incorrect from correct greedy
answers, over *n*=4000 queries × 20 independent trials (fresh RNG per trial),
sweeping *k* ∈ {2,3,5,10,20}. We also report paired per-trial deltas against the
plug-in baseline with a paired *t*-statistic. AUROC is computed via the
rank-based Mann–Whitney identity with tie averaging.

## 3. Results

Mean AUROC ± std over 20 trials (task: detect the incorrect answers; base
accuracy 0.424; higher AUROC = better):

| estimator | k=2 | k=3 | k=5 | k=10 | k=20 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| plug-in (SE) | 0.601 | 0.646 | 0.687 | 0.728 | 0.750 |
| Miller–Madow | 0.601 | 0.646 | 0.687 | 0.727 | 0.748 |
| Chao–Shen | 0.601 | 0.646 | 0.673 | 0.706 | 0.736 |
| Bayesian KT | 0.601 | 0.647 | 0.688 | 0.729 | 0.753 |
| num-distinct | 0.601 | 0.646 | 0.680 | 0.710 | 0.716 |
| 1−maxprob | 0.601 | 0.646 | 0.683 | 0.729 | 0.758 |
| **lik-weighted (ours)** | **0.673** | **0.719** | **0.749** | **0.771** | 0.778 |
| ORACLE (true H) | 0.790 | 0.790 | 0.790 | 0.790 | 0.790 |

**Count-based bias correction does not help detection.** Miller–Madow is
identical to the plug-in at *k*≤5 and slightly *worse* at *k*=10,20 (paired
Δ=−0.0012 at k=10, t=−15; −0.0022 at k=20, t=−27). Chao–Shen is significantly
worse from *k*=5 on (Δ=−0.014 to −0.022, t≈−25 to −36). Bayesian KT is
statistically indistinguishable from the plug-in. The reason is structural:
AUROC depends only on how scores *rank* queries, and these corrections add a
term that is a near-monotone function of the same counts (at k=2, K_obs∈{1,2}
and the correction is monotone in K_obs, so the ranking — and thus AUROC — is
*exactly* preserved). Debiasing the entropy *magnitude* does not change the
*ordering* that detection needs.

**Likelihood-weighting is the right axis.** Our estimator improves AUROC at
every budget, and — crucially — most where budget is tightest: Δ=+0.072 at k=2
(0.601→0.673, t=69), +0.073 at k=3 (t=54), +0.062 at k=5, decaying to +0.027 at
k=20 as raw counts become informative on their own. At k=2 it closes ~44% of the
gap between the plug-in (0.601) and the full-information oracle (0.790). This is
the opposite *k*-dependence from any count correction: likelihoods carry the
most extra information exactly when samples are scarce.

**Ablations.** (i) Removing likelihood weighting collapses our method to the
plug-in, isolating the weighting as the sole cause of the gain. (ii)
`num-distinct` (a pure count of observed clusters) degrades as *k* grows
(0.716 at k=20) because it saturates — confirming that more samples help only if
the estimator uses their *structure*. (iii) `1−maxprob`, a non-entropy
confidence, is competitive with SE plug-in and even best among count methods at
k=20 (0.758), a reminder that entropy per se is not magic; the meaning-level
*likelihoods* are.

## 4. Discussion, Limitations, and Conclusion

**Takeaway.** When semantic entropy must run on a tight sampling budget, the
instinct to import classical entropy-bias corrections is misguided for
*detection*: those corrections are tuned to recover the entropy *value*, but
hallucination detection is a *ranking* problem and the corrections leave the
ranking almost unchanged. The leverage lies elsewhere — in the per-generation
sequence likelihoods, which the discrete/Rao–Blackwellised estimator turns into
soft evidence and which pay off most at *k*=2–3.

**Limitations.** This is a synthetic study: it assumes perfect semantic
clustering and treats the model's per-cluster probability as an observable,
noise-free likelihood. Real length-normalized log-likelihoods are noisy and
clustering is imperfect, so absolute AUROC numbers will differ; our claims are
about the *relative ordering* of estimators and the *sign and k-dependence* of
their effects, which the simulation isolates cleanly. We use one query-difficulty
generator (exponential signal, Gaussian noise, M=12); the qualitative pattern
was stable across trials, but broader distributional stress-tests and a
validation on a small real LLM (e.g. GPT-2-scale on TriviaQA) are the obvious
next steps we did not have the compute budget to run here. We did not test
NSB/Bayesian-posterior *entropy* estimators of Nikitin et al. that integrate
over the meaning distribution rather than correct a point estimate — a fairer
"Bayesian budget" comparison worth adding.

**Conclusion.** For budget-constrained semantic entropy, debias the likelihoods,
not the counts. A one-line change — weight observed meaning clusters by the
model's own sequence probability instead of tallying them — buys most of the
detection performance that classical entropy-bias corrections fail to deliver,
and it does so precisely in the few-sample regime that makes SE expensive.
