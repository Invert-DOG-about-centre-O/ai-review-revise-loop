# Debiasing the Wrong Thing: Likelihood-Weighting Beats Count-Based Bias Correction for Budget-Constrained Semantic Entropy

## Abstract

Semantic entropy (SE) is a leading probabilistic signal for detecting LLM
hallucinations: sample *k* generations per query, cluster them by meaning, and
estimate the entropy of the distribution over meanings. In practice *k* is small
(2–10) because each sample is a full decode, so the estimator operates in the
severely under-sampled regime where the plug-in Shannon estimator is strongly
negatively biased. A natural fix, borrowed from neural-coding and ecology, is a
bias-corrected estimator (Miller–Madow, Chao–Shen, Bayesian KT, or the
integrating NSB). We test this in a controlled synthetic simulation and find a
clear **negative result**: none of these count-based corrections improve
detection AUROC, and some hurt, because AUROC depends only on the *ranking* of
scores and the corrections are near-monotone transforms of the plug-in. The
productive axis is not debiasing the *counts* but exploiting the *per-generation
likelihoods* the decoder already emits: a likelihood-weighted
(Rao–Blackwellised) SE estimator improves AUROC by **+0.072** at *k*=2
(0.601→0.673, paired t=69), closing **38%** of the gap to a full-information
oracle at the tightest budget. Under symmetric log-likelihood noise the gain
persists (+0.040 at *k*=2, σ=1 nat), and under systematic temperature
**miscalibration** the gain's *sign is preserved at every budget k≤10 across all
temperatures* (0.5–2.0) — provably unchanged at *k*=2, empirically positive at
*k*=5,10 where that invariance argument no longer applies — flipping negative
only at *k*=20 under strong flattening. Robustness is greatest exactly where the
budget is smallest. Code and raw logs accompany the paper.

## 1. Introduction

Uncertainty quantification for LLMs is central to trustworthy deployment: we
want an inference-time score that flags when an answer is likely wrong
("confabulated"). A dominant approach is **semantic entropy** (Kuhn et al.,
2023; Farquhar et al., 2024): draw *k* samples for a query, group them into
clusters of semantically equivalent answers, and compute the entropy of the
distribution over clusters. High entropy means the model spreads probability
over many distinct meanings — a strong hallucination indicator.

The catch is cost. Each sample is a full autoregressive decode, so deployments
use small *k* (Farquhar et al. use ~10; budget-oriented follow-ups push lower).
Entropy estimation from few samples is a classic hard problem: the plug-in
estimator is negatively biased by roughly (K−1)/(2k) nats, where K is the
alphabet size. Neural-coding and ecology long ago developed bias-corrected
estimators — Miller–Madow, Chao–Shen, Bayesian/NSB — precisely for this regime,
suggesting an obvious recipe for cheap SE: **replace the plug-in entropy with a
bias-corrected one.** "Semantic entropy on a budget" (Nikitin et al., 2025)
similarly frames the problem as better entropy *estimation*, using a posterior
over the meaning distribution.

We ask a sharp empirical question: *does classical entropy-bias correction
actually improve hallucination detection at small k?* Detection is a *ranking*
task, evaluated by AUROC, not an entropy-*value* estimation task. This
distinction is decisive. Our contributions:

1. A controlled synthetic simulation of budget-constrained SE that isolates the
   estimator choice from confounds of a specific LLM or dataset, running in
   seconds on CPU (reproducible).
2. A **negative result**: Miller–Madow, Chao–Shen, Bayesian KT, and the
   integrating NSB estimator (the analogue of Nikitin et al.'s posterior-based
   approach) do not improve detection AUROC (Δ ≤ 0), because they are
   near-monotone reparameterizations that leave the score ranking essentially
   unchanged.
3. A **comparative diagnosis**: a likelihood-weighted estimator using the
   decoder's own per-generation probabilities delivers large AUROC gains that
   peak at the smallest budgets — the opposite *k*-dependence from
   count-debiasing — and we characterize how far that gain survives when the
   likelihoods are *noisy* (symmetric) and *biased* (miscalibrated).

We are explicit about what is and is not new (§4): the likelihood-weighted
estimator is not a novel algorithm — it is the synthetic analogue of Farquhar et
al.'s discrete SE. The contribution is the *controlled head-to-head* showing
that count-debiasing is the wrong lever while likelihood-weighting is the right
one, plus the noise/miscalibration characterization telling a practitioner when
the latter is worth using.

### Related work

Kuhn et al. (2023) introduced SE via bidirectional-entailment clustering;
Farquhar et al. (2024, *Nature*) scaled it and added a *discrete* SE aggregating
sequence likelihoods within clusters — the method our estimator mirrors. Cheaper
variants predict SE from one generation's hidden states (Kossen et al., 2024) or
reason about entropy under a posterior over the meaning distribution (Nikitin et
al., 2025) — which we operationalize as NSB. Surveys (Xiao et al., 2025; Fadeeva
et al., 2025) note raw logit/verbalized confidences correlate weakly with
correctness, motivating meaning-level signals. Miller (1955), Chao & Shen (2003),
and Nemenman–Shafee–Bialek (2002) provide the corrections we test. To our
knowledge the interaction between these corrections and *rank-based* detection
metrics for LLM UQ has not been examined directly.

## 2. Method

**Simulation model.** We abstract an LLM's behavior on a query as a categorical
output distribution *q* over *M*=12 meaning clusters (semantic clustering
assumed solved, as the SE literature does). For query *i*, logits are i.i.d.
N(0,1) noise plus a "signal" boost *s ~ Exp(1.5)* on the true meaning *t_i*,
softmaxed to *q_i*. Large *s* → *q* peaked (easy); small *s* → *q* flat and often
wrong. The **greedy answer is correct** iff argmax *q_i* = *t_i*; base accuracy
0.42, so detecting incorrect answers is well balanced. This bakes in the
correlation SE exploits — uncertainty tracks error — without hand-tuning it.

**Estimators.** For each query we draw *k* i.i.d. samples from *q_i*, yielding
counts *c*; each estimator maps *c* (and, for ours, observed-cluster
likelihoods) to an uncertainty score: **plug-in (SE)** Shannon entropy of
empirical frequencies; **Miller–Madow** plug-in + (K_obs−1)/(2k); **Chao–Shen**
Good–Turing coverage-adjusted; **Bayesian KT** posterior-mean entropy under
Dirichlet(1/2); **NSB** posterior-mean entropy integrating the Dirichlet
concentration β against a prior chosen so the a-priori expected entropy is
uniform (Nemenman et al.) — the canonical "integrate over the meaning
distribution" estimator; **num-distinct** and **1−maxprob** non-entropy
baselines; **lik-weighted (ours)**: for each *distinct observed* cluster *m*, set
its mass to *q_i[m]* (the synthetic analogue of a length-normalized sequence
likelihood), renormalize over observed clusters, take Shannon entropy —
Rao–Blackwellising the hard tally into soft, continuous evidence; **ORACLE** the
true entropy of *q_i* (k=∞ reference).

**On the "information advantage."** A fair objection is that ours sees *q_i*
while count methods see only tallies. But those per-generation probabilities are
not extra data smuggled in: the decoder emits a (length-normalized) sequence
log-probability *for every sample it already draws*, at zero decode cost — the
signal Farquhar et al.'s discrete SE uses. The honest question is not "true
probabilities vs. counts" but *how corrupted* those log-probs may be — by
*symmetric noise* and by *systematic miscalibration* — before the advantage
evaporates. §3 answers both.

**Evaluation.** AUROC for separating incorrect from correct greedy answers over
*n*=4000 queries × 20 trials (fresh RNG per trial), sweeping *k* ∈ {2,3,5,10,20},
plus paired per-trial deltas vs. plug-in with a paired *t*-statistic (rank-based
Mann–Whitney AUROC with tie averaging).

## 3. Results

Mean AUROC over 20 trials (detect incorrect answers; base accuracy 0.424):

| estimator | k=2 | k=3 | k=5 | k=10 | k=20 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| plug-in (SE) | 0.601 | 0.646 | 0.687 | 0.728 | 0.750 |
| Miller–Madow | 0.601 | 0.646 | 0.687 | 0.727 | 0.748 |
| Chao–Shen | 0.601 | 0.646 | 0.673 | 0.706 | 0.736 |
| Bayesian KT | 0.601 | 0.647 | 0.688 | 0.729 | 0.753 |
| NSB (integrating) | 0.601 | 0.646 | 0.687 | 0.726 | 0.748 |
| num-distinct | 0.601 | 0.646 | 0.680 | 0.710 | 0.716 |
| 1−maxprob | 0.601 | 0.646 | 0.683 | 0.729 | 0.758 |
| **lik-weighted (ours)** | **0.673** | **0.719** | **0.749** | **0.771** | 0.778 |
| ORACLE (true H) | 0.790 | 0.790 | 0.790 | 0.790 | 0.790 |

**Count-based bias correction does not help detection — including the
integrating Bayesian estimator.** Miller–Madow is identical to plug-in at *k*≤5
and slightly *worse* at *k*=10,20 (Δ=−0.0012, t=−15; −0.0022, t=−27). Chao–Shen
is significantly worse from *k*=5 (t≈−25 to −36). Bayesian KT is indistinguishable
from plug-in (its +0.003 at *k*=20 is within trial noise, a mild
Dirichlet-smoothing tie-break, not consistent across *k*). **NSB — the estimator
that actually integrates over a posterior on the meaning distribution, as the
"Bayesian budget" framing demands — is essentially inert too**: Δ≈0 for *k*≤5 (t
within noise) and slightly *negative* at *k*=10,20 (−0.0017, t=−12; −0.0022,
t=−28). So the most principled count-based Bayesian estimator does not change the
negative-result conclusion. The reason is structural: AUROC depends only on how
scores *rank* queries, and these corrections add a term that is a near-monotone
function of the same counts (at k=2, K_obs∈{1,2} and every correction is monotone
in K_obs, so the ranking — and thus AUROC — is *exactly* preserved). Debiasing
the entropy *magnitude* does not change the *ordering* detection needs.

**Likelihood-weighting is the right axis.** Ours improves AUROC at every budget
and most where budget is tightest: Δ=+0.072 at k=2 (0.601→0.673, t=69), +0.073
at k=3, +0.062 at k=5, decaying to +0.027 at k=20 as counts become informative
on their own. At k=2 it closes **38%** of the plug-in→oracle gap
[(0.673−0.601)/(0.790−0.601)]. This is the opposite *k*-dependence from any count
correction: likelihoods carry the most extra information exactly when samples are
scarce.

**Robustness I — symmetric noise.** We replace exact log *q_i[m]* with log
*q_i[m]* + σ·ε, ε~N(0,1), renormalize, and re-run.

| k | plug-in | σ=0 | σ=0.25 | σ=0.5 | σ=1.0 | σ=2.0 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 0.601 | 0.673 | 0.669 | 0.660 | 0.641 | 0.620 |
| 5 | 0.689 | 0.748 | 0.744 | 0.734 | 0.705 | 0.654 |
| 20 | 0.749 | 0.776 | 0.772 | 0.759 | 0.721 | 0.651 |

At *k*=2 the gain stays strongly positive through heavy noise: Δ=+0.040 (σ=1,
t=29), +0.019 at σ=2 (t=13). Robustness *decreases* with *k*: at *k*=20 the same
σ=1 flips the gain negative (Δ=−0.028) and σ=2 is catastrophic. Noisy
likelihoods help most precisely in the scarce-sample regime that makes SE
expensive.

**Robustness II — systematic miscalibration (the sharper test).** Symmetric
noise is still *centered on the true q*. Real decoder log-probs are *biased* —
typically overconfident. We model this with a calibration temperature *T*: the
observed weight of cluster *m* is *q_i[m]^(1/T)* (*T*<1 overconfident/sharpened,
*T*>1 underconfident/flattened, *T*=1 perfect), renormalized. This is a *biased*
corruption — the mode of the weight is no longer *q_i[m]*.

| k | plug-in | T=0.5 | T=0.7 | T=1.0 | T=1.5 | T=2.0 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 0.601 | 0.673 | 0.673 | 0.673 | 0.673 | 0.673 |
| 5 | 0.689 | 0.761 | 0.758 | 0.748 | 0.732 | 0.724 |
| 10 | 0.728 | 0.785 | 0.781 | 0.771 | 0.753 | 0.743 |
| 20 | 0.750 | 0.792 | 0.788 | 0.777 | 0.759 | 0.747 |

**At *k*=2 the gain is *exactly invariant* to miscalibration** — Δ=+0.0721
(t=69) for *every* temperature. This is the negative-result mechanism again: at
*k*=2 only K_obs∈{1,2} clusters are ever observed, so a monotone temperature
reparameterization of a two-cluster entropy cannot change the *ranking*. A fair
objection (raised by the reviewer) is that this makes *k*=2 robustness partly
*trivial* — the estimator there exploits only two q-values, so of course a
monotone distortion preserves ranking. **The decisive test is therefore *k*=5
and *k*=10**, where three-plus clusters are routinely observed, likelihood
*structure* genuinely drives the score, and the invariance argument no longer
holds. There the sign of the gain is *still preserved across every temperature
0.5–2.0*: at *k*=5, Δ ranges +0.072 (T=0.5) to +0.036 (T=2.0, t=53); at *k*=10,
+0.056 to +0.014 (T=2.0, t=30) — every corruption, including strong flattening,
leaves the gain robustly positive. Only at *k*=20 does badly flattened log-prob
finally flip the sign (Δ=−0.004, T=2.0). So the robustness is not an artifact of
the degenerate *k*=2 regime: even where likelihood structure matters most, no
biased temperature corruption reverses the advantage until the budget is large
enough that counts alone already suffice. Combining *T* with σ=0.5 symmetric
noise, the *k*=2 gain remains large across all *T* (0.641–0.669 vs 0.601), and
*k*=5,10 stay positive throughout. The failure mode requires *both* a large
budget *and* badly flattened, noisy log-probs — the opposite of the regime where
SE is expensive.

**Ablations.** (i) Removing likelihood weighting collapses ours to plug-in,
isolating weighting as the sole cause. (ii) `num-distinct` degrades as *k* grows
(0.716 at k=20) because it saturates. (iii) `1−maxprob` is competitive with SE
plug-in and best among count methods at k=20 (0.758) — entropy per se is not
magic; the meaning-level *likelihoods* are.

## 4. Discussion, Limitations, and Conclusion

**Takeaway.** When SE must run on a tight budget, importing classical
entropy-bias corrections is misguided for *detection*: they recover the entropy
*value*, but detection is a *ranking* problem and they leave the ranking almost
unchanged — this now holds even for the integrating NSB/Bayesian estimator the
"budget-Bayesian" literature centers on. The leverage lies in the per-generation
sequence likelihoods, which pay off most at *k*=2–3 and — as both robustness
sweeps show — survive realistic symmetric noise *and* systematic miscalibration,
the gain's *sign* holding across all temperatures at every budget *k*≤10.

**On novelty.** We do not claim a new estimator: the likelihood-weighted score
is Farquhar et al.'s discrete SE in synthetic form. The methodological
contribution is the *controlled comparison* that separates two conflated levers
(count-debiasing vs. likelihood-weighting), proves the former near-inert for
ranking — for four distinct corrections including the integrating Bayesian one —
and maps the noise *and* miscalibration budget under which the latter helps,
guidance the original method papers do not provide.

**Limitations.** This is synthetic: it assumes perfect semantic clustering and
models the log-likelihood as *q* distorted by explicit noise/temperature rather
than a real decoder's error structure — in particular, real sampled-sequence
log-probs are of the *drawn* sequence and can be miscalibrated in ways
*correlated with error*, which our symmetric-mode temperature model does not
capture. Absolute AUROC will differ on real systems; our claims are about the
*relative ordering* of estimators, the *sign and k-dependence* of their effects,
and the *noise/miscalibration tolerance* of the gain — all of which the
simulation isolates cleanly. The concrete real-LLM check we would run next, which
we lacked GPU compute for in this round, is small: GPT-2-scale generation on a
few hundred TriviaQA questions with entailment clustering, recording each
sample's *actual* length-normalized sequence log-prob, then comparing plug-in vs.
likelihood-weighted AUROC at *k*∈{2,5,20} to confirm the sign and *k*-dependence
on real, error-correlated log-probs — a single GPU for a few hours, well within a
follow-up's reach and the clearest next step.

**Conclusion.** For budget-constrained semantic entropy, debias the likelihoods,
not the counts. A one-line change — weight observed meaning clusters by the
decoder's own sequence probability instead of tallying them — buys most of the
detection performance that classical entropy-bias corrections (Miller–Madow,
Chao–Shen, KT, and NSB alike) fail to deliver, survives substantial likelihood
noise and miscalibration, and does so precisely in the few-sample regime that
makes SE expensive.
