# Confidence Is Not Comparable Across Groups: The Distributional Cost of Confidence-Based Reliance in Human-AI Teams

## Abstract

Appropriate-reliance research seeks decision rules that let a human accept correct AI advice and reject incorrect advice, most commonly by routing a case to the AI when its confidence exceeds a tuned threshold. We show that this standard rule carries a hidden *distributional* cost. In a controlled simulation with a real logistic-regression classifier and a simulated human, we induce the canonical socio-technical bias source — a minority group under-represented and subject to concept drift at training time — and find that the AI becomes badly *miscalibrated across groups*: at the accuracy-maximizing confidence threshold, the model is 91% accurate on the majority group but only 25% accurate on the minority group, despite emitting nearly identical average confidence (0.84 vs. 0.85). A global confidence threshold improves team accuracy (0.71→0.77) but leaves a large between-group error gap (0.36). A simple fairness-aware variant — group-conditional thresholds — simultaneously raises accuracy to 0.82 *and* shrinks the gap to 0.09. We trace the accuracy/fairness frontier and vary human skill to show the effect is robust. The finding reframes appropriate reliance as an inherently socio-technical, per-group calibration problem rather than a single scalar-threshold problem.

## 1. Introduction

As AI systems are embedded in consequential decisions — hiring, lending, clinical triage — the object of study is increasingly the *human-AI team*, not the model alone. Two research communities speak to this. The **appropriate-reliance / trust-calibration** community studies when a human should defer to an AI, aiming to avoid *over-reliance* (accepting wrong advice) and *under-reliance* (rejecting right advice) [Vasconcelos et al.; PLOS One 2020]. Its workhorse intervention is confidence-conditioned reliance: show or act on AI advice when the model's confidence clears a threshold. The **sociotechnical fairness / algorithm-auditing** community studies how bias enters through data collection, representation, and organizational deployment, and insists that fairness is a property of the *system*, not a statistic of the model [Dolata et al. 2022; Emerald JICES 2026].

These conversations rarely meet. Reliance policies are typically evaluated by aggregate team accuracy; audits typically examine the model's standalone predictions. We ask a question at their intersection: **what does a confidence-based reliance rule do to the distribution of errors across social groups?**

Our answer is a cautionary one. Model confidence is only useful for reliance if it is *comparable* — if "0.85 confident" means the same reliability everywhere. Under the standard representation-harm mechanism, it does not. We show a global threshold silently concentrates residual error on the group it already fails, and that making the threshold *group-conditional* — a minimal, auditable change — dominates it on both accuracy and fairness. Our contribution is (i) a compact, reproducible simulation isolating the mechanism; (ii) a demonstration that the standard reliance rule has a distributional cost invisible to aggregate metrics; and (iii) a simple fairness-aware reliance rule as a baseline for future work.

### Related Work

Our survey situates the paper across three strands. **Trust calibration and appropriate reliance:** measurement constructs distinguish a user's psychological *trust* from system *trustworthiness*, and define appropriate reliance as tracking actual capability under operating conditions [arXiv:2604.23896]; adaptive schemes present cues on detecting over/under-trust [PLOS One 2020, PMC8181412]; transparency of uncertainty aids calibration [arXiv:2605.18036]. Nearly all operationalize reliance through a confidence signal. **Learning-to-defer / selective prediction:** complementary routing between model and human is framed as an accuracy-optimization problem, seldom auditing *whose* errors remain. **Sociotechnical fairness and auditing:** bias is treated not as statistical error but as procedural and constitutive of the system [Dolata 2022; ScienceDirect S266709682400048X], with calls to embed socio-technical interventions and holistic audits of how predictions drive final actions [Royal Society Open Science 2024]. Miscalibration across groups is documented for standalone classifiers; our novelty is showing it propagates *through the reliance policy* into team-level distributional harm, and that per-group thresholds are a remedy.

## 2. Method

**Population and concept drift.** Each instance has 6 continuous features and a binary sensitive attribute $g\in\{A,B\}$ with a 20% minority group $B$. Labels follow a logistic model whose weight vector *differs by group* ($w_A \ne w_B$, partial concept drift). Group membership is deliberately **not** a feature available to the model, mirroring real settings where the protected attribute is unobserved or legally excluded.

**The AI.** A logistic-regression classifier trained on 4,000 instances, but with 60% of minority-group training rows dropped — the representation-harm mechanism (under-collection of minority data). The model learns the majority rule $w_A$ and applies it everywhere, producing predictions and a confidence $c=\max_k p_k$.

**The human.** A simulated decision maker with group-*independent* accuracy (default 0.75); errors are drawn independently of the AI's, so the human is genuinely complementary. This is a deliberately optimistic, controllable stand-in for a person — it lets us isolate the reliance policy's effect rather than model human psychology.

**Reliance policies (baselines + ours).**
1. **AI-only** — always take AI prediction.
2. **Human-only** — never consult AI.
3. **Global threshold** (standard appropriate reliance) — take the AI iff $c \ge \tau^\*$, where $\tau^\*$ is tuned on a held-out validation set to maximize team accuracy.
4. **Group-conditional thresholds** (ours) — a separate $\tau_g$ per group, each tuned to maximize that group's team accuracy.

**Evaluation.** On a 6,000-instance test set we report team accuracy and the **between-group error gap** $|\,\mathrm{err}_A-\mathrm{err}_B\,|$. We add two ablations: (A) a sweep of the global threshold to trace the accuracy/fairness frontier and the fraction of cases routed to the AI; (B) a sweep of human accuracy (0.60–0.90) to test robustness. A diagnostic measures the AI's *actual* accuracy on cases it is confident about, per group. Everything is CPU-only, runs in ~2s, uses fixed seeds, and is in `reliance_fairness.py` (raw log in `run_output.txt`).

## 3. Results

**The AI is miscalibrated across groups (the mechanism).** On test data the standalone AI is 80% accurate on group A but only 34% on group B (error 0.20 vs 0.66). Critically, its *confidence does not signal this*: among cases where $c \ge \tau^\*=0.72$, mean confidence is essentially equal across groups (A: 0.844, B: 0.848), yet the AI is right 91% of the time on A and only **25%** on B. High confidence is a reliable "trust me" signal for the majority and an actively *misleading* one for the minority.

**The standard reliance rule improves accuracy but is distributionally unjust.**

| Policy | Team acc | err A | err B | Error gap |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. AI-only | 0.710 | 0.201 | 0.656 | 0.455 |
| 2. Human-only | 0.751 | 0.247 | 0.253 | 0.005 |
| 3. Global threshold (τ\*=0.72) | 0.768 | 0.163 | 0.519 | 0.356 |
| 4. Group-conditional (ours) | **0.819** | 0.163 | 0.254 | **0.092** |

The global threshold lifts accuracy over AI-only (0.71→0.77) but *keeps a 0.356 error gap* — it happily routes confident-but-wrong minority cases to the AI. Group-conditional thresholds instead learn $\tau_A=0.72,\ \tau_B=0.99$: because minority confidence is uninformative, the rule defers almost all minority cases to the more reliable human. It **dominates** the global rule — higher accuracy (0.819) *and* a far smaller gap (0.092) — a Pareto improvement, not a trade-off.

**Ablation A — the frontier (global threshold).** Sweeping $\tau$ shows the global rule *can* reduce the gap only by cranking $\tau$ up to 0.95, at which point it routes just 5% of cases to the AI (acc 0.752, gap 0.060) — throwing away the AI's value on the majority to protect the minority. There is no single global threshold that is both accurate and fair.

| τ | acc | gap | % to AI |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.50 | 0.710 | 0.455 | 100% |
| 0.70 | 0.766 | 0.373 | 57% |
| 0.80 | 0.767 | 0.279 | 35% |
| 0.90 | 0.758 | 0.131 | 14% |
| 0.95 | 0.752 | 0.060 | 5% |

**Ablation B — robustness to human skill.** Across human accuracy 0.60–0.90, group-conditional thresholds match or beat the global rule on accuracy and cut the gap substantially whenever the human is competitive with the AI (e.g. h=0.70: gap 0.342→0.126; h=0.60: 0.447→0.188). When the human is very strong (0.90) both policies defer heavily and the gap closes on its own; the group-conditional advantage is largest in the realistic regime where human and AI are comparably skilled.

## 4. Conclusion

Confidence-based reliance — the default operationalization of appropriate reliance — implicitly assumes model confidence is *comparable across people*. Under the ordinary socio-technical bias mechanism of minority under-representation with concept drift, that assumption fails: identical confidence hides an enormous reliability gap, and a globally accuracy-optimal threshold concentrates error on the already-disadvantaged group. A minimal, auditable fix — group-conditional reliance thresholds — recovers a Pareto improvement in our simulation. The broader lesson for socio-technical practice is that reliance policies must be audited *per group*, and that team-level "appropriate reliance" cannot be certified by aggregate accuracy alone.

**Limitations.** The human is a simplified, group-uniform simulation, not a real participant, and real humans may themselves be biased or influenced by AI advice (automation bias); results are illustrative of a mechanism, not effect-size estimates. Group-conditional thresholds require observing $g$ at decision time, which is often legally or practically constrained — motivating future work on reliance rules that restore calibration comparability without the protected attribute (e.g., proxy-based or calibration-corrected confidence). Validation on real audited datasets with human-subject reliance data is the natural next step.
