# Confidence Is Not Comparable Across Groups: The Distributional Cost of Confidence-Based Reliance in Human-AI Teams

## Abstract

Appropriate-reliance research seeks decision rules that let a human accept correct AI advice and reject incorrect advice, most commonly by routing a case to the AI when its confidence exceeds a tuned threshold. We show that this standard rule carries a hidden *distributional* cost. In a controlled simulation with a real logistic-regression classifier and a simulated human, we induce the canonical socio-technical bias source — a minority group under-represented and subject to concept drift at training time — and find the AI becomes badly *miscalibrated across groups*: at the accuracy-maximizing threshold it emits nearly identical mean confidence on the two groups (0.844 vs. 0.848) yet is right 91% of the time on the majority and only 25% on the minority. Across **30 random seeds**, a global confidence threshold improves team accuracy (0.771±0.002) but leaves a large between-group error gap (0.279±0.019); a fairness-aware variant — group-conditional thresholds, i.e. equalized-odds-style post-processing — simultaneously raises accuracy (0.820±0.002) and shrinks the gap (0.087±0.005), winning on *both* axes in 30/30 seeds (paired gap reduction 0.192±0.017, Cohen's *d*=4.1, paired *t* p=4×10⁻¹⁷). The magnitude is a tunable function of concept-drift severity, so we report the *direction and mechanism*, not an effect size; we further show the fix degrades gracefully under a noisy (even group-dependent) protected-attribute proxy, and that its advantage shrinks — but does not reverse — as the human becomes automation-biased. The finding reframes appropriate reliance as an inherently socio-technical, per-group calibration problem rather than a single scalar-threshold problem.

## 1. Introduction

As AI systems are embedded in consequential decisions — hiring, lending, clinical triage — the object of study is increasingly the *human-AI team*, not the model alone. Two research communities speak to this. The **appropriate-reliance / trust-calibration** community studies when a human should defer to an AI, aiming to avoid *over-reliance* (accepting wrong advice) and *under-reliance* (rejecting right advice) [Vasconcelos et al.; PLOS One 2020]. Its workhorse intervention is confidence-conditioned reliance: act on AI advice when the model's confidence clears a threshold. The **sociotechnical fairness / algorithm-auditing** community studies how bias enters through data collection, representation, and organizational deployment, and insists fairness is a property of the *system*, not a statistic of the model [Dolata et al. 2022; Emerald JICES 2026].

These conversations rarely meet. Reliance policies are typically evaluated by aggregate team accuracy; audits typically examine the model's standalone predictions. We ask a question at their intersection: **what does a confidence-based reliance rule do to the distribution of errors across social groups?**

Model confidence is only useful for reliance if it is *comparable* — if "0.85 confident" means the same reliability everywhere. Under the standard representation-harm mechanism, it does not. We show a global threshold silently concentrates residual error on the group it already fails, and that making the threshold *group-conditional* dominates it on both accuracy and fairness. Our contribution is (i) a compact, reproducible simulation isolating the mechanism; (ii) a demonstration, with multi-seed variance and formal significance, that the standard reliance rule has a distributional cost invisible to aggregate metrics; and (iii) a characterization of *when* a simple fairness-aware reliance rule helps — how its advantage scales with drift, survives a noisy or group-dependent proxy, and erodes under human automation bias.

### Related Work

**Trust calibration and appropriate reliance:** measurement constructs distinguish a user's psychological *trust* from system *trustworthiness* and define appropriate reliance as tracking actual capability [arXiv:2604.23896]; adaptive schemes present cues on detecting over/under-trust [PLOS One 2020, PMC8181412]; uncertainty transparency aids calibration [arXiv:2605.18036]. Nearly all operationalize reliance through a scalar confidence signal. **Learning-to-defer / selective prediction** frames model–human routing as accuracy optimization, seldom auditing *whose* errors remain. **Fairness post-processing:** group-conditional thresholds are the classic remedy for group-dependent error rates (Hardt, Price & Srebro, 2016, equalized odds) [NeurIPS 2016]; our group-conditional policy is exactly this method, applied not to the classifier but to the *reliance decision*. Our novelty is therefore not the mechanism of the fix but the demonstration that miscalibration propagates *through the reliance policy* into team-level distributional harm, and that the standard confidence-threshold rule is the vector. **Sociotechnical auditing** treats bias as procedural and constitutive of the system [Dolata 2022; ScienceDirect S266709682400048X], with calls for holistic audits of how predictions drive final actions [Royal Society Open Science 2024].

## 2. Method

**Population and concept drift.** Each instance has 6 continuous features and a binary sensitive attribute $g\in\{A,B\}$ with 20% minority $B$. Labels follow a logistic model whose weight vector *differs by group* ($w_A \ne w_B$, partial concept drift). Group membership is deliberately **not** a model feature, mirroring settings where the protected attribute is unobserved or legally excluded. To test whether the effect is a free knob, we interpolate $w_B(\alpha)=(1-\alpha)w_A+\alpha w_B^0$ and sweep $\alpha\in[0,1]$ (no drift → full drift).

**The AI.** Logistic regression trained on 4,000 instances with 60% of minority training rows dropped — the representation-harm mechanism. The model learns the majority rule and applies it everywhere, emitting a confidence $c=\max_k p_k$.

**The human.** A simulated decision maker with group-*independent* accuracy (default 0.75); errors are drawn independently of the AI's, so the human is genuinely complementary. This is a deliberately optimistic, controllable stand-in; Ablation E relaxes it to a correlated (automation-biased) human.

**Reliance policies.** (1) **AI-only**; (2) **Human-only**; (3) **Global threshold** (standard) — take the AI iff $c\ge\tau^\*$, $\tau^\*$ tuned on validation to maximize team accuracy; (4) **Group-conditional** (ours, = equalized-odds post-processing) — a separate $\tau_g$ per group, each tuned to maximize that group's team accuracy. Policy 4 uses strictly *more* information than policy 3 (the sensitive attribute at decision time); "dominates" below is therefore a value-of-information result, and we quantify how much of that value survives when $g$ is noisy.

**Evaluation.** On a 6,000-instance test set we report team accuracy and the **between-group error gap** $|\mathrm{err}_A-\mathrm{err}_B|$. We run the full pipeline over **30 seeds** (independent data *and* human draws), reporting means with 95% CIs, paired difference tests (Cohen's *d*, paired *t*, Wilcoxon), and a per-seed win count. Ablations: (A) global-threshold frontier; (B) human-skill sweep; (C) **drift sensitivity** over $\alpha$; (D) **noisy proxy**, symmetric and group-dependent; (E) **automation-biased human**. CPU-only, seconds, fixed seeds (`reliance_fairness.py`, `extra_experiments.py`, `round2_experiments.py`).

## 3. Results

**The AI is miscalibrated across groups (the mechanism).** On the reference seed the standalone AI is 80% accurate on group A but 34% on B. Its *confidence does not signal this*: among cases with $c\ge\tau^\*$, mean confidence is essentially equal (A 0.844, B 0.848), yet the AI is right 91% on A and only **25%** on B. High confidence is a reliable "trust me" for the majority and an actively *misleading* one for the minority.

**The standard rule improves accuracy but is distributionally unjust — and this replicates across seeds.** Over 30 seeds:

| Policy | Team acc (95% CI) | Error gap (95% CI) |
|---|---|---|
| Global threshold (standard) | 0.771 ± 0.002 | 0.279 ± 0.019 |
| Group-conditional (ours) | **0.820 ± 0.002** | **0.087 ± 0.005** |

The gap reduction is large and reliable, not a single-draw artifact: paired reduction 0.192 ± 0.017 (Cohen's *d* = 4.1; paired *t* p = 4×10⁻¹⁷, Wilcoxon p = 2×10⁻⁹), accuracy gain 0.049 ± 0.002 (*d* = 11.4, p = 5×10⁻³¹), and group-conditional wins on **both** accuracy and gap in **30/30** seeds. It learns $\tau_A\approx0.72,\ \tau_B\approx0.99$: because minority confidence is uninformative, it defers almost all minority cases to the more reliable human. This is a Pareto improvement *given* access to $g$, not a trade-off.

**Ablation A — the frontier.** Sweeping the global $\tau$ shows it can shrink the gap only by cranking $\tau$ to 0.95, at which point it routes just 5% of cases to the AI (acc 0.752, gap 0.060) — discarding the AI's majority value to protect the minority. No single global threshold is both accurate and fair.

**Ablation C — the effect is not a free knob; it tracks drift.** Sweeping $\alpha$ (10 seeds each), every gap is a monotone function of how orthogonal the group rules are:

| α | cos(wA,wB) | AI acc on B | global gap | grp-cond gap | Δ(advantage) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.00 | 1.00 | 0.774 | 0.011 | 0.013 | −0.002 |
| 0.25 | 0.96 | 0.689 | 0.059 | 0.053 | 0.006 |
| 0.50 | 0.48 | 0.559 | 0.158 | 0.088 | 0.070 |
| 0.75 | −0.35 | 0.427 | 0.228 | 0.081 | 0.147 |
| 1.00 | −0.64 | 0.349 | 0.275 | 0.081 | 0.194 |

This answers the concern that the headline number is engineered: it *is* set by drift severity, so we report the mechanism, not a magnitude. At mild drift ($\alpha\le0.25$) the gap is negligible and the two policies are indistinguishable; the below-chance AI on B and the large gap arise only near full orthogonality. Crucially, the group-conditional advantage grows *monotonically* with drift while its own gap stays bounded (~0.08) — the fix is robust across the whole regime, and the harm it prevents is worst exactly where drift is severe.

**Ablation D — the fix survives a noisy, even group-dependent, proxy.** Group-conditional reliance needs $g$ at decision time. Using a proxy $\tilde g$ that flips $g$ with probability $\varepsilon$ (30 seeds), tuned and applied on $\tilde g$ but *evaluated on true $g$*: at symmetric $\varepsilon$=0.10 acc=0.808/gap=0.108, and even at $\varepsilon$=0.30 it beats the global baseline on both (acc 0.783 > 0.771; gap 0.152 < 0.279). The more realistic failure mode is a proxy that is *worse for the minority*. Fixing majority noise at 0.05 and raising minority noise $\varepsilon_B$:

| ε_A | ε_B | team acc | error gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.05 | 0.05 | 0.814 | 0.100 |
| 0.05 | 0.20 | 0.806 | 0.135 |
| 0.05 | 0.40 | 0.796 | 0.184 |

Even when the minority attribute is flipped 40% of the time, the rule still beats the global baseline on both metrics (acc 0.796 > 0.771; gap 0.184 < 0.279). The value degrades gracefully rather than collapsing: an imperfect audited proxy — not a perfect legal disclosure — suffices to recover most of the benefit.

**Ablation E — automation bias softens but does not reverse the advantage.** The clean result assumes human errors are independent of the AI. Real automation bias correlates them: on high-confidence AI cases ($c\ge0.8$) the human *anchors* to the AI's answer with probability $\beta$, so confident-but-wrong minority advice can drag the human down — attacking the "reliable fallback" the fix relies on. Sweeping $\beta$ (30 seeds):

| β | global gap | grp-cond acc | grp-cond gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.00 | 0.276 | 0.820 | 0.091 |
| 0.50 | 0.289 | 0.801 | 0.185 |
| 1.00 | 0.298 | 0.784 | 0.273 |

As anticipated, the fix weakens: at full anchoring its gap climbs from 0.09 to 0.27. But it never *reverses* — at every $\beta$ the group-conditional rule still beats the global rule on both accuracy (0.784 vs 0.771 even at $\beta$=1) and gap (0.273 vs 0.298). Automation bias raises the value of *not* routing miscalibrated minority cases to a human who will rubber-stamp them, motivating reliance rules that also protect the human from confident-but-wrong minority advice. (Ablation B: across human accuracy 0.60–0.90 the group-conditional rule matches or beats the global rule on accuracy and cuts the gap; its advantage is largest where human and AI are comparably skilled.)

## 4. Conclusion

Confidence-based reliance implicitly assumes model confidence is *comparable across people*. Under the ordinary socio-technical bias mechanism of minority under-representation with concept drift, that assumption fails: identical confidence hides a large reliability gap, and a globally accuracy-optimal threshold concentrates error on the already-disadvantaged group — a pattern that replicates across 30 seeds with tight CIs and formal significance. Group-conditional reliance — the classic equalized-odds post-processing rule, applied to the *reliance decision* — recovers a Pareto improvement given the protected attribute, retains most of its benefit under a noisy or group-dependent proxy, and still wins (if by less) under an automation-biased human. The broader lesson: reliance policies must be audited *per group*; team-level "appropriate reliance" cannot be certified by aggregate accuracy alone.

**Limitations.** The human is a simplified simulation; Ablation E adds one automation-bias model but not real behavioral data, so results illustrate a mechanism, not field effect sizes. The gap magnitude is a function of the imposed drift, not an estimate of any real-world quantity (Ablation C). The remedy is not novel as a technique (Hardt et al. 2016) and requires observing $g$, at least as a proxy, at decision time — motivating future work on reliance rules that restore calibration comparability without the protected attribute (e.g. calibration-corrected confidence). Validation on real audited datasets with human-subject reliance data is the natural next step.
