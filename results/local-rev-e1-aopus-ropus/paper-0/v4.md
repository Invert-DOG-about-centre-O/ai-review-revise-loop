# Exploration Is Not Enough: Why Selective-Labels Feedback Loops Entrench Disparity, and When Coverage Constraints Repair Them

## Abstract

When a deployed predictive model decides who gets a scarce resource (loans, interviews, bail), it observes outcomes *only* for the applicants it accepts. Retraining on this self-selected data — the **selective-labels** regime — is a canonical socio-technical failure mode: the model's own decisions shape the data it learns from. We study, in a controlled simulation, whether such loops amplify disparity between two groups that are *equally qualified in truth* but differ in a group-correlated feature offset (proxy discrimination). We compare deployment policies under identical dynamics: a no-retrain **static** control, **greedy** retraining, **ε-greedy exploration**, **group-targeted exploration**, and two **coverage constraints** (demographic-parity and equal-opportunity). Central finding: greedy retraining partially self-corrects a biased seed but *plateaus* at a persistent approval gap. Uniform exploration does not close it (and worsens utility); targeting exploration at the under-covered group narrows the gap but on an intrinsic fairness–utility frontier — an ε and budget sweep shows *no* exploration setting reaches parity's utility, because the harm is structural range-restriction in the feature–outcome mapping, not label scarcity. Only a policy that *guarantees representative coverage* drives the gap to zero, at regret statistically indistinguishable from greedy; an equal-opportunity variant additionally closes the residual TPR gap for free. **New in this revision:** the full ranking survives a *non-linear* (gradient-boosted-tree) estimator — where exploration overshoots even more badly; a *deployable* equal-opportunity rule using predicted (not oracle) labels recovers most of the closure; and the parity–greedy utility tie is re-examined across budgets. We release the simulator, sweeps, and logs.

## 1. Introduction

A recurring lesson from the socio-technical literature is that AI harms cannot be diagnosed from the model in isolation: they emerge from the coupling of a model to the human and institutional context that supplies its data and consumes its decisions [4, 5]. Selective labels are a sharp instance. A lender only learns whether *accepted* applicants repay; rejected applicants are censored [7]. Retraining on accepted-only data closes a loop — decisions determine the training distribution, which determines the next decisions — and this loop can silently entrench inequity even when no protected attribute is used maliciously and the groups are, in fact, equally creditworthy.

Two intuitions suggest easy fixes, both worth testing. From **bandits/active learning**, the disparity looks like an *exploration* problem: the model under-samples a group, so inject randomness [2]. From **fair ML**, it looks like a *constraint* problem: enforce representative coverage. These prescribe different interventions with different costs, and it is not obvious which — if either — repairs a feedback loop, or at what utility price. We build a minimal, fast, fully-observable testbed to compare them, and to isolate *the loop itself* (via a no-retrain control) from the initial data bias.

**Contributions.** (i) A compact, reproducible simulation of a selective-labels retraining loop with proxy discrimination, in which two groups are equally qualified by construction, so any gap is an artifact of the dynamics. (ii) Evidence that exploration is the wrong lever: *uniform* exploration is insufficient and utility-harmful, and even *group-targeted* exploration traces an intrinsic fairness–utility frontier dominated at every ε and budget by a coverage constraint — and this dominance *widens* under a non-linear estimator, where exploration overshoots. (iii) Evidence that a coverage constraint eliminates the gap at regret indistinguishable from greedy, that an equal-opportunity target additionally closes the residual TPR gap, and that a *deployable* (non-oracle) equal-opportunity rule recovers most of that closure.

### Related Work

Our study sits in the line on **simulated fairness feedback loops**. Ensign et al. [8] show predictive-policing loops self-reinforce when observations condition on prior deployment; D'Amour et al. [9] (ML-fairness-gym) argue static fairness metrics mislead and must be evaluated over induced dynamics; Liu et al. [10] show a one-step "fair" decision can still degrade a group's welfare through delayed impact. Relative to that body — which mostly studies whether a *fairness criterion* helps or harms over time — our contribution is a controlled *policy comparison at fixed estimator*: we vary only the deployment-time decision rule, so the loop, not the loss function, is the isolated variable. This lets us make the mechanistic claim, untested head-to-head in prior simulations, that exploration and coverage are *not* interchangeable remedies, and to quantify the gap as a frontier. We complement the framing that safety and fairness are properties of model-plus-context [4, 5], that fairness metrics can lack construct validity [6], and that reliance/override interact with perceived fairness [1, 3].

## 2. Method

**Data-generating process.** Each round a cohort of \(n{=}400\) applicants arrives. Group \(g\in\{A,B\}\) is uniform. Latent qualification \(q\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\) is drawn *identically* for both groups, and true repayment is \(\Pr(\text{repay})=\sigma(1.2q)\) — so **A and B are equally creditworthy**. The model never sees \(q\); it sees four noisy proxy features with a **group-correlated offset**, \(x_d = q + \text{off}(g) + \varepsilon_d\), with \(\text{off}(A){=}{+}1.3\), \(\text{off}(B){=}{-}1.3\) (we also test \(2.0\)). For the *same* qualification, B's observables look worse — proxy discrimination / redlining. The group indicator *is* a feature, so with enough per-group outcome data the model can correct the offset; it can only do so for groups it approves.

**Loop.** An estimator is trained on all outcomes observed so far. It scores the cohort; a budget of \(k=0.5n\) applicants is approved; **outcomes are revealed only for approved applicants** and appended. Our default estimator is an L2-regularized logistic model (hand-coded); the revision re-runs the comparison with a **gradient-boosted-tree** estimator (50 trees, depth 3). We seed the loop with a *biased* history under-representing B (B appears w.p. 0.15), modeling inherited institutional bias. We run 25 rounds, average over 8 seeds, report per-seed SEs.

**Policies (identical everywhere else).** **static** — train once on the seed, never retrain (isolates seed bias). **greedy** — approve top-\(k\) by predicted repay. **explore** — ε-greedy: \(\varepsilon\) of budget spent on *uniformly* random approvals. **explore-targeted** — same budget, spent *only on group B* (informed exploration at the under-covered group). **parity** — group-conditional thresholds equalizing *approval rate*. **eq-opp** — coverage equalizing per-group *true-positive rate*; the oracle variant sets the split from realized cohort labels, the **deployable** variant from the model's *predicted* repay probabilities (no future labels).

**Metrics.** *Approval-rate gap* \(=\Pr(\text{appr}\mid A)-\Pr(\text{appr}\mid B)\); *TPR gap* = per-group difference in fraction of *truly-qualified* applicants approved; *regret* = % utility shortfall vs. a clairvoyant oracle approving the \(k\) highest true-repay-prob applicants (utility = repayers \(-0.6\times\) defaulters). Mean \(\pm\) SE over 8 seeds; paired tests on key utility comparisons.

## 3. Results

Averaged over 8 seeds, 25 rounds (mean \(\pm\) SE; `results.json`, `sweep.json`, `rev.json`):

| Policy | Mean appr. gap | Final TPR gap | Regret % |
|---|---|---|---|
| static (no retrain) | 0.349 ± 0.057 | 0.352 | 15.72 ± 1.83 |
| greedy | 0.128 ± 0.014 | 0.125 | 8.79 ± 0.63 |
| explore (ε=.15, uniform) | 0.132 ± 0.014 | 0.147 | 17.21 ± 0.37 |
| explore-targeted (B only) | 0.038 ± 0.013 | 0.096 | 16.69 ± 0.61 |
| **parity** | **−0.000 ± 0.000** | 0.027 ± 0.015 | **8.30 ± 0.59** |
| **eq-opp** (oracle) | **−0.003 ± 0.001** | **−0.001 ± 0.001** | **8.00 ± 0.63** |

**The loop leaves an entrenched, not amplified, gap.** The greedy approval-gap trajectory is \([0.37, 0.10, 0.12, 0.11, 0.10]\) over rounds \([0,4,9,14,24]\): it starts at seed-bias level, relaxes, then **plateaus near 0.10**. Retraining helps versus the frozen model (static 0.35 gap, 15.7% regret) but cannot escape the loop: qualified B applicants are scored low by the offset, so B's observed outcomes come from a range-restricted, self-selected slice that keeps the learned offset-correction too weak.

**Exploration does not repair it.** Uniform ε-greedy leaves the gap unchanged (0.132) while nearly *doubling* regret (17.2% vs 8.8%): random exploration collects a little more B data of the same restricted kind, not *representative* coverage. Group-targeted exploration works partially but sweeping ε shows the tradeoff is intrinsic:

| targeted ε | Mean appr. gap | Regret % |
|---|---|---|
| 0.05 | 0.090 ± 0.015 | 11.60 ± 0.34 |
| 0.10 | 0.062 ± 0.012 | 13.50 ± 0.59 |
| 0.15 | 0.038 ± 0.013 | 16.69 ± 0.61 |
| 0.30 | −0.106 ± 0.006 | 22.16 ± 0.41 |

Regret climbs monotonically; the cheapest near-equity setting (ε=0.15) still costs ~16.7%, and pushing ε high enough to *close* the gap *overshoots* it (ε=0.30 → −0.106) at 22% regret. Parity attains gap 0.000 at 8.3% — a point **no ε reaches**. Across budgets \(\in\{0.3,0.5,0.7\}\), parity's (regret, gap) is (9.9,0.00)/(8.3,0.00)/(5.2,0.00) versus targeted's (18.3,0.08)/(16.7,0.04)/(12.8,−0.03): **parity dominates targeted exploration at every ε and budget we tested** — reallocating the budget beats randomizing part of it, everywhere on the grid.

**Coverage eliminates the gap at no utility penalty.** Equalizing approval *rates* forces a representative B slice each round, closing the approval gap to 0.000 and TPR gap to 0.027. Its regret (8.30) is comparable to greedy's (8.79): paired greedy−parity = 0.49 ± 0.34, reversing in 2/8 seeds, \(t{=}1.43\) (\(p{\approx}0.20\)) — statistically indistinguishable. Switching the coverage *target* to equal TPR (**eq-opp**) drives the TPR gap to −0.001 ± 0.001 at regret 8.00 — if anything marginally cheaper.

**(Q3) Is the parity–greedy tie stable across budgets?** The tie holds at budget 0.3 (diff 0.86 ± 0.51, \(t{=}1.69\), \(p{\approx}0.14\)) and 0.5, but at budget 0.7 parity is *significantly cheaper* than greedy (diff 0.49 ± 0.18, \(t{=}2.74\), \(p{\approx}0.03\), 2/8 reversals). So the utility cost of guaranteeing parity is never positive in our grid, and at high budget it is significantly *negative* — the "indistinguishable" headline is if anything conservative. We still make no general claim that parity beats greedy.

**(Q2) A deployable equal-opportunity rule.** The eq-opp result above is an oracle: the per-group split is chosen from the cohort's realized repay labels, unavailable at decision time. Replacing them with the model's *predicted* repay probabilities yields a fully deployable rule. It closes the TPR gap to 0.034 ± 0.014 at regret 8.07 ± 0.56 — versus greedy's 0.125 and the oracle's −0.001. So ~70% of the "for-free" closure survives with no oracle information and no extra regret; the residual 0.034 (≈ parity's 0.027) is the price of estimating TPR from a biased scorer rather than knowing it, and quantifies the oracle-vs-deployable gap the previous version only flagged.

**(Q1) Rankings survive a non-linear estimator.** Re-running with gradient-boosted trees, which can correct the offset more flexibly:

| GBT policy | Mean appr. gap | Final TPR gap | Regret % |
|---|---|---|---|
| greedy | 0.043 ± 0.015 | 0.061 | 13.36 ± 0.50 |
| explore-targeted | −0.176 ± 0.012 | −0.148 | 20.11 ± 0.23 |
| parity | −0.000 ± 0.000 | 0.012 | 13.26 ± 0.46 |
| eq-opp (oracle) | −0.002 ± 0.002 | 0.002 | 13.18 ± 0.38 |

The full ordering holds. The flexible estimator shrinks greedy's *entrenched* gap (0.043 vs logistic 0.128) — it corrects the offset better given data — but does **not** eliminate it, and coverage still closes it at regret indistinguishable from greedy (paired greedy−parity = 0.10 ± 0.20, \(t{=}0.50\), 4/8 reversals). Exploration fares *worse* here: targeted ε=0.15 now badly *overshoots* (gap −0.176, TPR gap −0.148) at the highest regret of any policy (20.1%). This is exactly what the structural-distortion account predicts: a more expressive model amplifies each explored B point, so the same randomness swings past equity, while a coverage constraint lands on it by construction. **Rankings are also robust to a larger offset (2.0):** greedy gap 0.154, targeted only 0.144 at 18.4% regret, parity 0.000 at 8.4%, eq-opp TPR gap −0.002.

## 4. Conclusion

In a controlled selective-labels loop where two groups are equally qualified by construction, greedy retraining entrenches a persistent disparity. Exploration is the wrong lever: uniform exploration fails outright, and group-targeted exploration trades fairness against utility along a frontier whose best corner is strictly dominated — at every ε and budget, and *more* so under a non-linear estimator, where it overshoots. A coverage constraint eliminates the approval gap at regret indistinguishable from greedy (and, at high budget, significantly cheaper); an equal-opportunity target additionally closes the residual TPR gap, most of it recoverable by a deployable non-oracle rule. The socio-technical takeaway: when a deployed model manufactures its own training data, the *decision policy* — specifically whether it guarantees *representative* coverage of disadvantaged groups, not merely more randomness — determines whether inequity becomes self-fulfilling.

**Limitations.** This is a stylized simulation (two groups, Gaussian latents, 8 seeds). We now test two estimator families (logistic, boosted trees), three budgets, an ε sweep, a larger offset, and a deployable eq-opp variant; richer multi-group structure or a live human decision-maker could still shift magnitudes. The novelty is an angle on an already-studied phenomenon [8,9,10] — a fixed-estimator, exploration-vs-coverage decomposition — not a new mechanism. All experiments (`sim.py`, `sweep.py`, `rev.py`, `analysis.py`) run in minutes on CPU.
