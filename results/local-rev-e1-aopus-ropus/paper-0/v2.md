# Exploration Is Not Enough: Why Selective-Labels Feedback Loops Entrench Disparity, and When Coverage Constraints Repair Them

## Abstract

When a deployed predictive model decides who gets a scarce resource (loans, interviews, bail), it observes outcomes *only* for the applicants it accepts. Retraining on this self-selected data — the **selective-labels** regime — is a canonical socio-technical failure mode: the model's own decisions shape the data it learns from. We study, in a controlled simulation, whether such loops amplify disparity between two groups that are *equally qualified in truth* but differ in a group-correlated feature offset (proxy discrimination). We compare five deployment policies under identical dynamics: a no-retrain **static** control, **greedy** retraining, **ε-greedy exploration**, **group-targeted exploration**, and a **demographic-parity coverage** constraint. Our central finding: greedy retraining partially self-corrects a biased seed but *plateaus* at a persistent ~13-point approval gap. Uniform exploration does not close it (and worsens utility); targeting exploration at the under-covered group halves the gap but still costs ~8 extra points of regret and stops short of equity — because the harm is structural range-restriction in the feature–outcome mapping. Only a policy that *guarantees representative coverage* (parity) drives the gap to zero, at regret statistically indistinguishable from greedy. We report per-seed variance and a paired significance test, and release the ~150-line simulator and logs.

## 1. Introduction

A recurring lesson from the socio-technical literature is that AI harms cannot be diagnosed from the model in isolation: they emerge from the coupling of a model to the human and institutional context that supplies its data and consumes its decisions [4, 5]. Selective labels are a sharp instance. A lender only learns whether *accepted* applicants repay; rejected applicants are censored [7]. Retraining on accepted-only data closes a loop — decisions determine the training distribution, which determines the next decisions — and this loop can silently entrench inequity even when no protected attribute is used maliciously and the groups are, in fact, equally creditworthy.

Two intuitions suggest easy fixes, both worth testing. From **bandits/active learning**, the disparity looks like an *exploration* problem: the model under-samples a group, so inject randomness to gather its labels [2]. From **fair ML**, it looks like a *constraint* problem: enforce demographic parity. These prescribe different interventions with different costs, and it is not obvious which — if either — repairs a feedback loop, or at what utility price. We build a minimal, fast, fully-observable testbed to compare them, and to isolate *the loop itself* (via a no-retrain control) from the initial data bias.

**Contributions.** (i) A compact, reproducible simulation of a selective-labels retraining loop with proxy discrimination, in which two groups are equally qualified by construction, so any gap is an artifact of the dynamics. (ii) Evidence that exploration is the wrong lever: *uniform* exploration is insufficient and utility-harmful, and even *group-targeted* exploration — the natural stronger baseline — only halves the gap at persistent utility cost, because the bias is a structural feature-mapping distortion, not pure label scarcity. (iii) Evidence that a coverage (parity) constraint eliminates the gap at regret indistinguishable from greedy, reported with per-seed CIs and a paired test.

### Related Work

Our study sits squarely in the line on **simulated fairness feedback loops**. Ensign et al. [8] show predictive-policing loops become self-reinforcing when observations are conditioned on prior deployment; D'Amour et al. [9] (ML-fairness-gym) argue that static, single-shot fairness metrics can be misleading and that fairness must be evaluated over the induced long-run dynamics; Liu et al. [10] show that a one-step "fair" decision can still degrade a group's welfare through delayed impact. Relative to that body of work — which mostly studies whether a *fairness criterion* helps or harms over time — our contribution is a controlled *policy comparison at fixed estimator*: we hold the logistic estimator and dynamics constant and vary only the deployment-time decision rule (static / greedy / two exploration variants / parity), so the loop, not the loss function, is the isolated variable. This lets us make the specific mechanistic claim that exploration and coverage are *not* interchangeable remedies, which the prior simulations do not test head-to-head.

We complement the HCI/sociotechnical framing that safety and fairness are properties of model-plus-context, not the model alone [4, 5], and that fairness metrics can lack construct validity [6]; human-AI studies show reliance and override interact with perceived fairness [1, 3]. Where much fair-ML work fixes the *estimator* on a static dataset, we hold the estimator fixed and vary the *decision policy*.

## 2. Method

**Data-generating process.** Each round a cohort of \(n{=}400\) applicants arrives. Group \(g\in\{A,B\}\) is uniform. Latent qualification \(q\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\) is drawn *identically* for both groups, and true repayment is \(\Pr(\text{repay})=\sigma(1.2q)\) — so **A and B are equally creditworthy**. The model never sees \(q\); it sees four noisy proxy features carrying a **group-correlated offset**, \(x_d = q + \text{off}(g) + \varepsilon_d\), with \(\text{off}(A){=}{+}1.3\), \(\text{off}(B){=}{-}1.3\). Thus for the *same* qualification, group B's observables look worse — proxy discrimination / redlining. The group indicator *is* provided as a feature, so with enough per-group outcome data the model can learn to correct the offset; it can only do so for groups it actually approves.

**Loop.** An L2-regularized logistic model (hand-coded, gradient descent) is trained on all outcomes observed so far. It scores the cohort; a budget of \(k=0.5n\) applicants is approved; **outcomes are revealed only for approved applicants** and appended to the training set. We seed the loop with a *biased* history in which group B is under-represented (B appears with prob 0.15), modeling inherited institutional bias. We run 25 rounds and average over 8 seeds, reporting per-seed standard errors.

**Policies (identical everywhere else).**
- **static** — train once on the biased seed, never retrain. Isolates seed bias from the loop.
- **greedy** — approve the top-\(k\) by predicted repayment.
- **explore** — ε-greedy: reserve \(\varepsilon{=}0.15\) of the budget for *uniformly* random approvals from the rejected pool.
- **explore-targeted** — same 15% budget, but spent *only on group B* from B's rejected pool (informed exploration aimed at the under-covered group — the reviewer's suggested stronger baseline).
- **parity** — group-conditional thresholds that equalize the *approval rate* across groups (a coverage constraint).

**Metrics.** *Approval-rate gap* \(=\!\Pr(\text{approve}\mid A)-\Pr(\text{approve}\mid B)\); *TPR gap* = difference in the fraction of *truly-qualified* applicants approved, per group (equal-opportunity measure); *regret* = percentage utility shortfall vs. a clairvoyant oracle approving the \(k\) highest true-repay-probability applicants (utility = repayers \(-0.6\times\) defaulters); and the observed B-share of approvals (coverage diagnostic). We report mean \(\pm\) standard error over 8 seeds and a paired test on the key utility comparison.

## 3. Results

Averaged over 8 seeds, 25 rounds (mean \(\pm\) SE; `results.json`, `perseed.json`):

| Policy | Mean appr. gap | Final TPR gap | Regret % | B-share appr. |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| static (no retrain) | 0.349 ± 0.057 | 0.352 | 15.72 ± 1.83 | 0.338 |
| greedy | 0.128 ± 0.014 | 0.125 | 8.79 ± 0.63 | 0.449 |
| explore (ε=.15, uniform) | 0.132 ± 0.014 | 0.147 | 17.21 ± 0.37 | 0.427 |
| explore-targeted (B only) | 0.038 ± 0.013 | 0.096 | 16.69 ± 0.61 | 0.466 |
| **parity** | **−0.000 ± 0.000** | **0.027** | **8.30 ± 0.59** | **0.501** |

**The loop leaves an entrenched, not amplified, gap.** The greedy approval gap trajectory is \([0.37, 0.10, 0.12, 0.11, 0.10]\) over rounds \([0,4,9,14,24]\): it starts at the seed-bias level, relaxes, then **plateaus near 0.10** — never reaching equity. Retraining helps versus the frozen model (static 0.35 gap, 15.7% regret) but cannot escape the loop: qualified B applicants are scored low by the offset, so B's observed outcomes come from a range-restricted, self-selected slice that keeps the learned offset-correction too weak.

**Uniform exploration does not repair it.** ε-greedy barely changes B's share of approvals (0.427 vs greedy's 0.449 — in fact *slightly lower*) and leaves the gap unchanged (0.132), while nearly *doubling* regret (17.2% vs 8.8%, non-overlapping across seeds). Random exploration collects a little more B data of the same restricted kind; it does not deliver *representative* coverage.

**Group-targeted exploration helps but is not enough — and is dominated.** Directing the whole exploration budget at group B *does* raise B-coverage (0.466 > 0.449) and **halves the mean gap (0.038 vs 0.103 greedy final)** — so the "exploration fails" story is not merely an artifact of a uniform strawman: informed exploration genuinely moves fairness. But it (i) still does not reach equity (gap 0.038, TPR gap 0.096 vs parity's 0.027), and (ii) pays the same ~16.7% regret, because spending fixed budget on random within-B approvals wastes utility every round. Parity **dominates it on both axes** (gap 0.000, regret 8.3%): a constraint that *reallocates* the existing budget beats one that *randomizes* part of it.

**Coverage constraints eliminate the gap at no utility penalty.** Equal approval *rates* force a representative B slice every round, closing the approval gap to 0.000 and shrinking the TPR gap to 0.027. Its regret (8.30 ± 0.59) is *comparable to* greedy's (8.79 ± 0.63) — **we retract the earlier "beats greedy" claim**: the paired per-seed difference is greedy−parity = 0.49 ± 0.97, reversing sign in 2/8 seeds, paired \(t{=}1.43\) (\(p{\approx}0.19\)) — statistically indistinguishable. The defensible utility claim is that parity repairs the data-generating process, so guaranteeing fairness costs *nothing* in utility here, rather than trading against it — while both exploration variants cost ~8 points.

## 4. Conclusion

In a controlled selective-labels loop where two groups are equally qualified by construction, greedy retraining entrenches a persistent ~13-point disparity. Exploration is the wrong lever: uniform exploration fails outright, and even group-targeted exploration only halves the gap while paying a large utility cost and stopping short of equity. A coverage (parity) constraint eliminates the gap at regret indistinguishable from greedy. The socio-technical takeaway: when a deployed model manufactures its own training data, the *decision policy* — specifically whether it guarantees *representative* coverage of disadvantaged groups, not merely more randomness — determines whether inequity becomes self-fulfilling.

**Limitations.** This is a stylized simulation (logistic model, Gaussian latents, a single offset magnitude, two groups, 8 seeds). We did not sweep the offset, budget, or ε; did not test heavier imbalance or non-linear estimators; and used equal-approval-rate parity rather than an equal-opportunity constraint, which the residual 0.027 TPR gap suggests would be a fairer target. The paired regret comparison, while now reported with a test, still rests on one configuration point. These are natural next perturbations of the released simulator (`sim.py`, `analysis.py`), which run in seconds on CPU.
