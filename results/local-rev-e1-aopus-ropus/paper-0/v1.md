# Exploration Is Not Enough: Why Selective-Labels Feedback Loops Entrench Disparity, and When Coverage Constraints Repair Them

## Abstract

When a deployed predictive model decides who gets a scarce resource (loans, interviews, bail), it observes outcomes *only* for the applicants it accepts. Retraining on this self-selected data — the **selective-labels** regime — is a canonical socio-technical failure mode: the model's own decisions shape the data it learns from. We study, in a controlled simulation, whether such loops amplify disparity between two groups that are *equally qualified in truth* but differ in a group-correlated feature offset (proxy discrimination). We compare four deployment policies under identical dynamics: a no-retrain **static** control, **greedy** retraining, **ε-greedy exploration** (the standard bandit remedy for label scarcity), and a **demographic-parity coverage** constraint. Our central finding is counterintuitive: greedy retraining partially self-corrects a biased seed but *plateaus* at a persistent ~13-point approval-rate gap, and mild exploration **does not close it** (and worsens utility), because the harm is structural range-restriction in the feature–outcome mapping, not mere label sparsity. Only a policy that *guarantees representative coverage* of the disadvantaged group (parity) drives the gap to zero — at *lower* regret than exploration. We release the ~150-line simulator and logs so the loop can be re-run and perturbed.

## 1. Introduction

A recurring lesson from the socio-technical literature is that AI harms cannot be diagnosed from the model in isolation: they emerge from the coupling of a model to the human and institutional context that supplies its data and consumes its decisions [4, 5]. Selective labels are a sharp instance. A lender only learns whether *accepted* applicants repay; rejected applicants are censored. Retraining on accepted-only data closes a loop — decisions determine the training distribution, which determines the next decisions — and this loop can silently entrench inequity even when no protected attribute is used maliciously and the groups are, in fact, equally creditworthy.

Two intuitions from adjacent fields suggest easy fixes, and both are worth testing. From **bandits/active learning**, the disparity looks like an *exploration* problem: the model under-samples a group, so inject randomness to gather its labels [2]. From **fair ML**, it looks like a *constraint* problem: enforce demographic parity. These prescribe different interventions with different costs, and it is not obvious which — if either — actually repairs a feedback loop, or at what utility price. We build a minimal, fast, fully-observable testbed to compare them head-to-head and to isolate *the loop itself* (via a no-retrain control) from the initial data bias.

**Contributions.** (i) A compact, reproducible simulation of a selective-labels retraining loop with proxy discrimination, in which two groups are equally qualified by construction, so any gap is an artifact of the socio-technical dynamics. (ii) An empirical result that mild exploration is *insufficient* — and utility-harmful — when the bias is a structural feature-mapping distortion rather than pure label scarcity. (iii) Evidence that a coverage (parity) constraint dominates on *both* fairness and utility, clarifying when each remedy is appropriate.

### Related Work

Sociotechnical evaluation frames safety and fairness as properties of the model-plus-context, not the model alone; Weidinger et al. [5] and NIST's AI RMF [4] argue technical metrics are necessary but insufficient. On the fairness side, a line of work stresses that fairness is contextual and that many metrics lack construct validity [6]. Human-AI decision studies show reliance and override behavior interact with perceived fairness [1] and that naïve advice-weighting harms outcomes [3]. Our study is complementary and mechanistic: rather than survey human perceptions, we isolate the *data-generating* feedback the deployed model creates, in the spirit of selective-labels and predictive-loop analyses, and quantify which deployment-time policy breaks it. Where much fair-ML work fixes the *estimator* on a static dataset, we hold the estimator fixed and vary the *decision policy*, showing the policy — not the loss function — governs whether the loop entrenches disparity.

## 2. Method

**Data-generating process.** Each round a cohort of \(n{=}400\) applicants arrives. Group \(g\in\{A,B\}\) is uniform. Latent qualification \(q\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\) is drawn *identically* for both groups, and true repayment is \(\Pr(\text{repay})=\sigma(1.2q)\) — so **A and B are equally creditworthy**. The model never sees \(q\); it sees four noisy proxy features carrying a **group-correlated offset**, \(x_d = q + \text{off}(g) + \varepsilon_d\), with \(\text{off}(A){=}{+}1.3\), \(\text{off}(B){=}{-}1.3\). Thus for the *same* qualification, group B's observables look worse — proxy discrimination / redlining. The group indicator *is* provided as a feature, so with enough per-group outcome data the model can learn to correct the offset; it can only do so for groups it actually approves.

**Loop.** An L2-regularized logistic model (hand-coded, gradient descent) is trained on all outcomes observed so far. It scores the cohort; a budget of \(k=0.5n\) applicants is approved; **outcomes are revealed only for approved applicants** and appended to the training set. We seed the loop with a *biased* history in which group B is under-represented (B appears with prob 0.15), modeling an inherited institutional bias. We run 25 rounds and average over 8 seeds.

**Policies (identical everywhere else).**
- **static** — train once on the biased seed, never retrain. Isolates the seed bias from the loop.
- **greedy** — approve the top-\(k\) by predicted repayment.
- **explore** — ε-greedy: reserve \(\varepsilon{=}0.15\) of the budget for random approvals from the rejected pool (the textbook label-scarcity remedy).
- **parity** — group-conditional thresholds that equalize the *approval rate* across groups (a coverage constraint).

**Metrics.** *Approval-rate gap* \(=\!\Pr(\text{approve}\mid A)-\Pr(\text{approve}\mid B)\); *TPR gap* = difference in the fraction of *truly-qualified* (repay\(=1\)) applicants approved, per group (a distributive-fairness / equal-opportunity measure); *regret* = percentage utility shortfall vs. a clairvoyant oracle that approves the \(k\) highest true-repay-probability applicants (utility = repayers \(-0.6\times\) defaulters); and the observed B-share of approvals (data-coverage diagnostic).

## 3. Results

Averaged over 8 seeds, 25 rounds (`results.json`, `run.log`):

| Policy | Final appr. gap | Mean appr. gap | Final TPR gap | Regret % | B-share of approvals |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| static (no retrain) | 0.325 | 0.349 | 0.352 | 15.7 | 0.338 |
| greedy | 0.103 | 0.128 | 0.125 | 8.8 | 0.449 |
| explore (ε=.15) | 0.127 | 0.132 | 0.147 | 17.2 | 0.428 |
| **parity** | **0.000** | **0.000** | **0.027** | **8.3** | **0.501** |

**The loop leaves an entrenched, not amplified, gap.** The greedy approval gap trajectory is \([0.37, 0.10, 0.12, 0.11, 0.10]\) over rounds \([0,4,9,14,24]\): it starts at the seed-bias level (0.37), relaxes, and then **plateaus near 0.10** — it never reaches equity. Retraining *helps* relative to the frozen model (static stays at 0.33 gap, 15.7% regret), but cannot escape the loop: qualified B applicants are scored low by the offset, so B's observed outcomes come from a range-restricted, self-selected slice that keeps the learned offset-correction too weak.

**Exploration does not repair it.** ε-greedy exploration barely changes the B-share of approvals (0.43 vs greedy's 0.45) and leaves the gap essentially unchanged (0.13), while nearly *doubling* regret (17.2% vs 8.8%) by spending budget on random low-value approvals. The reason is diagnostic for practice: the harm here is not that B is *unlabeled* but that B's labeled sample is *systematically shifted* by the very decision rule. Random exploration collects a little more B data of the same restricted kind; it does not guarantee the *representative* coverage needed to identify the offset.

**Coverage constraints dominate.** Enforcing equal approval *rates* forces the model to approve a representative slice of B every round, closing the approval gap to 0.000 and shrinking the TPR gap to 0.027 — and it does so at the **lowest regret (8.3%)**, beating even greedy. Guaranteeing coverage repairs the data-generating process, so the estimator itself becomes better calibrated for B, aligning the fairness fix with the utility objective rather than trading against it.

**Ablation (seed bias).** The static control confirms the seed bias alone is severe and persistent (0.33 gap); the contrast static→greedy shows retraining is a partial, insufficient remedy; greedy→parity shows *how* the loop is broken is what matters, not merely *that* the model retrains.

## 4. Conclusion

In a controlled selective-labels loop where two groups are equally qualified by construction, greedy retraining entrenches a persistent ~13-point disparity that mild exploration fails to remove and even makes costlier, whereas a coverage (parity) constraint eliminates it at lower regret. The practical, socio-technical takeaway: when a deployed model manufactures its own training data, the *decision policy* — not the loss function or an exploration bonus — determines whether inequity becomes self-fulfilling; interventions must guarantee *representative* coverage of disadvantaged groups, not just more randomness.

**Limitations & what we did not get to.** This is a stylized simulation (logistic model, Gaussian latents, a single offset magnitude, two groups). We did not sweep the offset, budget, or ε; did not test heavier group-imbalance or non-linear estimators; and used equal-approval-rate parity rather than an equal-opportunity constraint, which the residual 0.027 TPR gap suggests would be a fairer target. The oracle and utility weights are illustrative. These are natural next perturbations of the released simulator (`sim.py`), which runs in seconds on CPU.
