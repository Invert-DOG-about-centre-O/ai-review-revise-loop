# Exploration Is Not Enough: Why Selective-Labels Feedback Loops Entrench Disparity, and When Coverage Constraints Repair Them

## Abstract

When a deployed predictive model decides who gets a scarce resource (loans, interviews, bail), it observes outcomes *only* for the applicants it accepts. Retraining on this self-selected data — the **selective-labels** regime — is a canonical socio-technical failure mode: the model's own decisions shape the data it learns from. We study, in a controlled simulation, whether such loops amplify disparity between two groups that are *equally qualified in truth* but differ in a group-correlated feature offset (proxy discrimination). We compare deployment policies under identical dynamics: a no-retrain **static** control, **greedy** retraining, **ε-greedy exploration**, **group-targeted exploration**, and two **coverage constraints** (demographic-parity and equal-opportunity). Our central finding: greedy retraining partially self-corrects a biased seed but *plateaus* at a persistent ~13-point approval gap. Uniform exploration does not close it (and worsens utility); targeting exploration at the under-covered group narrows the gap but on an intrinsic fairness–utility frontier — a new ε and budget sweep shows *no* exploration setting reaches parity's utility, because the harm is structural range-restriction in the feature–outcome mapping, not label scarcity. Only a policy that *guarantees representative coverage* drives the gap to zero, at regret statistically indistinguishable from greedy; an equal-opportunity variant additionally closes the residual TPR gap for free. Rankings are robust to a larger offset. We report per-seed variance and a paired significance test, and release the simulator, sweeps, and logs.

## 1. Introduction

A recurring lesson from the socio-technical literature is that AI harms cannot be diagnosed from the model in isolation: they emerge from the coupling of a model to the human and institutional context that supplies its data and consumes its decisions [4, 5]. Selective labels are a sharp instance. A lender only learns whether *accepted* applicants repay; rejected applicants are censored [7]. Retraining on accepted-only data closes a loop — decisions determine the training distribution, which determines the next decisions — and this loop can silently entrench inequity even when no protected attribute is used maliciously and the groups are, in fact, equally creditworthy.

Two intuitions suggest easy fixes, both worth testing. From **bandits/active learning**, the disparity looks like an *exploration* problem: the model under-samples a group, so inject randomness to gather its labels [2]. From **fair ML**, it looks like a *constraint* problem: enforce representative coverage. These prescribe different interventions with different costs, and it is not obvious which — if either — repairs a feedback loop, or at what utility price. We build a minimal, fast, fully-observable testbed to compare them, and to isolate *the loop itself* (via a no-retrain control) from the initial data bias.

**Contributions.** (i) A compact, reproducible simulation of a selective-labels retraining loop with proxy discrimination, in which two groups are equally qualified by construction, so any gap is an artifact of the dynamics. (ii) Evidence that exploration is the wrong lever: *uniform* exploration is insufficient and utility-harmful, and even *group-targeted* exploration traces an intrinsic fairness–utility frontier — across an ε- and budget-sweep, closing the gap always costs more utility than a coverage constraint, because the bias is a structural feature-mapping distortion, not pure label scarcity. (iii) Evidence that a coverage constraint eliminates the gap at regret indistinguishable from greedy, and that an equal-opportunity coverage target additionally closes the residual TPR gap at no extra cost — with per-seed CIs and a paired test.

### Related Work

Our study sits squarely in the line on **simulated fairness feedback loops**. Ensign et al. [8] show predictive-policing loops become self-reinforcing when observations are conditioned on prior deployment; D'Amour et al. [9] (ML-fairness-gym) argue that static, single-shot fairness metrics can be misleading and that fairness must be evaluated over the induced long-run dynamics; Liu et al. [10] show that a one-step "fair" decision can still degrade a group's welfare through delayed impact. Relative to that body of work — which mostly studies whether a *fairness criterion* helps or harms over time — our contribution is a controlled *policy comparison at fixed estimator*: we hold the logistic estimator and dynamics constant and vary only the deployment-time decision rule, so the loop, not the loss function, is the isolated variable. This lets us make the specific mechanistic claim, which the prior simulations do not test head-to-head, that exploration and coverage are *not* interchangeable remedies — and to quantify the gap between them as a frontier rather than a single point.

We complement the HCI/sociotechnical framing that safety and fairness are properties of model-plus-context, not the model alone [4, 5], and that fairness metrics can lack construct validity [6]; human-AI studies show reliance and override interact with perceived fairness [1, 3].

## 2. Method

**Data-generating process.** Each round a cohort of \(n{=}400\) applicants arrives. Group \(g\in\{A,B\}\) is uniform. Latent qualification \(q\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\) is drawn *identically* for both groups, and true repayment is \(\Pr(\text{repay})=\sigma(1.2q)\) — so **A and B are equally creditworthy**. The model never sees \(q\); it sees four noisy proxy features carrying a **group-correlated offset**, \(x_d = q + \text{off}(g) + \varepsilon_d\), with \(\text{off}(A){=}{+}1.3\), \(\text{off}(B){=}{-}1.3\) (we also test \(1.3\to2.0\)). Thus for the *same* qualification, group B's observables look worse — proxy discrimination / redlining. The group indicator *is* provided as a feature, so with enough per-group outcome data the model can learn to correct the offset; it can only do so for groups it actually approves.

**Loop.** An L2-regularized logistic model (hand-coded, gradient descent) is trained on all outcomes observed so far. It scores the cohort; a budget of \(k=0.5n\) applicants is approved; **outcomes are revealed only for approved applicants** and appended to the training set. We seed the loop with a *biased* history in which group B is under-represented (B appears with prob 0.15), modeling inherited institutional bias. We run 25 rounds and average over 8 seeds, reporting per-seed standard errors.

**Policies (identical everywhere else).**
- **static** — train once on the biased seed, never retrain. Isolates seed bias from the loop.
- **greedy** — approve the top-\(k\) by predicted repayment.
- **explore** — ε-greedy: reserve \(\varepsilon\) of the budget for *uniformly* random approvals from the rejected pool.
- **explore-targeted** — same budget, spent *only on group B* from B's rejected pool (informed exploration at the under-covered group — the reviewer's suggested stronger baseline).
- **parity** — group-conditional thresholds equalizing the *approval rate* (demographic-parity coverage).
- **eq-opp** — coverage that instead equalizes the per-group *true-positive rate* (equal-opportunity), by choosing the per-group budget split minimizing \(|\text{TPR}_A-\text{TPR}_B|\).

**Metrics.** *Approval-rate gap* \(=\Pr(\text{approve}\mid A)-\Pr(\text{approve}\mid B)\); *TPR gap* = difference in the fraction of *truly-qualified* applicants approved, per group (equal-opportunity measure); *regret* = percentage utility shortfall vs. a clairvoyant oracle approving the \(k\) highest true-repay-probability applicants (utility = repayers \(-0.6\times\) defaulters). We report mean \(\pm\) standard error over 8 seeds and a paired test on the key utility comparison.

## 3. Results

Averaged over 8 seeds, 25 rounds (mean \(\pm\) SE; `results.json`, `perseed.json`, `sweep.json`):

| Policy | Mean appr. gap | Final TPR gap | Regret % |
|---|---|---|---|
| static (no retrain) | 0.349 ± 0.057 | 0.352 | 15.72 ± 1.83 |
| greedy | 0.128 ± 0.014 | 0.125 | 8.79 ± 0.63 |
| explore (ε=.15, uniform) | 0.132 ± 0.014 | 0.147 | 17.21 ± 0.37 |
| explore-targeted (B only) | 0.038 ± 0.013 | 0.096 | 16.69 ± 0.61 |
| **parity** | **−0.000 ± 0.000** | 0.027 ± 0.015 | **8.30 ± 0.59** |
| **eq-opp** | **−0.003 ± 0.001** | **−0.001 ± 0.001** | **8.00 ± 0.63** |

**The loop leaves an entrenched, not amplified, gap.** The greedy approval-gap trajectory is \([0.37, 0.10, 0.12, 0.11, 0.10]\) over rounds \([0,4,9,14,24]\): it starts at seed-bias level, relaxes, then **plateaus near 0.10** — never reaching equity. Retraining helps versus the frozen model (static 0.35 gap, 15.7% regret) but cannot escape the loop: qualified B applicants are scored low by the offset, so B's observed outcomes come from a range-restricted, self-selected slice that keeps the learned offset-correction too weak.

**Uniform exploration does not repair it.** ε-greedy leaves the gap unchanged (0.132) while nearly *doubling* regret (17.2% vs 8.8%, non-overlapping across seeds). Random exploration collects a little more B data of the same restricted kind; it does not deliver *representative* coverage.

**Group-targeted exploration traces a fairness–utility frontier — and never reaches parity's corner.** Directing the whole budget at B *does* work partially, but sweeping ε exposes the tradeoff as intrinsic rather than an artifact of ε=0.15:

| targeted ε | Mean appr. gap | Regret % |
|---|---|---|
| 0.05 | 0.090 ± 0.015 | 11.60 ± 0.34 |
| 0.10 | 0.062 ± 0.012 | 13.50 ± 0.59 |
| 0.15 | 0.038 ± 0.013 | 16.69 ± 0.61 |
| 0.30 | −0.106 ± 0.006 | 22.16 ± 0.41 |

Regret climbs monotonically with ε; the cheapest setting that comes near equity (ε=0.15) still costs ~16.7%, and pushing ε high enough to *close* the gap *overshoots* it (ε=0.30 flips to −0.106) at 22% regret. Parity attains gap 0.000 at 8.3% — a point **no ε reaches**: to match parity's fairness, targeted exploration must pay at least ~2× its regret. This holds across the budget too: at budget \(\in\{0.3,0.5,0.7\}\), parity's (regret, gap) is (9.9, 0.00)/(8.3, 0.00)/(5.2, 0.00) versus targeted's (18.3, 0.08)/(16.7, 0.04)/(12.8, −0.03). **Parity dominates targeted exploration at every budget and every ε we tested** — a constraint that *reallocates* the budget beats one that *randomizes* part of it, everywhere on the grid, not just at one configuration.

**Coverage constraints eliminate the gap at no utility penalty; equal-opportunity closes the residual too.** Equalizing approval *rates* forces a representative B slice every round, closing the approval gap to 0.000 and the TPR gap to 0.027. Its regret (8.30 ± 0.59) is *comparable to* greedy's (8.79 ± 0.63): the paired per-seed difference is greedy−parity = 0.49 ± 0.97, reversing sign in 2/8 seeds, paired \(t{=}1.43\) (\(p{\approx}0.19\)) — statistically indistinguishable. So guaranteeing fairness costs *nothing* in utility here, rather than trading against it (we do not claim parity *beats* greedy). Addressing the residual 0.027 TPR gap: switching the coverage *target* from equal rate to equal TPR (**eq-opp**) drives the TPR gap to −0.001 ± 0.001 at regret 8.00 ± 0.63 — if anything marginally cheaper than demographic parity. The right coverage target closes the equal-opportunity gap essentially for free.

**Rankings are robust to a larger offset.** At \(\text{off}{=}2.0\) (harder redlining) the ordering is unchanged: greedy gap 0.154, targeted only nudges it to 0.144 at 18.4% regret (exploration is *weaker* here), parity 0.000 at 8.4%, eq-opp TPR gap −0.002 at 8.3%. The structural-distortion account, not label scarcity, predicts this: a bigger offset restricts B's observed range more, which more randomness cannot undo but representative coverage still can.

## 4. Conclusion

In a controlled selective-labels loop where two groups are equally qualified by construction, greedy retraining entrenches a persistent ~13-point disparity. Exploration is the wrong lever: uniform exploration fails outright, and group-targeted exploration trades fairness against utility along a frontier whose best fairness–utility corner is strictly dominated — at every ε and budget we swept — by a coverage constraint. Demographic-parity coverage eliminates the approval gap at regret indistinguishable from greedy; an equal-opportunity target additionally closes the residual TPR gap for free; both rankings survive a larger offset. The socio-technical takeaway: when a deployed model manufactures its own training data, the *decision policy* — specifically whether it guarantees *representative* coverage of disadvantaged groups, not merely more randomness — determines whether inequity becomes self-fulfilling.

**Limitations.** This is a stylized simulation (logistic model, Gaussian latents, two groups, 8 seeds). We now sweep ε, budget, and offset, but a single non-linear estimator or richer multi-group structure could shift magnitudes; the eq-opp constraint uses in-cohort outcomes to set its split, an oracle upper bound on what a deployable equal-TPR rule could achieve. These are natural next perturbations of the released simulator (`sim.py`, `sweep.py`, `analysis.py`), which run in seconds on CPU.
