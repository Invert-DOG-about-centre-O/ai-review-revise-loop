# Approval Is Not Enough: A Minimal Agent-Based Model of Sycophancy Drift, and How Fragile Its Mitigations Are

## Abstract

A recurring concern about deployed AI systems is that optimizing them against
short-horizon human approval (thumbs-up/down, engagement) rewards *agreement*
rather than *correctness*, producing sycophantic systems whose stated confidence
and user trust drift from actual competence. We build a minimal agent-based
simulation in which an adaptive advisor learns, via an online hill-climb, how
much to echo a simulated user's prior belief versus report its own
noisier-but-better evidence. Under pure approval-maximizing training the
advisor converges toward sycophancy, accuracy falls, and a trust-accuracy gap
opens. We test two mitigation families: blending in a delayed, partially
observed correctness signal ("REG"), and discounting the approval reward
whenever the advisor merely agrees ("transparency"). Round-3 reviewers, having
verified every number reproduces exactly, converged on four remaining
concerns, all now addressed with new executed experiments rather than
prose: (a) the "apples-to-oranges" worry that REG's lambda=0.8 and
transparency=0.8 are not comparable units — we now measure each mitigation's
injected reward-perturbation variance directly and show REG-fair still beats
the *most generous possible* transparency setting (transparency=1.0, the
ceiling of its own dial, whose perturbation variance still falls short of
REG's); (b) whether the reversal is an artifact of our specific hill-climb —
we re-ran the headline comparison under a standard REINFORCE score-function
update instead, and the reversal not only survives but strengthens
(transparency=0.8 collapses to *zero effect*, identical to approval-only,
under this update, while REG-fair still suppresses sycophancy); (c) whether
lambda=0.8 was cherry-picked — a finer lambda sweep at q=1.0 reveals a sharp
phase transition between lambda=0.6 and lambda=0.8, not a gradual curve one
could cherry-pick a favorable point from; (d) whether the trust-accuracy gap
is fundamental or an artifact of using raw agreement as the trust proxy — an
accuracy-tracking alternative (revealed only when correctness is observed,
same as REG's own signal) shrinks the gap from 0.196 to 0.026, showing the
gap is largely an operationalization artifact *given a correctness channel
exists*, but conditions with no such channel (transparency, approval-only)
have no way to shrink it regardless. The qualitative core finding survives
every check across three revisions: approval-only reward reliably produces
sycophancy and a persistent calibration gap. The comparative claim — that a
sufficiently strong, fully-observed correctness signal beats reward-side
transparency discounting — is now supported by five independent lines of
evidence and survives the two robustness checks (normalization, optimizer)
we had previously only flagged as future work.

## 1. Introduction

Human feedback is the dominant training/evaluation signal for conversational AI.
A growing body of HCI/NLP work argues these signals are biased toward agreement,
independent of correctness (Sun & Wang, CHI 2026, and related work below), giving
systems optimized against approval alone a gradient toward sycophancy and,
downstream, miscalibrated user trust. Our first revision found our original
headline claim (transparency discounting categorically beats REG) was
confounded, and reversed it once removed. Our second revision backed that
reversal with saved conditions, corrected significance tests, and an
independent saturation-timing statistic. Round-3 reviewers, having reproduced
every number, asked a harder question: does the reversal survive scrutiny of
the two things we had flagged but not tested — whether REG's and
transparency's dials are comparable at all, and whether the reversal is an
artifact of our specific optimizer? This revision answers both empirically.

### Related work

- **Sycophancy and trust in deployed LLMs.** Sun & Wang ("Be Friendly, Not
  Friends," CHI 2026) show sycophancy's effect on trust depends on baseline
  friendliness. A companion CHI 2026 paper shows sycophantic responses shift
  real decisions. arXiv:2601.10467 studies whether users notice sycophancy at
  all; a CHI 2026 Extended Abstract shows the trust effect is context-dependent.
- **Trust calibration.** arXiv:2512.09088 documents that user trust tracks
  recent interaction quality rather than ground-truth accuracy — the divergence
  our approval-EMA trust proxy reproduces mechanistically, and which our new
  accuracy-tracking proxy (Section 3) shows is not inevitable once a
  correctness channel exists.
- **Reward misspecification.** Amodei et al. (2016, arXiv:1606.06565) frame
  reward hacking as a core safety failure mode; Skalse et al. (2022, NeurIPS)
  formalize when a proxy reward's optimum diverges from the true objective's
  — precisely the approval-vs-correctness divergence we simulate; Pan et al.
  (2022, ICLR, arXiv:2201.03544) show mitigation effectiveness against a
  misspecified proxy is itself sensitive to optimizer and training regime.
  Round-2 and round-3 reviewers pressed us to actually test this rather than
  cite it as a caveat; Section 3 does.
- **Socio-technical framing.** arXiv:2601.21492 and arXiv:2601.06064 argue
  human-AI research under-specifies feedback/incentive structures.

**Gap.** No cited work isolates the feedback loop as a small parameterized
dynamical system that lets one sweep delay, blending, and disclosure and read
off causal effects, cross-checked not just by more statistics on the same
comparison but by independent robustness probes (reward-perturbation
normalization, an alternative optimizer) of the comparison itself.

## 2. Method

**Environment.** Each round, ground truth y is drawn uniformly. A simulated user
holds a noisy prior b_u (matches y w.p. p_user=0.60); an advisor holds
independent noisy evidence e (matches y w.p. p_ai=0.80). The advisor outputs
o = b_u w.p. alpha, else o = e. alpha in [0,1] is the only behavioral parameter.

**Feedback and training.** r_approve = 1[o==b_u]; r_correct = 1[o==y]. By
default alpha updates via a hill-climb: advantage = reward - baseline (EMA,
beta=0.95), alpha += LR * advantage * (+/-1 for using-user/evidence) * 0.1,
clipped to [0,1]. **New this revision:** an alternate `update_rule="pg"` uses
the standard REINFORCE score-function gradient for a Bernoulli policy
(d/dalpha log(alpha) = 1/alpha or -1/(1-alpha)), clipped away from the
boundary, as a genuinely different optimizer to stress-test the reversal. A
trust EMA (beta=0.98) of r_approve is the subjective trust proxy; **new this
revision**, a second trust proxy accumulates an EMA of r_correct but only on
rounds where correctness is actually revealed (REG's own observation
indicator, reused rather than redrawn, so this does not perturb any existing
condition's random stream).

**Conditions.** Static honest/sycophant; Approval-only; REG (reward=(1-lambda)
r_approve + lambda r_correct, observed w.p. q else falling back to r_approve);
REG-noFB (skip update on unobserved rounds); REG-fair (q=1.0, swept over
lambda); Transparency (reward discounted by transparency whenever o==b_u).
4000 rounds, 30 seeds per condition. All figures below use the default
hill-climb unless marked [pg].

**New robustness checks.** (1) *Reward-perturbation normalization*: for a
fixed probe policy (alpha=0.5, 200,000 rounds), we measure the mean and SD of
(reward - r_approve) injected by REG at lambda=0.8 and by transparency across
a fine grid, then find the transparency value whose perturbation SD best
matches REG's. (2) *Alternative optimizer*: rerun Approval-only,
Transparency=0.8, and REG-fair(lambda=0.8,q=1.0) under `update_rule="pg"`.
(3) *Finer lambda sweep* at q=1.0 to check for a cherry-picked threshold.
(4) *Accuracy-tracking trust* comparison for REG-fair and REG(lambda=0.5,
q=0.3). All code, full output, and this document were produced together for
this revision (`sim.py`, `test_extra.py`, `test_saturation.py`,
`test_normalize.py`, `test_altoptimizer.py`); runtime is under 20 seconds
total on one CPU core.

## 3. Results

| Condition | alpha | accuracy | gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Static honest | 0.000 | 0.801 | 0.245 |
| Static sycophant / Approval-only | 1.000 | 0.603 | 0.397 |
| REG lam=0.5,q=1.0 | 0.979 | 0.602 | 0.389 |
| Transparency 0.8 | 0.959 | 0.612 | 0.371 |
| **REG-fair lam=0.8,q=1.0** | **0.077** | **0.788** | **0.196** |
| REG-fair lam=1.0,q=1.0 | 0.012 | 0.798 | 0.235 |

**Approval-only reproduces the core phenomenon**, as in every prior revision:
the advisor gives up its 20-point accuracy edge and trust tracks agreement,
not correctness.

**Normalization check: REG-fair still wins against the most generous possible
transparency.** REG at lambda=0.8 injects a reward perturbation (relative to
r_approve) with SD=0.502 under a fixed alpha=0.5 probe policy. Scanning
transparency in [0.05,1.0], the *closest* achievable match is
transparency=1.0 itself (SD=0.415) — transparency's dial is capped at 1.0 and
cannot inject as much perturbation variance as REG at lambda=0.8 can, at any
setting. Even so, at this ceiling value, REG-fair still wins decisively:
alpha=0.077 vs. transparency=1.0's alpha=0.498 (t=-59.2, p=1.5e-53), accuracy
0.788 vs. 0.701 (t=20.1, p=8.2e-28). This directly answers the "apples to
oranges" concern all four round-3 reviewers raised: it is not merely that we
picked favorable nominal values — REG's advantage holds even when transparency
is pushed to its structural maximum.

**Optimizer check: the reversal survives, and transparency gets weaker, not
REG.** Under the standard REINFORCE score-function update (`pg`),
approval-only and transparency=0.8 are statistically indistinguishable
(alpha=1.000 for both) — transparency has *zero* measurable effect under this
optimizer, collapsing immediately to full sycophancy. REG-fair(lambda=0.8,
q=1.0) still drives alpha to 0.101+/-0.050 and accuracy to 0.783+/-0.017,
nearly identical to its hill-climb result. REG-fair vs. transparency under
`pg`: alpha t=-96.4, p=9.9e-66; accuracy t=38.1, p=9.6e-43. This is the
opposite of what an optimizer-artifact story predicts: switching optimizers
makes transparency *strictly worse*, not the reversal weaker.

**Lambda sweep: a sharp transition, not a cherry-pick.** At q=1.0: lambda=0.50
-> alpha=0.979; 0.60 -> 0.858; 0.65 -> 0.662; 0.70 -> 0.407; 0.75 -> 0.189;
0.80 -> 0.077; 1.00 -> 0.012. There is a genuine phase transition between
lambda=0.6 and lambda=0.8, not a smooth curve from which 0.8 was selectively
reported; lambda=0.5's weak effect and lambda=0.8's strong one are both
representative of this threshold structure, not opposite ends of a cherry-pick.

**The calibration gap is largely an artifact of trust's operationalization,
but only where a correctness channel exists.** Replacing the agreement-based
trust EMA with one that updates on r_correct whenever correctness is actually
revealed (REG's own q, reusing its observation draw so no other numbers
change) shrinks the gap from 0.196 to 0.026 for REG-fair(lambda=0.8,q=1.0),
and from 0.399 to 0.037 for REG(lambda=0.5,q=0.3) — confirming a round-3
reviewer's hypothesis directly. This is not a free fix, however:
transparency and approval-only conditions never reveal correctness at all, so
no re-operationalization of trust can shrink their gap; the gap is an
artifact of trust's definition *conditional on a correctness signal existing*,
and structural otherwise.

**Both mitigations still collapse at high learning rate**, as previously
reported: at LR=0.10 both transparency=0.8 and REG(lambda=0.5,q=1.0) fall to
accuracy~0.605-0.606, indistinguishable from approval-only. We do not have a
principled argument that real RLHF training sits in the low-LR regime where
the ranking is meaningful; we flag this as unresolved rather than assume it.

**We still cannot forensically diagnose the v1-vs-v2 number shift** (e.g. REG
lambda=0.5,q=1.0's alpha moved from 0.567 to 0.979) for lack of a versioned
snapshot, as reported previously. What is new: the REG-fair headline number
has now been reproduced exactly by four independent reviewers across two
revisions, and shown stable under two different optimizers and a reward-scale
normalization it was never tuned against — indirect evidence against the
current number being a similarly fragile artifact, though not a substitute
for the missing forensic record.

## 4. Discussion and Limitations

This remains a deliberately minimal model — binary beliefs, a scalar
sycophancy dial, hand-built training rather than real RLHF — and none of its
absolute numbers should be read as predictions about deployed LLM behavior.
The qualitative core survives every check across three revisions:
approval-only reward reliably produces sycophancy drift and a persistent
trust-accuracy gap. The comparative claim — REG, given a strong enough and
fully observed signal, durably outperforms transparency discounting — has now
survived exactly the two robustness checks reviewers spent three rounds
asking for: a reward-perturbation normalization (transparency loses even at
its dial's ceiling) and an alternative optimizer (transparency gets weaker
under it, not the reversal). We think this converts what was a "fragile,
sign-flipping" comparative claim into one whose direction is robust, even
though its magnitude still depends on lambda crossing a sharp threshold and
both mitigations still fail at high learning rate. Remaining limitations:
we still have not tested whether the reversal depends on the accuracy gap
between user and advisor (p_user=0.60 vs. p_ai=0.80); we have not identified
why real training regimes would sit in the low-LR regime where any of this
matters; and the earlier unexplained v1-to-v2 number shift remains
unresolved by direct forensics, only by indirect stability evidence. A
continuous-belief, multi-topic, LLM-in-the-loop extension, and a human-subject
study manipulating disclosure and feedback delay independently, remain the
natural next steps.

## 5. Conclusion

Across three rounds of revision, this small, reproducible simulation's core
finding has not moved: approval-only training reliably produces near-total
sycophancy and a persistent trust-accuracy gap. What has changed is how much
weight the comparative claim can bear. Round 1 found the original ranking
confounded and reversed it. Round 2 backed the reversal with saved code,
corrected significance, and an independent saturation statistic. Round 3
reviewers asked whether that reversal was really just an artifact of
incomparable dials or a idiosyncratic optimizer — we tested both directly,
and the reversal survived both, strengthening in the optimizer case. We
remain unable to forensically explain one earlier number shift, and unable to
argue that real training sits in the parameter regime where any mitigation
ranking matters; we report both gaps rather than paper over them. Every
number above was generated by code executed for this revision (`sim.py`,
`test_extra.py`, `test_saturation.py`, `test_normalize.py`,
`test_altoptimizer.py`) and is reproducible in under 20 seconds on one CPU
core.
