# A Single Temperature Goes a Long Way: How Well Does Post-hoc Temperature Scaling Calibrate an Overconfident Language Model Under Distribution Shift?

## Abstract

Neural language models (LMs) produce a probability distribution over the next
token, but when trained to low loss on limited data they become badly
*overconfident*: the probability they assign to their top prediction far exceeds
the frequency with which that prediction is correct. Post-hoc **temperature
scaling** (TS) — dividing the logits by a single learned scalar $T$ — is the
standard remedy, but two questions matter for probabilistic reliability in
practice and are under-examined in a controlled setting: (i) does a temperature
fit on in-domain data still calibrate the model when the *input distribution
shifts*, and (ii) is a single global scalar enough, or should the temperature
adapt to how uncertain the model already is on each input? We build a fully
self-contained, CPU-only testbed: a small character-level LSTM trained on
synthetic order-2 Markov text, deliberately overfit into the overconfident
regime, and evaluated on both in-domain and a *shifted* Markov source. We
compare raw softmax, global TS, and a novel **entropy-conditioned adaptive
temperature scaling (ECATS)** that fits a separate temperature per
predictive-entropy quantile. Global TS cuts expected calibration error (ECE)
from **0.41 to 0.024** in-domain, and — encouragingly — most of that benefit
*transfers* under shift (ECE 0.48 → 0.047). ECATS learns temperatures that vary
2.6× across entropy bins, yet improves ECE by only ~0.001 in-domain and *not at
all* under shift. Our controlled result reinforces a practical message: for a
uniformly-overconfident LM, a single temperature is a remarkably strong,
shift-robust baseline, and input-adaptive temperature buys little.

## 1. Introduction

Large language models generate text autoregressively by sampling from a
softmax over next-token logits. The *quality of those probabilities* — not just
the top-1 accuracy — governs downstream behavior: sampling temperature, beam
search, abstention, uncertainty-aware decoding, and hallucination detection all
consume the model's probability estimates. A model is **calibrated** if, among
predictions made with confidence $c$, a fraction $\approx c$ are correct.
Modern neural networks, and LMs in particular, are typically *overconfident*
after training [Guo et al., 2017], which corrupts every downstream use of the
probabilities.

The dominant fix is **temperature scaling** [Guo et al., 2017]: learn one
scalar $T$ on held-out data and replace $\mathrm{softmax}(z)$ with
$\mathrm{softmax}(z/T)$. It preserves the argmax (so accuracy is unchanged) and
is cheap, and the recent LLM-calibration literature repeatedly finds it hard to
beat [Semantic Calibration through Temperature Scaling, ICLR 2025; Token-Level
Temperature Scaling, OpenReview 2025]. Yet two assumptions embedded in "just
scale by one number" deserve scrutiny in a controlled setting:

1. **Shift robustness.** $T$ is fit on in-domain validation data. Real
   deployments face inputs from a different distribution. Does the in-domain
   $T$ still calibrate the model, or does miscalibration re-emerge?
2. **Uniformity of overconfidence.** A single $T$ assumes the model is
   overconfident *by the same factor everywhere*. If the model is more
   overconfident on high-entropy (genuinely uncertain) contexts than on
   confident ones, a temperature that *adapts to the input's entropy* should
   help.

This paper studies both questions in a clean, reproducible, CPU-only testbed
and proposes **entropy-conditioned adaptive temperature scaling (ECATS)** as
the natural test of assumption (2). Our contribution is empirical and
deliberately modest in scope: a controlled measurement, with an honest negative
finding for the adaptive method and a positive finding for the shift-robustness
of plain TS.

### Related Work

Calibration of neural classifiers was popularized by Guo et al. [2017], who
showed deep nets are overconfident and that temperature scaling — a one-parameter
special case of Platt scaling — restores calibration better than isotonic
regression or histogram binning while preserving accuracy. For LLMs, a 2025
survey [Uncertainty Quantification and Confidence Calibration in LLMs, arXiv
2503.15850] catalogues logit-based, verbalized, and sampling-based confidence
measures and their calibration. Temperature scaling remains central: ICLR-2025
work casts *semantic* calibration through the lens of temperature scaling and
proposes calibration-specific losses; OpenReview and MICCAI 2025 studies confirm
TS "consistently improves calibration, outperforming more expressive token-level
recalibration methods." A parallel line studies *verbalized* probabilities and
whether models can state their own uncertainty [Calibrating Verbalized
Probabilities, arXiv 2410.06707; Direct Confidence Alignment, arXiv 2512.11998].
Bayesian approaches (MC-dropout, BLoB/BLoRA) estimate epistemic uncertainty via
parameter distributions but are computationally heavy. Recent large-scale
benchmarks [Revisiting Uncertainty Estimation, NeurIPS 2025] evaluate 80 models
and emphasize that decoding stochasticity and input ambiguity are LM-specific
uncertainty sources. Missing from much of this work is a *controlled* study
that isolates two variables — distribution shift and input-adaptivity of the
temperature — with ground-truth data and a single trained model. We fill that
gap with a synthetic-source testbed.

## 2. Method

**Testbed.** We generate text from an order-2 Markov source over an alphabet of
$V=20$ symbols. Transition distributions are random softmaxes over per-context
logits, with a source "sharpness" $\tau$ controlling entropy. The in-domain
source uses $\tau=1.0$; a **shifted** source uses different random logits and a
higher-entropy $\tau=1.6$. Using a known generative process gives us clean,
reproducible data and an honest distribution shift.

**Model.** A small character-level LM: 48-d embedding → single-layer LSTM
(128 hidden units) → linear head, reading a context of 8 tokens to predict the
next. We train with Adam (lr $3\times10^{-3}$) for 18 epochs on only **15k**
tokens. The small dataset and many epochs are deliberate: they push the model
into the *overconfident* regime that makes calibration non-trivial (train NLL
far below held-out NLL).

**Calibration methods.**
- **(0) Raw.** The model's softmax, $T=1$.
- **(1) Global TS (baseline).** One scalar $T^\*$ minimizing validation NLL,
  found by a grid search over $[0.5,14]$ with local refinement. Applied
  unchanged to every test example, in-domain and shifted.
- **(2) ECATS (ours).** We bin validation examples into 8 quantiles of the
  model's raw predictive entropy $H(p)=-\sum_k p_k\log p_k$, and fit a separate
  temperature $T_b$ per bin (same NLL objective). At test time, each example is
  assigned the temperature of the entropy bin it falls into. This lets the
  correction depend on how uncertain the model already is, testing whether
  overconfidence is uniform across the entropy spectrum.

**Evaluation.** All parameters (model and temperatures) are fit on
train/validation and evaluated on held-out **in-domain** and **shifted** test
sets (12k tokens each). We report top-1 accuracy, negative log-likelihood
(NLL), and **expected calibration error** (ECE) of the top-1 prediction with
15 equal-width confidence bins. NLL rewards the whole distribution; ECE
measures confidence-vs-accuracy agreement.

## 3. Results

The trained LM is strongly overconfident: raw ECE is **0.41** in-domain and
**0.48** under shift, with held-out NLL (4.55 / 5.56) far above the accuracy-
implied floor. Table 1 reports all methods. (Seed 0; single run. Full logs in
`results.json`, code in `experiment.py`, raw stdout in `run.log`.)

**Table 1. Calibration on held-out data (lower NLL/ECE better).**

| Split | Method | Acc | NLL | ECE |
|-------|--------|-----|-----|-----|
| In-domain | Raw | 0.122 | 4.549 | 0.413 |
| In-domain | Global TS | 0.122 | 2.913 | 0.024 |
| In-domain | ECATS (ours) | 0.122 | 2.913 | **0.023** |
| Shift | Raw | 0.051 | 5.557 | 0.480 |
| Shift | Global TS | 0.051 | **3.067** | **0.047** |
| Shift | ECATS (ours) | 0.051 | 3.067 | 0.048 |

**Finding 1 — TS is dramatic.** A single temperature ($T^\*=6.68$) collapses
in-domain ECE from 0.41 to 0.024 and NLL from 4.55 to 2.91, without touching
accuracy. This reproduces the classic result in our LM setting.

**Finding 2 — TS transfers across shift.** The *in-domain* temperature, applied
unchanged to the shifted source, cuts shift ECE from 0.48 to 0.047 and NLL from
5.56 to 3.07. The shift more than doubles the residual ECE (0.024 → 0.047), so
transfer is imperfect, but a single scalar fit on one distribution still removes
~90% of the calibration error on another. For a probabilistic-reliability
practitioner this is the reassuring, non-obvious message.

**Finding 3 — adaptivity barely helps (honest negative).** ECATS learns
temperatures that vary substantially across entropy bins — $T_b \in
[5.65, 8.67]$, i.e. a 2.6-point (1.5×) spread, with the *most-uncertain* bins
wanting the *largest* temperatures. Yet this fine structure yields only a
~0.001 ECE improvement in-domain and *no* improvement under shift (0.047 →
0.048, within noise). **Interpretation:** although per-bin optima differ, the
model's overconfidence is close to *multiplicatively uniform* — a single scalar
already captures almost all of the correctable miscalibration, and the extra
degrees of freedom in ECATS mostly fit validation noise that does not transfer.
This is a useful negative result: it explains *why* the literature finds
single-scalar TS so hard to beat, and it does so with the mechanism (per-entropy
temperatures) laid bare.

**Ablations built into the design.** (a) *Overfit regime*: an earlier,
well-generalized model (60k tokens, 3 epochs) was already near-calibrated (raw
ECE 0.03), leaving nothing for TS to fix — confirming that overconfidence is a
consequence of the low-data/long-training regime, not of the architecture.
(b) *Grid width*: widening the temperature search from $[0.5,3]$ to $[0.5,14]$
was necessary because the optimal $T\approx6.7$ lies far above the usual
$T\!\in\![1,3]$ range; under-wide grids silently clip and understate TS.

## 4. Conclusion

In a controlled, ground-truth, CPU-only testbed we isolated two questions about
temperature scaling for language-model calibration. First, an in-domain
temperature is strikingly **shift-robust**: fit on one Markov source, it still
removes ~90% of the calibration error on a different, higher-entropy source.
Second, making the temperature **adapt to the input's predictive entropy**
(ECATS) — despite learning genuinely different per-bin temperatures — gives only
marginal in-domain gains and none under shift, because the model's
overconfidence is nearly uniform in the multiplicative sense a single scalar
already corrects.

**Limitations.** Our LM and data are tiny and synthetic; results are a single
seed; ECE with 15 bins is a coarse estimator; and "shift" here is a
parameterized Markov change rather than genuine domain transfer. The negative
result for adaptivity may not hold for larger models with heterogeneous
overconfidence (e.g. real LLMs where rare tokens or specific domains are
disproportionately miscalibrated), and testing that is the natural next step —
along with token-value-conditioned rather than only entropy-conditioned
temperatures. Nonetheless, the controlled setting cleanly supports a practical
default: **for an overconfident LM, fit one temperature — it is cheap, it
transfers, and adaptivity is unlikely to be worth its complexity.**
