# A Single Temperature Goes a Long Way: How Well Does Post-hoc Temperature Scaling Calibrate an Overconfident Language Model Under Distribution Shift?

## Abstract

Neural language models (LMs) produce a probability distribution over the next
token, but when trained to low loss on limited data they become badly
*overconfident*: the probability they assign to their top prediction far exceeds
the frequency with which it is correct. Post-hoc **temperature scaling** (TS) —
dividing the logits by a single learned scalar $T$ — is the standard remedy, but
two questions matter for probabilistic reliability and are under-examined in a
controlled setting: (i) does a temperature fit in-domain still calibrate the
model when the *input distribution shifts*, and (ii) is a single global scalar
enough, or should the temperature adapt to how uncertain the model already is on
each input? We build a fully self-contained, CPU-only testbed: a small
character-level LSTM trained on synthetic order-2 Markov text, deliberately
overfit into the overconfident regime, and evaluated on in-domain and *shifted*
Markov sources. We compare raw softmax, global TS, and **entropy-conditioned
adaptive temperature scaling (ECATS)** — a diagnostic that fits a separate
temperature per predictive-entropy quantile, used not as a proposed recalibrator
but as an *instrument* to test whether overconfidence is entropy-uniform. Across
**5 seeds**, global TS cuts expected calibration error (ECE) from **0.43 to
0.015** in-domain, and most of that benefit *transfers* under shift (0.48 →
0.046). ECATS learns temperatures varying **1.5×** across entropy bins, yet
changes ECE by only ~0.001 — a small but *statistically significant* (paired
t-test *and* non-parametric paired bootstrap) in-domain gain and an equally small
significant *loss* under shift. A shift-magnitude sweep shows TS transfer is
nearly *invariant* to shift magnitude; a **four-point skill sweep** at matched
shift ratio shows transfer degrades **monotonically** with the model's in-domain
skill (shift-ECE reduction falls 90→57% as accuracy climbs 11→35%). The practical
message: for a uniformly-overconfident LM, a single temperature is a strong,
shift-robust baseline, and input-adaptive temperature buys almost nothing.

## 1. Introduction

Large language models generate text autoregressively by sampling from a softmax
over next-token logits. The *quality of those probabilities* — not just top-1
accuracy — governs downstream behavior: sampling temperature, beam search,
abstention, and hallucination detection all consume the model's probability
estimates. A model is **calibrated** if, among predictions made with confidence
$c$, a fraction $\approx c$ are correct. Modern neural networks, and LMs in
particular, are typically *overconfident* after training [Guo et al., 2017],
corrupting every downstream use of the probabilities.

The dominant fix is **temperature scaling** [Guo et al., 2017]: learn one scalar
$T$ on held-out data and replace $\mathrm{softmax}(z)$ with
$\mathrm{softmax}(z/T)$. It preserves the argmax (accuracy is unchanged), is
cheap, and the recent LLM-calibration literature repeatedly finds it hard to beat
[ICLR 2025; OpenReview 2025]. Yet two assumptions in "just scale by one number"
deserve scrutiny in a controlled setting:

1. **Shift robustness.** $T$ is fit in-domain. Deployments face inputs from a
   different distribution. Does the in-domain $T$ still calibrate the model?
2. **Uniformity of overconfidence.** A single $T$ assumes the model is
   overconfident *by the same factor everywhere*. If it is more overconfident on
   high-entropy contexts, a temperature that *adapts to the input's entropy*
   should help.

Our contribution is empirical and deliberately modest: a controlled measurement
with an honest negative finding for adaptivity and a positive finding for the
shift-robustness of plain TS — with multi-seed variance, paired significance
tests, a shift-magnitude sweep, and a skill sweep. The central *finding* is that
overconfidence here is nearly multiplicatively uniform; ECATS is the diagnostic
that surfaces it, not a method we advocate.

### Related Work

Calibration of neural classifiers was popularized by Guo et al. [2017], who
showed temperature scaling — a one-parameter special case of Platt scaling —
restores calibration better than isotonic regression or histogram binning while
preserving accuracy. For LLMs, a 2025 survey [arXiv 2503.15850] catalogues
logit-based, verbalized, and sampling-based confidence measures. TS remains
central: ICLR-2025 work casts *semantic* calibration through temperature scaling;
OpenReview and MICCAI 2025 studies confirm TS "consistently improves calibration,
outperforming more expressive token-level recalibration methods." A parallel line
studies *verbalized* probabilities [arXiv 2410.06707; 2512.11998]. Bayesian
approaches (MC-dropout, BLoB) estimate epistemic uncertainty but are heavy.
Recent benchmarks [NeurIPS 2025] evaluate 80 models and emphasize decoding
stochasticity and input ambiguity as LM-specific uncertainty sources.

**Where ECATS sits.** Prior *conditional* TS variants partition by a discrete
label — class-conditional TS uses the predicted class; token-level TS [OpenReview
2025] fits per-token corrections over the vocabulary. ECATS conditions on a
continuous instance-level axis, the model's own **predictive entropy**, binned
into quantiles. It is orthogonal to vocabulary-partitioned schemes and
operationalizes assumption (2) — that overconfidence may scale with uncertainty.
Missing from prior work is a *controlled* study isolating shift and
input-adaptivity with ground-truth data and a single trained model; we fill that
gap.

## 2. Method

**Testbed.** We generate text from an order-2 Markov source over $V=20$ symbols.
Transition distributions are random softmaxes over per-context logits, with a
source "sharpness" $\tau$ controlling entropy (smaller $\tau$ = sharper). Shift =
re-drawing the transition logits and (optionally) changing $\tau$. A known
generative process gives clean, reproducible data and a tunable shift.

**Model.** 48-d embedding → single-layer LSTM (128 units) → linear head, reading
8 tokens to predict the next. We train with Adam (lr $3\times10^{-3}$) for 18
epochs on only **15k** tokens. The small data and many epochs deliberately push
the model into the *overconfident* regime (train NLL far below held-out NLL).

**Calibration methods.** *(0) Raw*: $T=1$. *(1) Global TS*: one scalar $T^\*$
minimizing validation NLL, grid over $[0.5,14]$ with local refinement, applied
unchanged everywhere. *(2) ECATS*: bin validation into 8 quantiles of raw
predictive entropy $H(p)=-\sum_k p_k\log p_k$ and fit a separate $T_b$ per bin;
at test time each example uses its entropy bin's temperature.

**Evaluation.** All parameters fit on train/validation; evaluated on held-out
**in-domain** and **shifted** test sets (12k tokens each). We report top-1
accuracy, NLL, and **ECE** of the top-1 prediction with 15 equal-width bins.
Noise is estimated over **5 seeds** (independent source draws, data, init) as
mean ± SD, with per-split 95% bootstrap CIs (300 resamples). For the ECATS-minus-
global ECE difference we run **paired t-tests** across seeds *and*, because $n=5$
makes any single test fragile, corroborate with a **non-parametric paired
bootstrap** (10k resamples of the 5 seed-differences) that assumes no normality.

## 3. Results

The trained LM is strongly overconfident: raw ECE **0.43 ± 0.006** in-domain,
**0.48 ± 0.002** under shift. Table 1 averages over 5 seeds.

**Table 1. Calibration on held-out data, mean ± SD over 5 seeds (lower better).**

| Split | Method | ECE |
|-------|--------|-----|
| In-domain | Raw | 0.427 ± 0.006 |
| In-domain | Global TS | 0.0145 ± 0.0036 |
| In-domain | ECATS | **0.0136 ± 0.0036** |
| Shift | Raw | 0.485 ± 0.002 |
| Shift | Global TS | **0.0462 ± 0.0021** |
| Shift | ECATS | 0.0470 ± 0.0023 |

**Finding 1 — TS is dramatic.** A single temperature ($T^\*=6.9 \pm 0.3$)
collapses in-domain ECE from 0.43 to 0.015 without touching accuracy, reproducing
the classic result in our LM setting.

**Finding 2 — TS transfers across shift.** The *in-domain* temperature, applied
unchanged to the shifted source, cuts shift ECE from 0.48 to 0.046 — a single
scalar fit on one distribution removes ~90% of the calibration error on another.
Transfer is imperfect (residual ECE triples, 0.015 → 0.046) and *conditional* —
see Finding 4.

**Finding 3 — adaptivity is significant but negligible (honest negative).** ECATS
learns per-seed $T_b$ ranges of roughly $[5.6, 8.1]$ — a **2.6-point additive
spread (~1.5× ratio)**, with the *most-uncertain* bins wanting the *largest*
temperatures. Yet the ECE effect is tiny and *sign-consistent across all 5
seeds*. A **paired t-test** finds ECATS improves in-domain ECE by $0.00087 \pm
0.00027$ ($t=-6.4$, $p=0.003$) and *degrades* shift ECE by $0.00077 \pm 0.00022$
($t=+6.9$, $p=0.002$). Because the t-test assumes normality of only 5 paired
differences, we corroborate with a **non-parametric paired bootstrap**: the 95%
CI of the mean difference *excludes zero* for both in-domain ($[-0.0011,
-0.0006]$) and shift ($[+0.0006, +0.0010]$), confirming the effect without any
distributional assumption. (The underpowered $n=5$ sign test can only reach
$p=0.0625$, so we do *not* lean on it.) Both effects are two orders of magnitude
below the raw miscalibration and well inside the bootstrap CIs of the individual
ECE values. **Interpretation:** although per-bin optima genuinely differ, the
model's overconfidence is close to *multiplicatively uniform* — a single scalar
captures nearly all correctable miscalibration, and ECATS's extra degrees of
freedom fit a whisper of validation noise that flips to a (small, significant)
loss under shift. This explains *why* single-scalar TS is so hard to beat.

**Finding 4 — what degrades transfer: skill, not shift size.** The paper's
earlier framing attributed weaker transfer to a "larger shift"; two controlled
sweeps overturn the naive reading.

**(a) Magnitude sweep.** Fixing the base source ($\tau_{\text{in}}=1.0$) and
varying the shifted source over $\tau_{\text{out}}\in\{1.0,1.15,1.3,1.6,2.2,3.2\}$
(3 seeds each), the global-TS shift-ECE reduction is essentially *flat at
90.1–90.4%* — transfer is **invariant to shift magnitude** within a base regime
(shift accuracy stays at the ~5% chance floor throughout).

**(b) Skill sweep (new).** To test the skill hypothesis directly — rather than
from just two endpoints — we sweep the base sharpness $\tau_{\text{in}}\in\{0.45,
0.6, 0.8, 1.0\}$ while holding the **shift *ratio* fixed** at
$\tau_{\text{out}}=1.56\,\tau_{\text{in}}$ (3 seeds each), so the distributional
gap is matched and only model skill varies. Transfer degrades **monotonically**
with skill:

| $\tau_{\text{in}}$ | In-dom. acc | $T^\*$ | Shift-ECE reduction |
|--------------------|-------------|--------|---------------------|
| 1.00 | 11% (2× chance) | 6.7 | **90.5%** |
| 0.80 | 15% | 4.7 | 84.1% |
| 0.60 | 24% | 3.1 | 71.3% |
| 0.45 | 35% (7× chance) | 2.3 | **56.6%** |

As in-domain accuracy climbs 11→35% at *constant* shift ratio, $T^\*$ falls
6.7→2.3 (the model gets sharper) and shift-transfer reduction falls monotonically
90.5→56.6%. **So the driver is not the size of the distributional gap but the
model's in-domain skill/confidence regime:** sharper, more skilled overconfidence
transfers less faithfully. This corrects Finding 2's implied "graceful
degradation with gap size" and answers whether the effect is monotonic — it is,
across four evenly-spaced skill levels. All three headline findings survive in the
high-skill regime (35% accuracy: raw ECE 0.29, global TS 0.028 ± 0.003, ECATS
0.026 ± 0.003; paired gain $-0.0020$, *not* significant by either t-test $p=0.11$
or the paired bootstrap whose CI includes zero), so "multiplicatively-uniform
overconfidence" is not an artifact of near-chance outputs, and ECATS again adds
nothing.

**Ablations built in.** (a) A well-generalized model (60k tokens, 3 epochs) was
already near-calibrated (raw ECE 0.03), leaving nothing for TS to fix —
overconfidence is a consequence of the low-data/long-training regime, not the
architecture. (b) Widening the grid from $[0.5,3]$ to $[0.5,14]$ was necessary:
the optimum $T\approx6.9$ lies far above the usual $[1,3]$ range; under-wide grids
silently clip and understate TS.

## 4. Conclusion

In a controlled, ground-truth, CPU-only testbed we isolated two questions about
TS for LM calibration, with 5-seed variance, paired significance tests (t-test
*and* non-parametric bootstrap), a shift-magnitude sweep, and a four-point skill
sweep. First, an in-domain temperature is **shift-robust** (removing ~90% of
calibration error), and — counter to a naive reading — this robustness is
*invariant to shift magnitude*; what erodes it is the model's own in-domain
skill, *monotonically*: at matched shift ratio, reduction falls from 90% for a
near-chance model to 57% for a sharply-peaked high-skill (35% accuracy) one.
Second, making the temperature **adapt to predictive entropy** changes ECE by
only ~0.001 — a paired-significant but negligible in-domain gain and an equally
negligible shift-time loss — because overconfidence is nearly multiplicatively
uniform. These conclusions hold across seeds and even when the model has real
predictive skill.

**Limitations.** Our LM and data are tiny and synthetic; five seeds bound but do
not eliminate variance (the paired t-test rests on a normality assumption for 5
differences, which is why we corroborate with a distribution-free bootstrap); ECE
with 15 bins is a coarse estimator; and "shift" is a parameterized Markov change,
not genuine domain transfer, so the practical guidance in the abstract is an
*extrapolation* to real LLMs, not a demonstration on them. The negative result
for adaptivity may not hold for larger models with *heterogeneous* overconfidence
(e.g. rare tokens or specific domains disproportionately miscalibrated); the
natural next step is real corpora and token-value- rather than only
entropy-conditioned temperatures. Within these bounds the setting cleanly
supports a practical default: **for an overconfident LM, fit one temperature — it
is cheap, it transfers robustly across shift magnitude, and adaptivity is
unlikely to be worth its complexity.**
