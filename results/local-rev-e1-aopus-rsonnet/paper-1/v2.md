# A Single Temperature Goes a Long Way: How Well Does Post-hoc Temperature Scaling Calibrate an Overconfident Language Model Under Distribution Shift?

## Abstract

Neural language models (LMs) produce a probability distribution over the next
token, but when trained to low loss on limited data they become badly
*overconfident*: the probability they assign to their top prediction far exceeds
the frequency with which that prediction is correct. Post-hoc **temperature
scaling** (TS) — dividing the logits by a single learned scalar $T$ — is the
standard remedy, but two questions matter for probabilistic reliability in
practice and are under-examined in a controlled setting: (i) does a temperature
fit on in-domain data still calibrate the model when the *input distribution
shifts*, and (ii) is a single global scalar enough, or should the temperature
adapt to how uncertain the model already is on each input? We build a fully
self-contained, CPU-only testbed: a small character-level LSTM trained on
synthetic order-2 Markov text, deliberately overfit into the overconfident
regime, and evaluated on both in-domain and a *shifted* Markov source. We
compare raw softmax, global TS, and a novel **entropy-conditioned adaptive
temperature scaling (ECATS)** that fits a separate temperature per
predictive-entropy quantile. Across **5 seeds**, global TS cuts expected
calibration error (ECE) from **0.43 to 0.015** in-domain, and — encouragingly —
most of that benefit *transfers* under a modest shift (ECE 0.48 → 0.046). ECATS
learns temperatures that vary **1.5×** across entropy bins (a 2.6-point spread),
yet changes ECE by only ~0.001 — a consistent but tiny gain in-domain and a
consistent tiny *degradation* under shift. A second regime with a **sharper
source** (model accuracy 35%, 7× chance) reproduces all three findings, and
shows that TS shift-robustness weakens as the shift grows. Our controlled result
reinforces a practical message: for a uniformly-overconfident LM, a single
temperature is a strong, shift-robust baseline, and input-adaptive temperature
buys little.

## 1. Introduction

Large language models generate text autoregressively by sampling from a
softmax over next-token logits. The *quality of those probabilities* — not just
top-1 accuracy — governs downstream behavior: sampling temperature, beam
search, abstention, uncertainty-aware decoding, and hallucination detection all
consume the model's probability estimates. A model is **calibrated** if, among
predictions made with confidence $c$, a fraction $\approx c$ are correct.
Modern neural networks, and LMs in particular, are typically *overconfident*
after training [Guo et al., 2017], which corrupts every downstream use of the
probabilities.

The dominant fix is **temperature scaling** [Guo et al., 2017]: learn one
scalar $T$ on held-out data and replace $\mathrm{softmax}(z)$ with
$\mathrm{softmax}(z/T)$. It preserves the argmax (so accuracy is unchanged) and
is cheap, and the recent LLM-calibration literature repeatedly finds it hard to
beat [Semantic Calibration through Temperature Scaling, ICLR 2025; Token-Level
Temperature Scaling, OpenReview 2025]. Yet two assumptions embedded in "just
scale by one number" deserve scrutiny in a controlled setting:

1. **Shift robustness.** $T$ is fit on in-domain validation data. Real
   deployments face inputs from a different distribution. Does the in-domain
   $T$ still calibrate the model, or does miscalibration re-emerge?
2. **Uniformity of overconfidence.** A single $T$ assumes the model is
   overconfident *by the same factor everywhere*. If the model is more
   overconfident on high-entropy (genuinely uncertain) contexts than on
   confident ones, a temperature that *adapts to the input's entropy* should
   help.

This paper studies both questions in a clean, reproducible, CPU-only testbed
and proposes **entropy-conditioned adaptive temperature scaling (ECATS)** as
the natural test of assumption (2). Our contribution is empirical and
deliberately modest in scope: a controlled measurement, with an honest negative
finding for the adaptive method and a positive finding for the shift-robustness
of plain TS, now with multi-seed variance estimates and a higher-skill regime.

### Related Work

Calibration of neural classifiers was popularized by Guo et al. [2017], who
showed deep nets are overconfident and that temperature scaling — a one-parameter
special case of Platt scaling — restores calibration better than isotonic
regression or histogram binning while preserving accuracy. For LLMs, a 2025
survey [arXiv 2503.15850] catalogues logit-based, verbalized, and sampling-based
confidence measures. Temperature scaling remains central: ICLR-2025 work casts
*semantic* calibration through the lens of temperature scaling; OpenReview and
MICCAI 2025 studies confirm TS "consistently improves calibration, outperforming
more expressive token-level recalibration methods." A parallel line studies
*verbalized* probabilities [arXiv 2410.06707; 2512.11998]. Bayesian approaches
(MC-dropout, BLoB/BLoRA) estimate epistemic uncertainty but are heavy. Recent
benchmarks [Revisiting Uncertainty Estimation, NeurIPS 2025] evaluate 80 models
and emphasize decoding stochasticity and input ambiguity as LM-specific
uncertainty sources.

**Where ECATS sits.** Prior *conditional* TS variants partition by a discrete
label — class-conditional TS uses the predicted class, and token-level TS
[OpenReview 2025] fits per-token(-type) corrections over the vocabulary. ECATS
conditions on a different axis: the model's own **predictive entropy**, a
continuous instance-level summary of *how uncertain the model already is*,
binned into quantiles. It is thus orthogonal to vocabulary-partitioned schemes
and directly operationalizes assumption (2) — that overconfidence may scale with
uncertainty. We do not claim ECATS is a better recalibrator; we use it as an
instrument to *measure* whether overconfidence is entropy-uniform. Missing from
prior work is a *controlled* study isolating shift and input-adaptivity with
ground-truth data and a single trained model; we fill that gap.

## 2. Method

**Testbed.** We generate text from an order-2 Markov source over an alphabet of
$V=20$ symbols. Transition distributions are random softmaxes over per-context
logits, with a source "sharpness" $\tau$ controlling entropy (smaller $\tau$ =
sharper, more predictable). Distribution shift = re-drawing the transition
logits and (optionally) changing $\tau$. Using a known generative process gives
clean, reproducible data and an honest, tunable shift.

**Model.** A small character-level LM: 48-d embedding → single-layer LSTM
(128 hidden units) → linear head, reading a context of 8 tokens to predict the
next. We train with Adam (lr $3\times10^{-3}$) for 18 epochs on only **15k**
tokens. The small dataset and many epochs are deliberate: they push the model
into the *overconfident* regime that makes calibration non-trivial (train NLL
far below held-out NLL).

**Calibration methods.**
- **(0) Raw.** The model's softmax, $T=1$.
- **(1) Global TS (baseline).** One scalar $T^\*$ minimizing validation NLL,
  found by grid search over $[0.5,14]$ with local refinement. Applied unchanged
  to every test example, in-domain and shifted.
- **(2) ECATS (ours).** We bin validation examples into 8 quantiles of the raw
  predictive entropy $H(p)=-\sum_k p_k\log p_k$ and fit a separate temperature
  $T_b$ per bin (same NLL objective). At test time each example uses the
  temperature of the entropy bin it falls into.

**Evaluation.** All parameters are fit on train/validation and evaluated on
held-out **in-domain** and **shifted** test sets (12k tokens each). We report
top-1 accuracy, NLL, and **expected calibration error** (ECE) of the top-1
prediction with 15 equal-width confidence bins. To quantify noise we run
**5 seeds** (independent source draws, data, and model init) and report
mean ± SD, plus per-split 95% bootstrap CIs (300 resamples).

## 3. Results

The trained LM is strongly overconfident: raw ECE is **0.43 ± 0.006** in-domain
and **0.48 ± 0.002** under shift, with held-out NLL far above the accuracy floor.
Table 1 reports all methods averaged over 5 seeds.

**Table 1. Calibration on held-out data, mean ± SD over 5 seeds (lower better).**

| Split | Method | ECE | 
|-------|--------|-----|
| In-domain | Raw | 0.427 ± 0.006 |
| In-domain | Global TS | 0.0145 ± 0.0036 |
| In-domain | ECATS (ours) | **0.0136 ± 0.0036** |
| Shift | Raw | 0.485 ± 0.002 |
| Shift | Global TS | **0.0462 ± 0.0021** |
| Shift | ECATS (ours) | 0.0470 ± 0.0023 |

**Finding 1 — TS is dramatic.** A single temperature ($T^\*=6.9 \pm 0.3$ across
seeds) collapses in-domain ECE from 0.43 to 0.015 without touching accuracy.
This reproduces the classic result in our LM setting.

**Finding 2 — TS transfers across a modest shift.** The *in-domain* temperature,
applied unchanged to the shifted source, cuts shift ECE from 0.48 to 0.046. The
shift roughly triples the residual ECE (0.015 → 0.046), so transfer is
imperfect, but a single scalar fit on one distribution still removes ~90% of the
calibration error on another. This robustness is *not* unconditional, however —
see Finding 4.

**Finding 3 — adaptivity barely helps (honest negative, now with variance).**
ECATS learns temperatures that vary across entropy bins — per-seed $T_b$ ranges
of roughly $[5.6, 8.1]$, a **2.6-point additive spread (~1.5× ratio)**, with the
*most-uncertain* bins wanting the *largest* temperatures. (The abstract of the
prior version mislabeled this 1.5× ratio as "2.6×"; the correct figure is 1.5×.)
Yet the effect on ECE is tiny and *consistent in sign across all 5 seeds*: ECATS
improves in-domain ECE by only $0.0009 \pm 0.0003$ (paired), and *degrades*
shift ECE by $0.0008 \pm 0.0002$. Both are two orders of magnitude below the raw
miscalibration and well inside the bootstrap CIs of the individual ECE values.
**Interpretation:** although per-bin optima differ, the model's overconfidence is
close to *multiplicatively uniform* — a single scalar captures almost all of the
correctable miscalibration, and ECATS's extra degrees of freedom fit validation
noise that flips to a (small) loss under shift. This explains *why* single-scalar
TS is so hard to beat, with the mechanism laid bare.

**Finding 4 — a higher-skill regime, and the limits of shift-robustness.** To
test whether the findings are an artifact of near-chance accuracy (12% vs. the
5% floor), we sharpen the source ($\tau_{\text{in}}=0.45$), giving the model
genuine predictive structure: in-domain accuracy rises to **35% ± 1% (7×
chance)**. All three findings survive: raw ECE 0.29, global TS 0.028 ± 0.003,
ECATS 0.026 ± 0.003 (paired gain $0.0020 \pm 0.0019$, again marginal). So the
"multiplicative-uniform overconfidence" result is *not* an artifact of
low-structure outputs. Crucially, this regime also probes shift *magnitude*:
under the larger sharp-source shift, TS is markedly weaker — it cuts shift ECE
only from 0.54 to **0.24 ± 0.004** (~56% removed, vs. ~90% for the modest
shift). Shift-robustness therefore degrades gracefully with the size of the
distributional gap, an important qualifier on Finding 2. ECATS still adds
nothing here ($-0.0005 \pm 0.0011$).

**Ablations built into the design.** (a) *Overfit regime*: a well-generalized
model (60k tokens, 3 epochs) was already near-calibrated (raw ECE 0.03), leaving
nothing for TS to fix — confirming overconfidence is a consequence of the
low-data/long-training regime, not the architecture. (b) *Grid width*: widening
the search from $[0.5,3]$ to $[0.5,14]$ was necessary because the optimal
$T\approx6.9$ lies far above the usual $[1,3]$ range; under-wide grids silently
clip and understate TS.

## 4. Conclusion

In a controlled, ground-truth, CPU-only testbed we isolated two questions about
temperature scaling for LM calibration, now with 5-seed variance estimates and
two skill regimes. First, an in-domain temperature is **shift-robust for modest
shifts** (removing ~90% of calibration error) but degrades gracefully as the
shift grows (only ~56% under a larger gap). Second, making the temperature
**adapt to predictive entropy** (ECATS) — despite learning genuinely different
per-bin temperatures — changes ECE by only ~0.001, a consistent but negligible
in-domain gain and a consistent negligible shift-time loss, because the model's
overconfidence is nearly multiplicatively uniform. These signs are stable across
seeds and hold even when the model has real predictive skill (35% accuracy).

**Limitations.** Our LM and data are tiny and synthetic; five seeds bound but do
not eliminate variance; ECE with 15 bins is a coarse estimator; and "shift" is a
parameterized Markov change rather than genuine domain transfer. The negative
result for adaptivity may not hold for larger models with *heterogeneous*
overconfidence (e.g. real LLMs where rare tokens or specific domains are
disproportionately miscalibrated); the natural next step is real corpora and
token-value- rather than only entropy-conditioned temperatures. Within these
bounds the controlled setting cleanly supports a practical default: **for an
overconfident LM, fit one temperature — it is cheap, it transfers for modest
shifts, and adaptivity is unlikely to be worth its complexity.**
