# When Does Censored Feedback Trap a Group? Feature Informativeness as the Governing Lever in Algorithmic Allocation

## Abstract

Algorithmic allocators (loans, patrols, screening) typically observe an outcome label *only for the people they act on*. This "selective labels" structure creates a feedback loop: a model that under-scores a group approves fewer of its members, observes fewer of their labels, and can stay wrong indefinitely. Much socio-technical work treats such traps as an intrinsic hazard of feedback loops. We ask a sharper, more actionable question: *when* does the trap actually bite, and *what governs its severity*? Using a controlled lending simulation in which two demographic groups are **identical in ground truth** — so every disparity that emerges is a pure loop artifact — we find that the loop is trapping only when the individual-level feature is weakly informative, forcing the model to lean on group identity. We show that **feature informativeness is a smooth, monotone governing lever**: as feature noise rises, the persistent approval-rate gap of a greedy allocator grows from 0.04 to 0.26 and its group-B calibration error quadruples. Against baselines (ε-greedy, UCB-style optimism, demographic-parity constraint) we find that (i) targeted exploration (UCB) Pareto-dominates random exploration (ε-greedy), buying fairness at *no* utility cost; (ii) a parity constraint erases the visible gap but leaves the underlying miscalibration intact; and (iii) counterintuitively, adding *more* seed data from the biased region worsens the trap — representativeness, not quantity, is what matters. Code and raw logs accompany the paper.

## 1. Introduction

A defining socio-technical property of deployed decision systems is that they generate the very data they later learn from. A lender records repayment only for approved applicants; predictive policing observes crime only where patrols are sent. This *selective-labels* / censored-feedback structure has been repeatedly identified as a driver of runaway disparity: the system's past decisions bias its future training data, and disparities can compound over time [1,2,4].

Most of this literature establishes *that* feedback loops can amplify bias and argues, correctly, that purely technical fixes are insufficient [1,3]. Less attention is paid to a question a practitioner actually needs answered: **under what conditions does the loop trap a group, and which design knob most controls the damage?** A blanket "feedback loops are dangerous" gives no guidance on where to spend a limited fairness budget.

This paper isolates one answer empirically. We build a minimal lending simulation with a deliberately strong null: the two groups are statistically identical in ground truth and in feature distribution. Any group gap that appears is therefore *manufactured entirely by the learning loop*, not by the world. Our central finding is that the trap is not automatic. When the model has a genuinely predictive *individual* feature, it rescues high-quality members of the disadvantaged group and the initial disparity decays on its own. The trap emerges only as the individual signal weakens and the model is pushed to rely on group identity as a proxy. Feature informativeness thus acts as a continuous dial on trap severity — a lever that is often more addressable in practice (better features, richer applications, human review of borderline cases) than the loop structure itself.

### Related Work

**Feedback loops and disparity amplification.** Ensign et al. and follow-on simulation studies of predictive policing show patrol-allocation loops amplify racial disparities via biased observation [2,7]. Recent work on training generative models on their own outputs ("model collapse" / fairness feedback loops) shows chains of self-training amplify representational disparity [4]. Our contribution is complementary: rather than demonstrating amplification, we characterize the *regime boundary* — the conditions separating self-correcting from self-trapping loops.

**Selective labels.** Lakkaraju et al. formalized the selective-labels problem for evaluating decision policies under censored outcomes. We use the same censoring structure as the *mechanism* of harm and study interventions on it.

**Exploration for long-term fairness.** A recurring proposal is that uncertainty-aware exploration, exposure allocation, or calibration can prevent disparity amplification [1]. We test two concrete instances (ε-greedy and a UCB-style optimism bonus) head-to-head and quantify their fairness–utility trade-offs.

**Sociotechnical framing.** A parallel critical literature argues fairness metrics can entrench power and that stakeholder-driven, non-technical remedies are essential [3,5]. Our results are consistent with the spirit of that critique: our parity-constrained baseline shows a purely metric-satisfying fix can mask, rather than cure, an epistemic deficit about a group.

## 2. Method

**World.** Applicants arrive in rounds of 400. Each has a scalar quality `x ~ N(0,1)` and group `g ∈ {A,B}` (50/50). The true repayment probability is `p(x) = σ(1.6x)`, **identical for both groups**. If approved, the applicant repays (label 1) with probability `p(x)`; utility is `+1` for a repaid loan and `−1` for a default (0 if not approved).

**Model.** A group-aware logistic regression on features `[1, x_obs, g, x_obs·g]`, fit by gradient descent (numpy only). The design lets the model represent per-group intercepts and slopes — so it *can* recover the (identical) truth, but must estimate each group's parameters from that group's observed labels, making it vulnerable to censoring. Crucially, the model observes a **noisy** feature `x_obs = x + N(0, σ_noise)`; ground-truth outcomes still depend on the clean `x`. `σ_noise` controls how much genuine individual signal is available versus how much the model must fall back on group identity.

**Censored feedback.** Labels are appended to the training set **only for approved applicants**; the model retrains every round (40 rounds).

**Seed bias.** Group A's seed labels span all `x`; group B's few seed labels are drawn only from a restricted low-`x` slice (`x < −0.3`) — a plausible historical artifact ("B was only ever approved when very safe"). This is the initial perturbation from which any trap must grow or decay.

**Allocators (baselines + interventions).**
- **greedy**: approve iff predicted `p ≥ τ=0.5` (myopic; no exploration).
- **eps_explore**: greedy, but with probability `ε` approve a uniformly random applicant (untargeted exploration).
- **ucb**: approve iff `pred + β·u ≥ τ`, where `u = 1/√(n_g+1)` grows for the group with fewer observed labels (targeted, uncertainty-directed exploration).
- **dp_parity**: enforce equal approval *rate* across groups each round (demographic parity by construction).

**Metrics** (mean over 8 seeds; "final window" = mean of last 10 rounds). *rate_gap*: |approval-rate A − approval-rate B| (allocative disparity, should be 0 since groups are identical). *tpr_gap*: gap in approval rate among the truly-creditworthy (`p≥τ`) — an equal-opportunity measure. *qual_appr*: fraction of truly-creditworthy applicants approved (allocative efficiency for deserving applicants). *util*: mean per-applicant net return. *calibErr_B*: |mean predicted − mean true| for group B.

## 3. Results

**The trap is not automatic; a strong feature self-corrects.** With an informative feature (`σ_noise=0.5`) the greedy allocator's approval-rate gap settles at only 0.044 despite the biased seed — the individual signal lets it approve high-`x` B applicants, whose labels correct the model. The manufactured disparity decays on its own.

**Feature informativeness is a smooth governing lever.** Sweeping `σ_noise` for greedy (Table 1) shows every harm metric worsening monotonically as the individual signal degrades and the model leans on group identity:

*Table 1 — greedy, final window vs feature noise (8 seeds).*

| σ_noise | rate_gap | tpr_gap | qual_appr | calibErr_B | util |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.5 | 0.044 | 0.054 | 0.839 | 0.021 | 0.219 |
| 1.0 | 0.047 | 0.069 | 0.730 | 0.022 | 0.169 |
| 2.0 | 0.084 | 0.105 | 0.602 | 0.030 | 0.102 |
| 3.0 | 0.142 | 0.170 | 0.509 | 0.033 | 0.068 |
| 4.0 | 0.262 | 0.293 | 0.331 | 0.080 | 0.029 |

The persistent approval-rate gap grows ~6× (0.044→0.262) and B-calibration error ~4× as the feature becomes uninformative. This is the paper's core claim: *the loop traps a group to the extent the model must proxy through group identity.*

**Dynamics.** In the trap regime (`σ_noise=2.0`) the initial gap is large (~0.44 at round 0, from the biased seed) and decays over rounds, but *slowly and unreliably* for greedy: the last-10 mean is 0.084 with a large across-seed s.d. of 0.071 — some seeds stay stuck. Exploration accelerates and stabilizes recovery: ε-greedy reaches 0.048 (s.d. 0.016) and UCB 0.060 (s.d. 0.031). The trajectory (rate_gap at rounds 0/5/10/20/39) is greedy `0.45/0.30/0.22/0.14/0.06` vs ε-greedy `0.39/0.17/0.07/0.04/0.07` — exploration closes the gap far faster.

**Targeted exploration Pareto-dominates random exploration** (trap regime, Table 2). Increasing the UCB bonus `β` improves qualified-approval from 0.60 to 0.71 and shrinks both gaps **while utility stays flat at 0.10** — because the exploration is directed at the under-sampled group. Raising ε in ε-greedy also shrinks gaps but *steadily destroys utility* (0.103→0.065 as ε goes 0→0.4), since it wastes approvals on random low-quality applicants of both groups.

*Table 2 — intervention strength trade-offs (trap regime, final window).*

| method | knob | rate_gap | tpr_gap | qual_appr | util |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ε-greedy | ε=0.0 | 0.056 | 0.071 | 0.604 | 0.103 |
| ε-greedy | ε=0.2 | 0.045 | 0.058 | 0.606 | 0.085 |
| ε-greedy | ε=0.4 | 0.038 | 0.058 | 0.585 | 0.065 |
| UCB | β=0.0 | 0.084 | 0.105 | 0.602 | 0.102 |
| UCB | β=1.2 | 0.063 | 0.078 | 0.677 | 0.103 |
| UCB | β=2.0 | 0.051 | 0.067 | 0.713 | 0.102 |

**A parity constraint hides the trap without curing it.** `dp_parity` drives the approval-rate gap to ~0.002 by construction and even improves tpr_gap (0.045 vs greedy 0.105), at essentially no utility cost (0.101). But its group-B calibration error (0.024) remains as high as greedy's — the model is *still* epistemically wrong about B; the constraint merely overrides its scores. The deficit would resurface the moment the constraint is relaxed or applied to a new sub-population. This mirrors the sociotechnical critique that metric-satisfying fixes can mask rather than remedy underlying harms [3,5].

**Representativeness beats quantity (counterintuitive).** Sweeping the group-B seed fraction (trap regime) shows *more* B seed data yielding a *larger* final gap: seed_B_frac 0.5 → rate_gap 0.116, calibErr_B 0.064, versus 0.05 → 0.057, 0.022. Because the B seed is region-restricted (only low-`x`, low-repayment), piling on more of it makes the model *more confident that B is bad*. The fix for a censored-feedback trap is representative exploration, not raw data volume.

## 4. Conclusion

Feedback loops in algorithmic allocation are widely flagged as bias amplifiers, but our controlled study — with groups identical in ground truth, so every gap is a pure artifact — shows the trap is conditional, not automatic, and that its severity is governed by a single interpretable lever: **feature informativeness**. When the model can rely on genuine individual signal it self-corrects; when it must proxy through group identity the loop traps the disadvantaged group, monotonically worse as signal degrades. Among interventions, uncertainty-directed exploration (UCB) is a genuine free lunch relative to random exploration, improving fairness at no utility cost; a demographic-parity constraint cosmetically closes the gap while leaving the model's beliefs about the group uncorrected; and adding more data from a biased region actively backfires.

**Limitations.** This is a toy simulation (scalar feature, logistic model, 40 rounds, two groups, identical ground truth). We deliberately assumed identical groups to isolate the loop; real settings add genuine base-rate differences, strategic applicants, and label noise. The uncertainty proxy is a per-group count, not a calibrated posterior. These are the natural next steps; the qualitative levers (informativeness governs trap severity; targeted > random exploration; parity ≠ calibration) are the transferable claims. Full code (`sim.py`) and raw logs (`run_output.txt`) accompany this paper for inspection and re-running.
