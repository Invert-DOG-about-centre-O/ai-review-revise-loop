# When Does Censored Feedback Trap a Group? Feature Informativeness as the Governing Lever in Algorithmic Allocation

## Abstract

Algorithmic allocators (loans, patrols, screening) typically observe an outcome label *only for the people they act on*. This "selective labels" structure creates a feedback loop: a model that under-scores a group approves fewer of its members, observes fewer of their labels, and can stay wrong indefinitely. Much socio-technical work treats such traps as an intrinsic hazard of feedback loops. We ask a sharper, more actionable question: *when* does the trap actually bite, and *what governs its severity*? Using a controlled lending simulation in which two demographic groups are **identical in ground truth** — so every disparity that emerges is a pure loop artifact — we find the loop is trapping only when the individual-level feature is weakly informative, forcing the model to lean on group identity. We show that **feature informativeness is a smooth, monotone governing lever**: as feature noise rises, the persistent approval-rate gap of a greedy allocator grows from 0.04 to 0.26 and its group-B calibration error quadruples. Against baselines (ε-greedy, UCB-style optimism, demographic-parity constraint), evaluated over 32 seeds with paired-bootstrap tests, we find that (i) targeted exploration (UCB) *matches* random exploration (ε-greedy) on fairness gaps — statistically indistinguishable (Δ=0.004, p=0.35) — while delivering significantly higher qualified-approval (+0.029, p<0.001) and utility, so it buys fairness at strictly lower efficiency cost; (ii) this "free lunch" is bounded — pushing the optimism bonus past a moderate level keeps shrinking gaps but begins to erode utility; (iii) a parity constraint erases the visible gap but leaves the underlying miscalibration intact; and (iv) counterintuitively, adding *more* seed data from the biased region worsens the trap. Code and raw logs accompany the paper.

## 1. Introduction

A defining socio-technical property of deployed decision systems is that they generate the very data they later learn from. A lender records repayment only for approved applicants; predictive policing observes crime only where patrols are sent. This *selective-labels* / censored-feedback structure has been repeatedly identified as a driver of runaway disparity: past decisions bias future training data, and disparities can compound over time [1,2,4].

Most of this literature establishes *that* feedback loops can amplify bias and argues, correctly, that purely technical fixes are insufficient [1,3]. Less attention is paid to a question a practitioner actually needs answered: **under what conditions does the loop trap a group, and which design knob most controls the damage?** A blanket "feedback loops are dangerous" gives no guidance on where to spend a limited fairness budget.

This paper isolates one answer empirically. We build a minimal lending simulation with a deliberately strong null: the two groups are statistically identical in ground truth and in feature distribution. Any group gap that appears is therefore *manufactured entirely by the learning loop*, not by the world. Our central finding is that the trap is not automatic. When the model has a genuinely predictive *individual* feature, it rescues high-quality members of the disadvantaged group and the initial disparity decays on its own. The trap emerges only as the individual signal weakens and the model is pushed to rely on group identity as a proxy. Feature informativeness thus acts as a continuous dial on trap severity — a lever often more addressable in practice (better features, richer applications, human review of borderline cases) than the loop structure itself.

### Related Work

**Feedback loops and disparity amplification.** Ensign et al. and follow-on simulation studies of predictive policing show patrol-allocation loops amplify racial disparities via biased observation [2,7]. Work on training generative models on their own outputs ("fairness feedback loops") shows self-training chains amplify representational disparity [4]. Our contribution is complementary: rather than demonstrating amplification, we characterize the *regime boundary* separating self-correcting from self-trapping loops.

**Selective labels.** Lakkaraju et al. formalized the selective-labels problem for evaluating decision policies under censored outcomes [6]. We use the same censoring structure as the *mechanism* of harm and study interventions on it.

**Exploration for long-term fairness.** A recurring proposal is that uncertainty-aware exploration or calibration can prevent disparity amplification [1]. We test two concrete instances (ε-greedy and UCB-style optimism) head-to-head and quantify their fairness–utility trade-offs with significance tests.

**Sociotechnical framing.** A parallel critical literature argues fairness metrics can entrench power and that non-technical remedies are essential [3,5]. Our parity-constrained baseline shows a purely metric-satisfying fix can mask, rather than cure, an epistemic deficit about a group.

## 2. Method

**World.** Applicants arrive in rounds of 400. Each has scalar quality `x ~ N(0,1)` and group `g ∈ {A,B}` (50/50). True repayment probability is `p(x) = σ(1.6x)`, **identical for both groups**. If approved, an applicant repays (label 1) with probability `p(x)`; utility is `+1` for a repaid loan, `−1` for a default (0 if not approved).

**Model.** Group-aware logistic regression on `[1, x_obs, g, x_obs·g]`, fit by gradient descent (numpy only). The design lets the model represent per-group intercepts and slopes — so it *can* recover the (identical) truth, but must estimate each group's parameters from that group's observed labels, making it vulnerable to censoring. Crucially, the model observes a **noisy** feature `x_obs = x + N(0, σ_noise)`; outcomes still depend on the clean `x`. `σ_noise` controls how much genuine individual signal is available versus how much the model must fall back on group identity.

**Censored feedback.** Labels are appended to the training set **only for approved applicants**; the model retrains every round (40 rounds).

**Seed bias.** Group A's seed labels span all `x`; group B's few seed labels come only from a restricted low-`x` slice (`x < −0.3`) — a plausible historical artifact ("B was only ever approved when very safe"). This is the initial perturbation from which any trap must grow or decay.

**Allocators.**
- **greedy**: approve iff predicted `p ≥ τ=0.5` (no exploration).
- **eps_explore**: greedy, but with probability `ε` approve a uniformly random applicant (untargeted exploration).
- **ucb**: approve iff `pred + β·u ≥ τ`, `u = 1/√(n_g+1)` grows for the group with fewer observed labels (targeted exploration).
- **dp_parity**: enforce equal approval *rate* across groups each round.

**Metrics** ("final window" = mean of last 10 rounds). *rate_gap*: |approval-rate A − B| (should be 0 since groups are identical). *tpr_gap*: gap in approval among the truly-creditworthy (`p≥τ`). *qual_appr*: fraction of truly-creditworthy applicants approved (allocative efficiency). *util*: mean per-applicant net return. *calibErr_B*: |mean predicted − mean true| for B. **Statistics.** The main allocator comparison uses **32 seeds**; allocator-vs-allocator differences are tested with a **paired bootstrap over seeds** (20 000 resamples, two-sided). Ablation sweeps use 8 seeds.

## 3. Results

**The trap is not automatic; a strong feature self-corrects.** With an informative feature (`σ_noise=0.5`) the greedy allocator's approval-rate gap settles at only 0.044 despite the biased seed — the individual signal lets it approve high-`x` B applicants, whose labels correct the model. The manufactured disparity decays on its own.

**Feature informativeness is a smooth governing lever.** Sweeping `σ_noise` for greedy (Table 1) shows every harm metric worsening monotonically as the individual signal degrades and the model leans on group identity:

*Table 1 — greedy, final window vs feature noise (8 seeds).*

| σ_noise | rate_gap | tpr_gap | qual_appr | calibErr_B | util |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.5 | 0.044 | 0.054 | 0.839 | 0.021 | 0.219 |
| 1.0 | 0.047 | 0.069 | 0.730 | 0.022 | 0.169 |
| 2.0 | 0.084 | 0.105 | 0.602 | 0.030 | 0.102 |
| 3.0 | 0.142 | 0.170 | 0.509 | 0.033 | 0.068 |
| 4.0 | 0.262 | 0.293 | 0.331 | 0.080 | 0.029 |

The persistent gap grows ~6× and B-calibration error ~4× as the feature becomes uninformative: *the loop traps a group to the extent the model must proxy through group identity.*

**Allocator comparison, with significance (32 seeds, trap regime `σ_noise=2.0`; Table 2).** At 32 seeds the greedy gap point-estimate settles to 0.063±0.040 (the earlier 8-seed 0.084 was inflated by one unlucky seed), still the widest and most variable. Both exploration methods significantly reduce it: paired bootstrap gives greedy−ε_greedy Δrate_gap = +0.014 (p=0.049) and Δtpr_gap = +0.015 (p=0.040); greedy−UCB Δrate_gap = +0.010 (p=0.085, marginal). `dp_parity` drives the visible gap to ~0.002 (vs greedy, Δ=−0.061, p<0.001).

*Table 2 — allocators, final window, mean±sd over 32 seeds.*

| method | rate_gap | tpr_gap | qual_appr | util | calibErr_B |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| greedy | 0.063±0.040 | 0.079±0.043 | 0.611 | 0.098±0.010 | 0.026 |
| eps_explore | 0.050±0.015 | 0.064±0.019 | 0.620 | 0.094±0.011 | 0.019 |
| ucb | 0.054±0.020 | 0.068±0.022 | 0.650 | 0.098±0.009 | 0.022 |
| dp_parity | 0.002±0.001 | 0.041±0.011 | 0.616 | 0.098±0.010 | 0.023 |

**Targeted exploration matches random exploration on fairness but is strictly more efficient.** We previously claimed UCB "Pareto-dominates" ε-greedy; the paired tests refine this. On the fairness gaps the two are **statistically indistinguishable** (UCB−ε_greedy Δrate_gap = +0.004, p=0.35). UCB's genuine, significant advantage is *efficiency*: qualified-approval +0.029 (p<0.001) and utility +0.005 (p=0.050), because directing exploration at the under-sampled group approves *deserving* B applicants rather than random low-quality ones. Random exploration reaches the same fairness only by wasting approvals — its utility (0.094) is the lowest of all methods. So the honest statement is: **UCB attains ε-greedy's fairness at strictly lower utility cost**, not that it dominates on fairness itself.

**The free lunch is bounded (answering: is UCB monotone in β?).** Extending the optimism bonus past the range originally reported (Table 3) shows gaps and qualified-approval keep improving with β, but **utility peaks near β≈2 and then declines** (0.102→0.096→0.088 as β goes 2→4→6). Beyond a moderate bonus the model over-approves genuinely low-quality applicants from the under-sampled group: the intervention stops being free and becomes a fairness-for-utility trade. The "free lunch" therefore holds only in a bounded, tunable regime, not for arbitrarily aggressive optimism.

*Table 3 — UCB vs β, trap regime (8 seeds).*

| β | rate_gap | tpr_gap | qual_appr | util |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2.0 | 0.051 | 0.067 | 0.713 | 0.102 |
| 4.0 | 0.047 | 0.052 | 0.789 | 0.096 |
| 6.0 | 0.045 | 0.045 | 0.845 | 0.088 |

**A parity constraint hides the trap without curing it.** `dp_parity` zeroes the visible gap by construction, yet its group-B calibration error (0.023) is statistically comparable to greedy's (0.026): the tiny difference (Δ=−0.003) is significant only because of its small variance, and is negligible in magnitude — the model is *still* epistemically wrong about B; the constraint merely overrides its scores. The deficit would resurface the moment the constraint is relaxed or applied to a new sub-population, mirroring the sociotechnical critique that metric-satisfying fixes can mask rather than remedy harm [3,5].

**Representativeness beats quantity (counterintuitive).** Sweeping the group-B seed fraction (trap regime) shows *more* B seed data yielding a *larger* final gap: seed_B_frac 0.5 → rate_gap 0.116, calibErr_B 0.064, versus 0.05 → 0.057, 0.022. Because the B seed is region-restricted (only low-`x`, low-repayment), piling on more of it makes the model *more confident that B is bad*. The fix for a censored-feedback trap is representative exploration, not raw data volume.

## 4. Conclusion

Feedback loops in algorithmic allocation are widely flagged as bias amplifiers, but our controlled study — groups identical in ground truth, so every gap is a pure artifact — shows the trap is conditional, not automatic, and its severity is governed by a single interpretable lever: **feature informativeness**. When the model can rely on genuine individual signal it self-corrects; when it must proxy through group identity the loop traps the disadvantaged group, monotonically worse as signal degrades. Among interventions, uncertainty-directed exploration (UCB) matches random exploration's fairness at significantly lower utility cost — a free lunch, but only up to a moderate optimism level, beyond which fairness is bought back with utility; a demographic-parity constraint cosmetically closes the gap while leaving the model's beliefs about the group uncorrected; and adding more data from a biased region actively backfires.

**Limitations.** This is a toy simulation (scalar feature, logistic model, 40 rounds, two groups, identical ground truth, additive-Gaussian noise on a single feature). We deliberately assumed identical groups to isolate the loop; real settings add genuine base-rate differences, strategic applicants, and label noise, and our headline monotonic trends have been verified only for this one noise mechanism — heteroskedastic-across-group noise and varying the seed-restriction threshold remain open. The UCB uncertainty proxy is a per-group count, not a calibrated posterior, so the *magnitude* of its efficiency edge is tied to that design even though its qualitative direction is significant. These are the natural next steps; the transferable claims — informativeness governs trap severity; targeted exploration is more efficient than random at equal fairness (within a bounded β); parity ≠ calibration; representativeness ≠ quantity — are the ones we stand behind.
