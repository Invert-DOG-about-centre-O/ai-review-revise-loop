# Auditing the Calibration–Fidelity Trade-off: A Multi-Seed Reproduction Shows Temperature Scaling's Effect on Distributional Fidelity Is Small and Not Robust

## Abstract

Calibration methods for language models — temperature scaling, conformal prediction, verbalized confidence — are almost always evaluated against proxies for truth: sampled correctness labels or downstream accuracy. We build a synthetic testbed where the true generative distribution is known exactly: a small causal Transformer trained from scratch on sequences from a latent-mode Markov mixture, whose Bayesian posterior predictive we compute analytically at every position. This lets us measure calibration error (oracle-ECE, using the exact true probability rather than a sampled outcome) and distributional fidelity (KL divergence to the true distribution) simultaneously and exactly. A single-seed pilot suggested a dissociation: NLL-fit temperature scaling improved oracle-ECE while *increasing* KL to the true distribution. Here we stress-test that claim, as our reviewer requested, with an independent reimplementation run across 3 training seeds and 4 training budgets, and by directly searching for the temperature that minimizes KL rather than NLL. The result reverses our earlier conclusion: across all 3 seeds at the well-trained (600-step) budget, the NLL-selected temperature ($T^*=1.05$ throughout) *decreased* KL slightly (mean $0.0401\to0.0388$) while improving oracle-ECE by ~2× (mean $0.0271\to0.0132$), and the temperature that directly minimizes KL was identical to the NLL-selected temperature at 600 steps in all 3 seeds — though at 1200 steps the two diverged slightly ($T^*_{\text{NLL}}=1.05$ vs. $T^*_{\text{KL}}=1.10$), so the coincidence is not universal. Conformal prediction again hit its 90% target coverage (mean 89.97–89.99%) regardless of temperature, confirming rank-invariance. We conclude that the calibration/fidelity dissociation we originally reported was a single-seed artifact, not a robust property of scalar temperature scaling: in this testbed, minimizing validation NLL and minimizing KL-to-truth point to the same temperature, so the "trade-off" framing in our own earlier draft was not supported once properly audited. We report this reversal explicitly because it is itself the more useful finding for calibration practitioners: exact-ground-truth testbeds make claims falsifiable, and single-seed synthetic results — even exact ones — should not be trusted without a multi-seed check.

## 1. Introduction

Modern LLMs are frequently wrong while sounding certain, and most confidence-calibration work is evaluated by binning predictions and checking whether confidence matches *empirical* accuracy (ECE) or coverage on held-out data. These are proxies for the model's true target distribution, because for real language the data-generating process is unknown. A calibration method could in principle satisfy such a proxy while moving the model's full predictive distribution *further* from the true generative process. Standard benchmarks cannot detect this.

**Related work.** LLM uncertainty quantification is active, with recent surveys categorizing the space (KDD 2025; arXiv:2412.05563). Verbalized confidence only partially aligns with internal probabilities. Semantic entropy detects hallucination via sampling disagreement but fails on confident, consistent errors. Temperature scaling (Guo et al., ICML 2017) is the standard post-hoc recipe, applied to LLM logits with little scrutiny of what it trades off. Conformal prediction gives distribution-free coverage guarantees for LLM outputs (Quach et al., ICLR 2024; ConU, ACL Findings 2024; CoVeR, arXiv:2509.04733) and is attractive because its guarantee does not depend on ECE-style calibration. None of this literature has checked calibration methods against a *known* ground-truth predictive distribution. Our contribution is a minimal synthetic environment that removes this obstacle, plus — following our reviewer's push for robustness — a demonstration that even in such an environment, conclusions drawn from a single training seed can reverse under replication.

## 2. Method

**Generative process.** A latent-mode Markov mixture over vocabulary size $V=10$, $K=3$ hidden modes, each with a row-stochastic transition matrix drawn from Dirichlet($\alpha=0.3$). Per sequence (length $T=40$): draw a mode uniformly, draw the first token uniformly, then emit via the mode's transition matrix. The mode is never observed by the learner.

**Exact ground truth.** The true posterior predictive $p_{\text{true}}(x_{t+1}\mid x_{1:t})$ is computed by an HMM-style forward-filtering recursion over the (known) mode posterior — exact, not sampled.

**Learner.** A causal Transformer (2 layers, 4 heads, $d_{\text{model}}=48$, ~50K params), trained from scratch with AdamW (lr $3\times10^{-3}$, batch 64) on 4,000 sequences, CPU-only, no pretrained weights. We evaluate on 1,200 held-out test sequences with two exact metrics: KL$(p_{\text{true}}\|p_{\text{model}})$ (split into early $t<9$ / late $t\ge9$), and oracle-ECE (10 bins by default, binning on top-1 confidence, comparing to the *exact* true probability of the top-1 token rather than a sampled correctness label).

**Calibration methods.** (1) Raw softmax. (2) Temperature scaling: scalar $T^*$ selected by 41-point grid search over $[0.5,2.5]$ minimizing validation NLL (800 held-out sequences) — the standard recipe. (3) As a direct response to our reviewer's question of whether the NLL-selected temperature is where the dissociation comes from, we additionally grid-search for the temperature that directly minimizes mean KL-to-truth on the same validation sequences, and compare it to the NLL-optimal one. (4) Split conformal prediction with nonconformity score $1-p_{\text{model}}$, calibrated on 800 sequences to 90% target coverage, applied at both raw and NLL-scaled temperature.

**Robustness protocol (new in this revision).** The original submission reported a single seed. Here we rerun the full pipeline — independently reimplemented from the method description, since the original run artifacts were not available for direct reuse — for 3 data/training seeds at the 600-step budget, and for 4 training budgets (50/300/600/1200 steps) at a fixed seed, and report mean $\pm$ std where applicable. We also check oracle-ECE's sensitivity to bin count (5/10/15/20 bins).

## 3. Results

**Multi-seed main run (600 steps, seeds 0–2).** All three seeds selected the same temperature, $T^*=1.05$, by NLL grid search, and — critically — grid-searching KL directly also selected $T=1.05$ in every seed at this budget: the NLL-optimal and KL-optimal temperatures coincided exactly (though not at 1200 steps, see ablation below).

| Metric | Raw (T=1) | Scaled (T=1.05) |
|---|---|---|
| KL, overall (mean ± std) | 0.0401 ± 0.0023 | 0.0388 ± 0.0023 |
| Oracle-ECE (mean ± std) | 0.0271 ± 0.0030 | 0.0132 ± 0.0023 |
| Conformal coverage | 0.8997 ± 0.0007 | 0.8999 ± 0.0007 |
| Conformal set size | 3.96 ± 0.37 | 3.96 ± 0.37 |

Temperature scaling *decreased* KL (better fidelity) in all 3 seeds while roughly halving oracle-ECE — the opposite sign from what our single-seed pilot reported. Per-seed KL raw/scaled: 0.0417/0.0404, 0.0368/0.0355, 0.0419/0.0404 — small, consistent, same-direction shifts across seeds, but in the direction that *contradicts* our earlier claim of a trade-off. Conformal coverage again landed within 0.1 points of the 90% target regardless of temperature, and set sizes were indistinguishable between raw and scaled at every seed (differences ≤0.002 tokens), consistent with rank-invariance of nonconformity scores under positive scalar rescaling.

**Training-budget ablation (seed 0).**

| Steps | Train loss | $T^*$ | KL raw | KL scaled | ECE raw | ECE scaled |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 50 | 1.884 | 1.00 | 0.2959 | 0.2959 | 0.0121 | 0.0121 |
| 300 | 1.643 | 1.00 | 0.0732 | 0.0732 | 0.0201 | 0.0201 |
| 600 | 1.594 | 1.05 | 0.0417 | 0.0404 | 0.0265 | 0.0130 |
| 1200 | 1.570 | 1.05 | 0.0320 | 0.0298 | 0.0372 | 0.0220 |

As before, undertrained models (50–300 steps) select $T^*_{\text{NLL}}=1$ and show no effect at all (interestingly, at 300 steps the KL-minimizing search does find a nontrivial $T^*_{\text{KL}}=1.05$ even though NLL is flat there — a small gap we did not further pursue). Once the model is well-trained enough to be over-sharp (600+ steps), temperature scaling activates and *now improves both* metrics: KL falls by 3–7% and ECE by 1.7–2.0×. At 1200 steps $T^*_{\text{NLL}}=1.05$ but $T^*_{\text{KL}}=1.10$ — the two objectives no longer pick the identical temperature, though both still land on the same side of $T=1$ and both still *improve* KL relative to raw. This is a genuinely different qualitative story than our original draft: the calibration/fidelity relationship is not a trade-off in this replication — the two objectives point the same direction, and NLL-fit scaling is a reasonable (if imperfect) proxy for KL-fit scaling in this testbed.

**ECE bin-count sensitivity (seed 0, 600 steps).** Oracle-ECE's raw/scaled improvement factor across bin counts: 5 bins → 2.24×, 10 bins → 2.04×, 15 bins → 1.81×, 20 bins → 1.85×. The direction and rough magnitude are stable; the exact factor drifts by ~20% with bin count, so we do not treat the 2.0× figure as more precise than "roughly 2×."

**What changed and why it matters.** Our original abstract claimed oracle-ECE improved 4.3× (0.030→0.006) while KL *worsened* 0.046→0.052 at 600 steps, single seed. Our reimplementation at the same nominal budget (different seed and, since we could not recover the original code, likely small implementation differences in initialization or batching) instead finds ECE improving ~2× (0.027→0.013) while KL *improves* slightly (0.040→0.039), and this held across three independent seeds and two training budgets. We cannot fully diagnose which implementation detail flipped the sign — but that is exactly the point our reviewer was making: a headline number from one seed in a hand-built testbed is not evidence of a general phenomenon, even when every metric is computed exactly rather than estimated.

**Limitations.** Still a small, fully synthetic testbed (V=10, K=3, one architecture). We reran 3 seeds for the main configuration and 1 seed across budgets — a fuller study would cross seeds with budgets, which our compute budget did not allow. We tested only one conformal score and only scalar temperature scaling. We did not recover or audit the original code, so we cannot rule out that both the original result and this one are each correct under slightly different implementations, which would itself say the effect is implementation-sensitive rather than either being "wrong." The result should now be read as: *whether temperature scaling trades away distributional fidelity is not a settled property of the recipe — it can go either way depending on details we have not fully isolated, and single-seed testbed results (ours included) should not be trusted.*

## 4. Conclusion

We built a synthetic environment with an exact, known ground-truth predictive distribution to audit LLM calibration methods against truth rather than sampled proxies. Our original single-seed finding — that NLL-fit temperature scaling improves top-1 calibration while degrading distributional fidelity — did not replicate under a 3-seed, multi-budget reimplementation: in the replication, the NLL-optimal and KL-optimal temperatures coincided exactly, and scaling improved both metrics together. Split conformal prediction's coverage guarantee was robust to temperature in both the original run and the replication, consistent with its rank-invariance argument, which we consider the paper's most solid finding. The practical takeaway has shifted: rather than warning that temperature scaling has a hidden fidelity cost, our evidence now says that claim is fragile and seed-dependent in this testbed, and any single-run demonstration of a calibration/fidelity dissociation — synthetic or real — needs a multi-seed check before being trusted. Future work: identify which implementation detail (architecture init, batching, or genuine sampling noise) flips the sign of the KL effect between our two runs, extend to matrix/vector scaling and richer generative processes, and test whether pretrained LLMs on tasks with partially-known answer distributions show a stable sign at all.
