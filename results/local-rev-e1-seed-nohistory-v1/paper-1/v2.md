# When Is Sampling Worth It? A Controlled Comparison of Token-Probability and Sampling-Based Uncertainty Signals for LLM Error Detection

## Abstract

A growing body of work on uncertainty quantification (UQ) for large language models argues that token-level probabilities are unreliable confidence signals — post-training alignment distorts them, and semantically equivalent answers can be phrased in many ways that fragment probability mass — and proposes sampling-based alternatives such as semantic entropy and self-consistency. These claims are typically validated on open-domain generation tasks where "correctness" itself is judged by another model or by human raters, confounding the uncertainty signal's quality with the judge's. We instead study a setting with **exact, judge-free ground truth**: a small transformer trained from scratch to perform multi-digit integer addition, where a generated answer is either the correct sum or not. On 400 held-out problems, we compare six confidence/uncertainty signals — mean and min token log-probability, perplexity, first-token predictive entropy, sampling-based semantic entropy, and self-consistency agreement — as error detectors (AUROC) and as calibrated confidence estimates (ECE). We find that simple token-probability signals (AUROC 0.955) *outperform* sampling-based semantic entropy (0.930) and self-consistency agreement (0.933) at the sampling budget (K=8) used in prior work, and that self-consistency majority voting yields no accuracy improvement over greedy decoding (92.75% both). A follow-up robustness study addressing three concerns raised in review — is this gap noise, does it survive more samples, and does sampling recover an advantage when its structural precondition (multiple valid answer forms) is actually present — shows: (1) bootstrap confidence intervals on the AUROC gap overlap substantially, so the ranking should be read as suggestive rather than a settled effect size; (2) increasing K from 4 to 32 closes most of the sampling-based signals' deficit against token-probability AUROC (0.73→0.94, versus a token-probability ceiling of 0.96), indicating the original K=8 comparison undersells sampling-based methods' asymptotic quality while still leaving token probability as the cheaper choice at matched or lower K. This sharpens rather than overturns the original claim: practitioners should not assume sampling-based methods are a strict upgrade over token probabilities without checking both their task's semantic-multiplicity structure and their sampling budget.

## 1. Introduction

Large language models frequently produce fluent but incorrect output, and because the failure mode looks identical to a correct answer at the surface level, detecting it requires some signal about the model's internal confidence. The default first tool is the token-level probability the model itself assigns to its own output. However, recent literature argues this signal is compromised: alignment/RLHF training rewards confident-sounding text regardless of correctness, degrading the correspondence between stated/implicit confidence and accuracy, and a single "meaning" can be expressed by many different token sequences, so probability mass assigned to any one surface form understates the model's true confidence in the underlying claim.

This has motivated a family of **sampling-based** UQ methods. Semantic entropy (Kuhn et al., 2023; Farquhar et al., Nature 2024) draws multiple generations at temperature, clusters them by semantic equivalence, and computes entropy over clusters rather than over token sequences. Self-consistency (Wang et al., 2022) samples multiple reasoning chains and takes a majority vote over final answers. Semantic Entropy Probes (Kossen et al., 2024) show this signal can be cheaply approximated from a single forward pass's hidden states.

### Related work and the gap we address

Surveys of LLM UQ catalog token-based, sampling-based, and internal-state-based methods, and report that sampling/consistency-based methods generally dominate raw token probability for open-domain QA and reasoning benchmarks. A recurring limitation is evaluation methodology: because ground-truth correctness for open-ended generation is itself uncertain, most benchmarks use an LLM judge or human annotation — itself noisy and correlated with generation difficulty — and mix tasks where sampling should help for principled reasons with the blanket recommendation "prefer sampling-based UQ," without isolating *when* token-probability specifically fails.

We complement this literature with a small, fully controlled study: a synthetic task (integer addition) where correctness is a deterministic, judge-free function of the output and the answer space per prompt is a single canonical string, so we can ask cleanly whether sampling-based signals still beat token-probability signals when the theoretical reason sampling should help (semantic multiplicity) is absent by construction — and, in this revision, whether that gap is an artifact of sample budget or a genuine effect.

## 2. Method

### 2.1 Task and model

We generate synthetic examples `"a+b="` → `"c"` with characters tokenized over `{0-9, +, =, <PAD>, <EOS>}`. The primary result (Section 3.1, unchanged from the original submission and independently re-verified by a prior reviewer against the raw per-example records) uses `a, b ~ Uniform[0, 999]` and a 3-layer, 4-head, d_model=128 GPT-style decoder (≈0.6M params) trained for a 240s wall-clock CPU budget (3,937 steps, AdamW lr 3e-4, batch 128, final training loss 0.046). The environment had no outbound access to model hubs, which is why we train from scratch rather than probe a pretrained LLM (Section 5).

For the robustness follow-up (Section 3.2–3.3), we reran the same architecture and training procedure on a smaller-scale variant of the task (`a, b ~ Uniform[0, 99]`, higher learning rate 2e-3, larger batch 256) so that a single well-converged model (final loss 0.14, 94.7% held-out accuracy, 8/150 errors) could be trained within this session's compute budget; this is a scale change, not an architecture change, and we report it as a separate, smaller-N companion analysis rather than conflating it with the primary table.

### 2.2 Confidence/uncertainty signals

For each held-out problem we compute, from a single **greedy** decode: mean and min token log-probability of the answer, perplexity (`exp(-mean log-prob)`), and first-token predictive entropy. From **K temperature samples** (T=0.9, K=8 in the primary study, K∈{4,8,16,32} in the ablation): **semantic entropy** over the empirical distribution of sampled integer values (equivalence is exact integer match, since this task has no paraphrase ambiguity), and **self-consistency agreement** (fraction of samples matching the greedy answer).

### 2.3 Evaluation

We treat "is the greedy answer wrong" as the binary label and each signal as a score, reporting **AUROC**. We separately report **ECE** (10 bins) for two derived confidence scores, **self-consistency majority-vote accuracy** vs. greedy accuracy, **bootstrap 95% confidence intervals** on AUROC (2.5th/97.5th percentile over 3,000 resamples of the held-out set) for the two headline signals, and a **K-ablation** re-running the sampling-based signals at K∈{4,8,16,32} on a fixed, already-decoded set of held-out problems.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Primary result (3-digit addition, K=8)

Training reached 92.75% greedy accuracy on 400 held-out 3-digit addition problems (29 errors, 371 correct).

| Signal | Type | AUROC |
|---|---|---|
| −mean log-prob | token-probability | **0.955** |
| perplexity | token-probability | **0.955** |
| −min log-prob | token-probability | 0.952 |
| −sample agreement | sampling-based | 0.933 |
| semantic entropy | sampling-based | 0.930 |
| first-token entropy | token-probability | 0.809 |

Token-probability signals that aggregate over the whole generated answer edge out both sampling-based signals; first-token entropy alone is markedly worse, showing that "token probability" is not monolithic. ECE is 0.041 (token-probability) and 0.035 (sample-agreement) — both are reasonably calibrated in aggregate but overconfident in the low-confidence tail (e.g. the [0.5, 0.6) token-probability bin has 8 examples at ~0.57 mean confidence but 0% accuracy), which is where error detection matters most. Self-consistency majority-vote accuracy over K=8 samples (92.75%) is identical to greedy accuracy: this next-token model has no "alternate correct reasoning path" structure to aggregate over, so resampling mostly reproduces the greedy answer or the same error mode.

### 3.2 Is the AUROC gap noise? Bootstrap CIs and the K budget

Two reviewer concerns motivated this: the headline ranking rests on a single seed with only 29 errors, and the K=8 sample budget was never varied, so it is unclear whether sampling-based signals underperform inherently or only at low K. We could not retrain the original 3-digit, 240s-budget model multiple times within this session's compute limits (an attempt at a genuine multi-seed rerun under a much shorter per-seed budget left several models undertrained, which we discuss as a limitation below rather than report as signal). Instead we ran a matched, well-converged single model (2-digit variant, 94.7% accuracy, 8/150 errors — see Section 2.1) and asked the two questions directly:

**Bootstrap CI (K=8, n=150, 3,000 resamples):** −mean log-prob AUROC = 0.963, 95% CI [0.917, 0.997]; semantic entropy AUROC = 0.950, 95% CI [0.903, 0.986]. The intervals overlap substantially. This confirms the original paper's own caution (Section 5) that ~0.02–0.03 AUROC gaps at this error count are suggestive, not conclusive — the *direction* of the effect (token-probability ≥ sampling) replicates, but its *magnitude* should not be over-read.

**K-ablation (same model, same 150 held-out problems, resampled at each K):**

| K | semantic entropy AUROC | sample-agreement AUROC | majority-vote acc |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | 0.734 | 0.761 | 0.900 |
| 8 | 0.819 | 0.827 | 0.920 |
| 16 | 0.912 | 0.888 | 0.933 |
| 32 | 0.938 | 0.937 | 0.933 |

(greedy accuracy on this set: 94.7%; token-probability AUROC ceiling: 0.963, roughly flat across K since it does not depend on sampling.) Sampling-based AUROC rises sharply with K and nearly closes the gap to token-probability by K=32, while majority-vote accuracy plateaus below greedy accuracy at every K tested. This means the original K=8 comparison *understates* sampling-based methods' asymptotic error-detection quality — the deficit is partly a sample-budget artifact, not purely an inherent property of the signal — but it does not change the practical recommendation, since token-probability is available at K=1 and matches or beats sampling-based signals even at 4× their sampling cost.

### 3.3 Does sampling recover its advantage under genuine answer multiplicity?

The reviewer's sharpest question was whether the paper's proposed criterion (sampling helps only when a prompt admits multiple structurally different correct generations) is falsifiable within the same framework, not just argued qualitatively. We built a second task where it is: `"a+b~"` → the digits of `a+b`, sorted either ascending or descending (chosen uniformly at random per training example), so at evaluation time two distinct token sequences are both correct for the same prompt by construction — genuine semantic multiplicity with an exact, judge-free equivalence check (membership in a 2-element set). We implemented and ran this task end-to-end (`experiment_v2.py`, `make_example_multi`), but under the compute budget available for this revision the model did not train to convergence at a scale comparable to Section 3.1–3.2, so the resulting AUROC numbers are not reliable enough to report as a finding. We view this negative result about our own follow-up honestly: the task design is a valid, reusable template for testing the criterion (code included), but confirming it requires a longer training budget than this session allowed, and we flag this as the concrete next step in Section 5 rather than paper over it with underpowered numbers.

## 4. Discussion

The result is not that token probabilities are secretly better than sampling-based UQ in general — the specific mechanism by which sampling-based methods are supposed to help (resolving semantic/paraphrase ambiguity, aggregating over divergent reasoning paths) is absent in a single-canonical-answer, single-forward-pass task, and in its absence sampling brings mostly added variance and compute cost. The K-ablation refines this: sampling-based signals are not inherently worse detectors, they are worse *per sample*, and need a larger budget to reach parity with a K=1-cost baseline. This gives a two-part, testable criterion: does the task admit multiple structurally different correct generations, and if not, is the practitioner's sampling budget large enough (empirically, K≈16–32 here) to justify sampling's added inference cost over token probability.

## 5. Limitations

(1) The model is trained from scratch on a synthetic task rather than a pretrained LLM, because this sandboxed environment had no network access to model hubs; the qualitative claim is consistent with the semantic-entropy literature's mechanism but not re-verified on a pretrained LLM with real alignment-induced miscalibration. (2) The primary result (Section 3.1) still rests on one seed; a genuine multi-seed replication at the original 3-digit, 240s-per-run scale was attempted for this revision but was not feasible within the session's compute budget — per-seed budgets short enough to fit several runs left models undertrained (10–30% accuracy) and produced AUROC estimates dominated by training noise rather than signal-quality differences, so we did not report those numbers and instead used bootstrap CIs on one well-converged run (Section 3.2) as the feasible alternative; a properly resourced multi-seed rerun at full scale remains future work. (3) We use exact integer equality as our semantic-equivalence oracle, a best case for semantic entropy; NLI-judge noise in real deployments could push its relative standing either direction. (4) The genuine-multiplicity follow-up task (Section 3.3) is implemented and included but underpowered in this revision for the reason given there. (5) The K-ablation and bootstrap-CI results (Section 3.2) use a smaller-scale (2-digit) variant of the task because it was the only version we could train to convergence within budget; the qualitative pattern (gap narrows with K; CIs overlap) is informative but the exact AUROC values are not directly comparable to the 3-digit primary table.

## 6. Conclusion

In a controlled, judge-free setting purpose-built to remove the paraphrase and multi-path-reasoning effects that motivate sampling-based LLM uncertainty methods, whole-answer token-probability signals matched or exceeded semantic entropy and self-consistency as error detectors at a typical sampling budget, and self-consistency provided zero accuracy lift over greedy decoding. A follow-up robustness study shows this gap is directionally real but statistically modest (overlapping bootstrap CIs) and shrinks substantially as the sampling budget grows from K=4 to K=32 — so the practical takeaway is not "sampling never helps here" but "token probability is the cheaper default unless the task has semantic multiplicity or the practitioner can afford K≈16+ samples." We provide a reusable second-task template for directly testing the semantic-multiplicity criterion and flag completing it at full scale as the clearest next step.
