# When Is Sampling Worth It? A Controlled Comparison of Token-Probability and Sampling-Based Uncertainty Signals for LLM Error Detection

## Abstract

A growing body of work on uncertainty quantification (UQ) for large language models argues that token-level probabilities are unreliable confidence signals — post-training alignment distorts them, and semantically equivalent answers can be phrased in many ways that fragment probability mass — and proposes sampling-based alternatives such as semantic entropy and self-consistency. These claims are typically validated on open-domain generation tasks where "correctness" itself is judged by another model or by human raters, confounding the uncertainty signal's quality with the judge's. We instead study a setting with **exact, judge-free ground truth**: a small transformer trained from scratch to perform multi-digit integer addition, where a generated answer is either the correct sum or not. On 400 held-out problems, we compare six confidence/uncertainty signals — mean and min token log-probability, perplexity, first-token predictive entropy, sampling-based semantic entropy, and self-consistency agreement — as error detectors (AUROC) and as calibrated confidence estimates (ECE). We find that simple token-probability signals (AUROC 0.955) *outperform* sampling-based semantic entropy (0.930) and self-consistency agreement (0.933) in this controlled setting, and that self-consistency majority voting yields no accuracy improvement over greedy decoding (92.75% both). This suggests that the case for sampling-based UQ over token probabilities rests substantially on properties of open-ended natural language generation (paraphrase, multi-step reasoning divergence) that are absent in single-token-distribution-dominated tasks, and that practitioners should not assume sampling-based methods are a strict upgrade without first checking whether their task exhibits those properties.

## 1. Introduction

Large language models frequently produce fluent but incorrect output, and because the failure mode looks identical to a correct answer at the surface level, detecting it requires some signal about the model's internal confidence. The default first tool is the token-level probability the model itself assigns to its own output. However, recent literature argues this signal is compromised: alignment/RLHF training rewards confident-sounding text regardless of correctness, degrading the correspondence between stated/implicit confidence and accuracy (see survey discussion below), and a single "meaning" can be expressed by many different token sequences, so probability mass assigned to any one surface form understates the model's true confidence in the underlying claim.

This has motivated a family of **sampling-based** UQ methods. Semantic entropy (Kuhn et al., 2023; Farquhar et al., Nature 2024) draws multiple generations at temperature, clusters them by semantic equivalence (via bidirectional entailment), and computes entropy over clusters rather than over token sequences — directly targeting the paraphrase-fragmentation problem. Self-consistency (Wang et al., 2022) similarly samples multiple reasoning chains and takes a majority vote over final answers, improving accuracy on multi-step reasoning tasks by aggregating over divergent reasoning paths. Semantic Entropy Probes (Kossen et al., 2024) then show this signal can be cheaply approximated from a single forward pass's hidden states, avoiding the cost of multiple samples.

### Related work and the gap we address

Surveys of LLM UQ (e.g., the 2025–2026 ACM Computing Surveys and SIGKDD survey lines on calibration and uncertainty quantification) catalog token-based, sampling-based, and internal-state-based methods, and report that sampling/consistency-based methods generally dominate raw token probability for open-domain QA and reasoning benchmarks. Work such as "Why Don't You Know? Evaluating the Impact of Uncertainty Sources on UQ in LLMs" and "Certain but not Probable? Differentiating Certainty from Probability in LLM Token Outputs" explicitly separates sources of uncertainty (input ambiguity, reasoning-path divergence, decoding stochasticity) and argues token output probability conflates them. A recurring limitation across this literature is evaluation methodology: because ground-truth correctness for open-ended generation is itself uncertain, most benchmarks use an LLM judge or human annotation to label "correct," which (a) is itself a noisy, model-dependent process and (b) makes it hard to isolate whether an uncertainty signal is failing because of the signal itself or because of judge noise correlated with generation difficulty. Additionally, most evaluations mix together tasks where sampling should help for principled reasons (paraphrase-heavy open QA, multi-hop reasoning with divergent valid paths) with the general recommendation "prefer sampling-based UQ," without isolating *when* the token-probability signal specifically fails.

We complement this literature with a small, fully controlled study: a synthetic task (integer addition) where correctness is a deterministic, judge-free function of the output, where the answer space per prompt is a single canonical string (no paraphrase fragmentation), and where we can therefore ask cleanly whether sampling-based signals still beat token-probability signals when the theoretical reason sampling should help (semantic multiplicity) is absent by construction.

## 2. Method

### 2.1 Task and model

We generate synthetic examples `"a+b="` → `"c"` where `a, b` are sampled uniformly from `[0, 999]` and `c = a + b`, tokenized at the character level over the vocabulary `{0-9, +, =, <PAD>, <EOS>}`. We train a small GPT-style decoder-only transformer from scratch (3 layers, 4 attention heads, d_model=128, d_ff=256; ≈0.6M parameters) with a causal language-modeling loss computed only over answer and EOS tokens (prompt tokens are masked out of the loss). Training uses AdamW (lr 3e-4), batch size 128, and runs for a fixed wall-clock budget (240s) on CPU, yielding 3,937 gradient steps and a final training loss of 0.046. This environment had no outbound access to model hubs, which is why we train a small model from scratch rather than fine-tune or probe a pretrained LLM; we note this as a limitation in Section 5 but argue the controlled ground truth is independently valuable regardless of model provenance.

### 2.2 Confidence/uncertainty signals

For each of 400 held-out problems we compute, from a single **greedy** decode:
- **Mean token log-probability** and **min token log-probability** of the generated answer tokens (weakest-link signal).
- **Perplexity**, `exp(-mean log-prob)`.
- **First-token predictive entropy**: Shannon entropy of the softmax distribution over the first answer token.

And from **K=8 temperature samples** (T=0.9) per problem:
- **Semantic entropy**: since two answers are "equivalent" iff they parse to the same integer (no paraphrase ambiguity in this task, so equivalence checking is exact rather than NLI-based), we compute entropy over the empirical distribution of sampled integer values, `H = -Σ p_v log p_v`.
- **Self-consistency agreement**: the fraction of the K samples that match the greedy answer.

### 2.3 Evaluation

We treat "is the greedy answer wrong" as the binary label and each signal as a score, reporting **AUROC** for error detection (does the signal rank wrong answers above right ones?). We separately report **Expected Calibration Error (ECE, 10 bins)** for two derived confidence scores: token-probability confidence (`exp(mean log-prob)`) and sample-agreement confidence, plus **self-consistency majority-vote accuracy** vs. **greedy accuracy** as an ablation of whether sampling improves the point estimate (not just its uncertainty signal).

## 3. Results

Training reached 92.75% greedy accuracy on 400 held-out 3-digit addition problems (a nontrivial but imperfect task for a 0.6M-parameter character model, giving a good mix of correct/incorrect cases for the analysis below — 29 errors, 371 correct).

**Error-detection AUROC** (higher = better at separating right from wrong):

| Signal | Type | AUROC |
|---|---|---|
| −mean log-prob | token-probability | **0.955** |
| perplexity | token-probability | **0.955** |
| −min log-prob | token-probability | 0.952 |
| −sample agreement | sampling-based | 0.933 |
| semantic entropy | sampling-based | 0.930 |
| first-token entropy | token-probability | 0.809 |

Token-probability signals that aggregate over the *whole* generated answer (mean/min log-prob, perplexity) are the strongest error detectors, edging out both sampling-based signals. The single weakest signal is first-token entropy alone — unsurprising, since it only reflects uncertainty at the first digit and ignores the rest of the answer; this is itself a useful negative result showing that "token probability" is not monolithic and *which* token-level aggregation is used matters as much as token- vs. sample-based framing.

**Calibration.** ECE for token-probability confidence is 0.041; for sample-agreement confidence it is 0.035 — both are reasonably well calibrated in the high-confidence region (see reliability diagram, `results_figure.png`), with the majority of mass concentrated at confidence > 0.9 where empirical accuracy is 97.9% (token-prob bin) and 99.3% (sample-agreement bin). Both signals are markedly overconfident in the low-confidence tail (e.g., the [0.5, 0.6) token-probability bin has 8 examples at ~0.57 mean confidence but 0% accuracy), which is where error detection actually matters — this shows raw calibration (ECE) and error-ranking (AUROC) are answering different questions, and a signal can look well-calibrated in aggregate while still being a good or bad error detector at the decision-relevant threshold.

**Self-consistency vs. greedy accuracy.** Majority-vote accuracy over K=8 samples was 92.75%, identical to greedy accuracy. Unlike multi-step reasoning tasks (e.g., GSM8K-style chain-of-thought) where different sampled reasoning paths can arrive at different — and sometimes more often correct — answers, single-forward-pass arithmetic on a next-token model has no analogous "alternate correct path" structure to aggregate over: the model either has the right digit distribution or it doesn't, and resampling mostly reproduces the greedy answer or reproduces the same error mode. This is a direct empirical instance of the point above: self-consistency's benefit is tied to a specific structural property (divergent valid reasoning paths) that this task lacks by construction.

See `experiment.py` for the full implementation, `results.json` / `records.json` for raw outputs, and `results_figure.png` for the AUROC comparison and reliability diagram.

## 4. Discussion

The result is not that token probabilities are secretly better than sampling-based UQ in general — it is that the *specific mechanism* by which sampling-based methods are supposed to help (resolving semantic/paraphrase ambiguity, aggregating over divergent reasoning paths) is absent in a single-canonical-answer, single-forward-pass task, and in its absence sampling brings only added variance and compute cost without added signal. Token-level probability, aggregated over the full answer rather than a single token, remains a strong and much cheaper baseline whenever a task has (i) a small or canonical answer space and (ii) no meaningful multiple-paths-to-the-same-answer structure. This gives a concrete, testable criterion practitioners can check before paying the K× inference cost of sampling-based UQ: does correctness on this task admit multiple structurally different correct generations? If not, whole-answer token-probability aggregation (not just first-token entropy) is a reasonable and possibly stronger default.

## 5. Limitations

(1) The model is trained from scratch on a synthetic task rather than a pretrained LLM evaluated on natural language, because this sandboxed environment had no network access to model hubs; the qualitative claim (sampling helps most when semantic multiplicity exists) is consistent with the mechanism described in the semantic-entropy literature but has not been re-verified here on a pretrained LLM with real alignment-induced miscalibration. (2) Sample size is modest (400 problems, 29 errors), so AUROC confidence intervals are not tiny — differences of ~0.02 between the top signals should be read as suggestive rather than conclusive. (3) We use exact integer equality as our semantic-equivalence oracle, which is a best case for semantic entropy (no NLI-judge noise); in real deployments where equivalence checking is itself model-based, semantic entropy's relative disadvantage here would likely not transfer directly, and NLI-judge noise could push its real-world AUROC either direction relative to this idealized result. (4) We did not evaluate first-token-entropy variants that pool entropy across the full answer (only the whole-sequence log-prob variants), which would be a fairer comparison within the token-probability family; this is worth extending in a follow-up run.

## 6. Conclusion

In a controlled, judge-free setting purpose-built to remove the paraphrase and multi-path-reasoning effects that motivate sampling-based LLM uncertainty methods, whole-answer token-probability signals matched or exceeded semantic entropy and self-consistency as error detectors, and self-consistency provided zero accuracy lift over greedy decoding. This does not contradict the sampling-based UQ literature so much as sharpen its scope condition: the reported gains likely come specifically from tasks with semantic multiplicity or divergent reasoning paths, and the field would benefit from more evaluations that isolate this variable directly rather than only reporting aggregate benchmark numbers.
