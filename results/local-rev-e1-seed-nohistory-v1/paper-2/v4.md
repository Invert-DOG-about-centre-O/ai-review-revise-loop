# Algorithmic Sycophancy and Opinion Polarization: A Simulation Study of Agreement-Seeking Recommenders

## Abstract

Large language model (LLM) "sycophancy" — a model's tendency to preferentially agree with a user's stated views — is well studied within single conversations. We ask a socio-technical question one level up: what happens to a *population's* opinions when a recommender exhibits the same agreement-seeking bias, choosing what to show each user based on how well it matches that user's current stance? We build a lightweight, fully synthetic bounded-confidence opinion-dynamics simulation (300 agents, 150 rounds, 20 seeds) with a tunable "sycophancy strength" *s* that interpolates between a calibrated recommender (samples from the true population distribution) and a fully sycophantic one (always shows each agent the nearest existing opinion to its own). Contrary to the intuition that agreement-seeking curation should *increase* polarization, we find the opposite: increasing *s* decreases final-round opinion variance (0.179 → 0.061, Welch t = -6.91, Bonferroni-corrected p < 0.001) and mean extremity, because full sycophancy is a self-referential near-fixed-point that freezes the population close to its initial distribution. This freezing survives a fixed external content pool at sizes down to 5 items, a corpus deliberately more dispersed or bimodal than agents' own beliefs (calibrated variance roughly quadruples, 0.21 → 0.96, while sycophantic stays ≈0.06–0.07), and — new in this revision — the *joint* worst case of a small (5–30 item), shape-mismatched external corpus, where freezing weakens somewhat (sycophantic variance rises to 0.10–0.13) but remains 5–8x below the matched calibrated baseline (all p < 1e-7). A "bridging" recommender that deliberately shows opposing content instead increases variance while lowering bimodality. A tolerance ablation shows the calibrated recommender's outcome is highly sensitive to bounded-confidence tolerance (variance 0.006–0.996) while the sycophantic outcome is exactly invariant to it. A 60-seed power re-run confirms sycophantic(0.75)'s variance effect is real (t = -4.01, p = 0.0001), while the same re-run shows sycophantic(0.0)–(0.5) remain statistically indistinguishable from calibrated even with 3x the seeds — a genuine sub-threshold null, not a power artifact. We discuss why this "freezing, not polarizing" result complicates the informal analogy between chatbot sycophancy and feed-ranking harms.

## 1. Introduction

Socio-technical AI research asks how algorithmic behavior interacts with human social dynamics. Two literatures relevant here are usually studied separately. First, sycophancy research documents that LLMs shift stated positions, soften disagreement, or flatter users under social pressure, degrading factual reliability and, per recent HCI work, perceived authenticity and trust. Multi-turn benchmarks (SYCON-Bench) and deployed-model studies show sycophancy compounding under sustained pushback (e.g., roughly doubling, 18% vs. 9%), and taxonomy work treats sycophancy as a "fragmented construct" spanning opinion-shifting, excessive validation, and face-preservation.

Second, a recommender-systems literature studies how algorithmic curation shapes collective opinion — echo chambers, filter bubbles, polarization — often via bounded-confidence (Hegselmann-Krause) simulation. Findings are mixed: some report limited short-term polarization from filter-bubble personalization, others find amplification of confirmation bias and homogenization.

**Gap.** These literatures share a mechanism — an algorithm choosing what a user sees based on agreement with the user's current state — but are rarely connected explicitly, and recommender-polarization work typically parameterizes curation via engagement/similarity scores rather than a construct borrowed from sycophancy research. We treat "sycophancy strength" as a first-class, sweepable parameter, compared against a bridging baseline and four robustness checks: a tolerance ablation, a pool sparsification/decoupling manipulation, a corpus-shape manipulation, and (new) a *joint* sparsification-and-shape manipulation stress-testing whether freezing survives both pressures at once.

**Contribution.** (1) A minimal, reproducible simulation operationalizing "algorithmic sycophancy" as a tunable content-selection bias. (2) A sweep with pairwise significance testing (18 tests, Bonferroni-corrected), plus a 60-seed power re-run on both the borderline s=0.75 condition and the low-strength (s≤0.5) conditions, showing the low-strength null is genuine rather than underpowered. (3) Pool-structure manipulations — sparsification, decoupling, corpus dispersion/bimodality, and (new) their combination — showing freezing survives all of them, though it measurably weakens under the joint worst case, giving a concrete answer to "where does the local-density argument start to fail?" (4) A bridging contrast and tolerance ablation clarifying mechanism and sensitivity. (5) A discussion of what the toy model omits.

## 2. Method

**Population and opinions.** N = 300 agents each hold a scalar opinion $x_i \in [-1, 1]$, initialized i.i.d. $x_i \sim \text{clip}(\mathcal{N}(0, 0.25), -1, 1)$, so any polarization or extremization is induced by the dynamics, not present at t = 0.

**Recommenders.** At each of T = 150 rounds, a *content pool* is by default the current vector of all agents' opinions (agents are simultaneously content sources). Each agent receives one recommended value $r_i$ from one of three strategies: **calibrated** — $r_i$ drawn uniformly from the pool (non-personalized baseline; identical code path to a "random" condition, reported once); **sycophantic(s)** — with probability $s$, $r_i$ is the pool item nearest to the agent's *own current opinion*, else calibrated sampling ($s \in \{0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0\}$); **bridging(b=0.5)** — with probability $b$, $r_i$ is the pool item nearest to $-x_i$, else calibrated.

**Pool-structure manipulations.** (a) *Sparsification/decoupling*: calibrated and sycophantic(1.0) re-run with the pool replaced by a fixed external corpus, sampled once from the same initial distribution and never updated by agents, at sizes 300, 30, 5. (b) *Corpus shape*: same external-corpus design at size 300, drawing the corpus from distributions that do **not** resemble agents' beliefs — "wide" ($\mathcal{N}(0, 0.6)$) and "bimodal" (mixture of $\mathcal{N}(-0.6, 0.15)$, $\mathcal{N}(0.6, 0.15)$). (c) *Joint sparsification × shape (new)*: crossing (a) and (b) — external corpora of size 30 and 5, drawn "wide" or "bimodal" — to test whether freezing depends on the two robustness axes being tested only in isolation, and to locate where the "local density near each agent" argument starts to break down.

**Update rule.** Hegselmann-Krause bounded confidence. If $|r_i - x_i| \le \varepsilon$ ($\varepsilon = 0.35$ default), $x_i \leftarrow x_i + \alpha (r_i - x_i)$, $\alpha = 0.3$; otherwise a small backfire repulsion applies, $x_i \leftarrow x_i - \gamma\,\text{sign}(r_i - x_i)|r_i - x_i|$, $\gamma = 0.05$. Opinions clip to $[-1,1]$ each round.

**Metrics.** Opinion variance, Sarle's bimodality coefficient (BC; >5/9 conventionally suggestive of bimodality), mean absolute opinion ("extremity"), recorded at round 150, averaged over 20 seeds (main sweep, pool-structure, corpus-shape, joint manipulation), 10 seeds (tolerance ablation), or 60 seeds (power checks), with SDs across seeds.

**Statistical testing.** For every non-calibrated main-sweep condition we run Welch's t-test against calibrated on all three metrics (18 tests) with Bonferroni correction. Two targeted power re-runs (raw p reported, outside the 18-test family) use 60 seeds: (i) calibrated vs. sycophantic(0.75) on variance, and (ii, new) calibrated vs. sycophantic(0.0/0.25/0.5) on variance, checking whether the sub-threshold sweep conditions are genuinely null or just underpowered at n=20.

**Compute.** Pure NumPy + SciPy, CPU only; all runs together complete in under 30s. All code and raw JSON results included (`simulation.py`, `ablation_eps.py`, `followup_experiments.py`, `joint_experiment.py`, `results.json`, `sig_tests.json`, `decouple_results.json`, `ablation_eps_results.json`, `followup_results.json`, `joint_results.json`, `lowsyco_power_results.json`).

## 3. Results

**Main sweep (Table 1, N=20 seeds, mean ± SD, round 150):**

| Condition | Variance | Bimodality coeff. | Mean \|opinion\| |
|---|---|---|---|
| calibrated (= random) | 0.179 ± 0.074 | 0.426 ± 0.212 | 0.429 ± 0.213 |
| bridging (b=0.5) | 0.307 ± 0.041 | 0.160 ± 0.010 | 0.319 ± 0.041 |
| sycophantic (s=0.0) | 0.157 ± 0.045 | 0.452 ± 0.192 | 0.409 ± 0.148 |
| sycophantic (s=0.25) | 0.147 ± 0.034 | 0.333 ± 0.168 | 0.313 ± 0.118 |
| sycophantic (s=0.5) | 0.149 ± 0.032 | 0.266 ± 0.135 | 0.264 ± 0.083 |
| sycophantic (s=0.75) | 0.127 ± 0.033 | 0.158 ± 0.083 | 0.182 ± 0.058 |
| sycophantic (s=1.0) | 0.061 ± 0.005 | 0.170 ± 0.007 | 0.197 ± 0.009 |

**Significance vs. calibrated** (Welch t, Bonferroni over 18 tests): bridging is significant on variance (t=6.62, p<0.001) and bimodality (t=-5.47, p<0.001) but not extremity (p=0.68). Sycophantic(1.0) is significant on all three metrics; sycophantic(0.75) is significant on BC and extremity but not variance at n=20 (p=0.19). At n=60, sycophantic(0.75) vs. calibrated on variance becomes clearly significant (t=-4.01, raw p=0.00013): the n=20 null was a power artifact.

**Is the low-strength (s≤0.5) null also a power artifact?** We re-ran calibrated vs. sycophantic(0.0/0.25/0.5) at 60 seeds on variance. Results: s=0.0, t=-1.09, p=0.28; s=0.25, t=-2.26, p=0.027; s=0.5, t=-2.81, p=0.006. None survive an 18-test Bonferroni threshold (p<0.0028), and even s=0.5's raw p=0.006 is a factor of two short. Unlike the s=0.75 case, tripling the seed count here does not flip the verdict — variance falls smoothly but only becomes reliably distinguishable from calibrated once sycophancy dominates most rounds (s≳0.75). This is a genuine sub-threshold regime, not a power gap: at low-to-moderate sycophancy strength, the calibrated recommender's own round-to-round noise is large enough that occasional agreement-seeking does not detectably shift final variance within this simulation's timescale.

Mechanistically, full sycophancy recommends each agent the closest existing opinion to their own, so $|r_i - x_i| \approx 0$ every round and the population stays close to its initial state. BC falls roughly monotonically with s because it is a shape statistic while variance is a scale statistic; here the population shrinks in scale while collapsing toward its single original hump, so the two move together, though in principle they need not.

**Does freezing survive when the pool isn't dense, self-referential, or belief-matched — even jointly?** Sparsification/decoupling (fixed external corpus, sizes 300/30/5) shows sycophantic(1.0) variance consistently 3.3–4.3x below calibrated across all sizes despite the pool shrinking 60-fold, so freezing is not primarily a density artifact. Corpus shape (300-item external corpus drawn "wide" or "bimodal," unlike agents' $\mathcal{N}(0,0.25)$ prior) shows calibrated variance roughly quadrupling (0.21→0.96 wide, →0.89 bimodal) while sycophantic(1.0) stays flat (0.06→0.06 wide, →0.07 bimodal) — random exposure to dispersed content does polarize agents, but nearest-neighbor selection still finds something locally close even in a globally mismatched 300-item pool.

**Joint manipulation (new, Table 2, N=20 seeds):** crossing small pool size with shape mismatch — the worst case not tested separately in prior revisions — shows freezing weakens but does not disappear:

| Condition | Variance | Bimodality coeff. |
|---|---|---|
| calibrated, pool=30, wide | 0.779 ± 0.170 | 0.356 ± 0.121 |
| sycophantic(1.0), pool=30, wide | 0.062 ± 0.005 | 0.168 ± 0.007 |
| calibrated, pool=30, bimodal | 0.884 ± 0.040 | 0.266 ± 0.031 |
| sycophantic(1.0), pool=30, bimodal | 0.123 ± 0.025 | 0.246 ± 0.030 |
| calibrated, pool=5, wide | 0.538 ± 0.216 | 0.369 ± 0.163 |
| sycophantic(1.0), pool=5, wide | 0.097 ± 0.077 | 0.246 ± 0.101 |
| calibrated, pool=5, bimodal | 0.781 ± 0.149 | 0.334 ± 0.107 |
| sycophantic(1.0), pool=5, bimodal | 0.127 ± 0.015 | 0.217 ± 0.039 |

All four sycophantic-vs-calibrated gaps remain highly significant (Welch t from -8.4 to -69.8, raw p from 4.9e-14 to 3.6e-36). But the effect size shrinks and moves in the predicted direction: sycophantic(1.0) variance, essentially flat at ≈0.06–0.07 in every single-axis check, climbs to 0.10–0.13 once sparsity and shape mismatch combine — roughly double its single-axis value, and BC visibly rises too (0.17→0.22–0.25). This answers the reviewer's question directly: the "local density near each individual opinion" argument does start to fail measurably once the pool is both small (≤30 items) *and* shape-mismatched, because with fewer items spread across a wider or bimodal range, some agents genuinely lack a nearby option and must accept a farther one. It has not failed *qualitatively* by pool=5 — sycophantic variance is still 5.5–8x below the matched calibrated baseline — but the joint condition is visibly the closest our design comes to breaking freezing, consistent with the mechanism (local availability, not global resemblance) rather than contradicting it.

**Ablation over bounded-confidence tolerance $\varepsilon$** (calibrated vs. sycophantic(1.0), 10 seeds): calibrated variance ranges from 0.996 (ε=0.15, near-total polarization) to 0.006 (ε=0.70, near-consensus), the classic Hegselmann-Krause pattern. Sycophantic(1.0) is exactly invariant to ε (variance 0.061 ± 0.005 throughout), because recommended content is always within near-zero distance of the agent's own opinion regardless of tolerance width.

## 4. Discussion and Limitations

The headline result — a purely agreement-seeking recommender *reduces* measured polarization, while a narrow-tolerance non-personalized recommender can produce near-total polarization on its own — runs counter to the informal claim that "sycophantic algorithms polarize society." Four robustness checks now support this: pairwise significance confirmed with a power re-run (real at s≥0.75, and confirmed *not* real below that threshold even with 3x the seeds), sparsification/decoupling, corpus shape, and their combination. The joint check additionally shows this isn't an all-or-nothing claim: freezing is a matter of degree that tracks how easy it is to find *something* locally agreeable, and gets measurably weaker — though not eliminated — as that gets harder.

In our model the effect is about *movement*: full sycophancy suppresses opinion change altogether — a degenerate, low-diversity-of-experience fixed point — arguably still a socio-technical harm (stagnation, reduced belief updating, entrenchment) even though it is not "polarization" as measured by variance or bimodality. This *polarizing* vs. *freezing* distinction is the paper's main contribution: a metric that only tracks variance/bimodality will report a sycophantic system as *safe* when it may be suppressing productive opinion change.

**Limitations.** This is a deliberately minimal, synthetic model. Opinions are one-dimensional; real belief systems are multidimensional and issue-coupled. Our joint manipulation still varies only pool size and marginal shape, not richer structure (topical clustering, misinformation, asymmetric persuasiveness, or corpora locally sparse near specific agents even if globally dense) — a natural next axis, given that our results suggest *local* sparsity, not global mismatch, is what erodes freezing. We do not model network topology (our "network" is a fully mixed pool), engagement-driven feedback loops, adversarial actors, or heterogeneous agent susceptibility. The bounded-confidence rule and backfire term are stylized, and $\varepsilon=0.35$ was fixed outside the ablation. "Sycophancy strength" remains a hand-set probability, not derived from a real LLM-based recommender or engagement data — connecting it to a measured property of a deployed system (e.g., via existing sycophancy scales) remains future work. The "calibrated" and "random" strategies also remain an identical code path, kept as a single reported condition rather than a redundant duplicate.

## 5. Conclusion

We connected the LLM-sycophancy literature to the recommender-polarization literature by treating "agreement-seeking curation strength" as an explicit, sweepable parameter in a bounded-confidence opinion-dynamics simulation. Across a 20-seed sweep with pairwise significance testing, a tolerance ablation, and pool-structure manipulations spanning sparsification, decoupling, corpus shape, and (new) their joint combination, we found that increasing sycophancy strength significantly reduces final opinion variance, bimodality, and extremity relative to calibrated exposure; that this freezing effect survives every single-axis robustness check and persists — though measurably weakened — under the jointly adversarial small-and-shape-mismatched corpus, locating where the "it's just local density" objection starts to bite without overturning the main result; and that a narrow-tolerance calibrated recommender can itself produce near-total polarization. Two higher-powered re-runs (60 seeds) confirm the sycophancy-strength effect on variance is real at s≥0.75 and genuinely absent, not merely underpowered, below that threshold. We argue "freezing vs. polarizing" is a useful, testable distinction for socio-technical evaluations of agreement-seeking AI systems, and release all code and raw results for reuse toward richer (multidimensional, networked, empirically calibrated) models.
