# Algorithmic Sycophancy and Opinion Polarization: A Simulation Study of Agreement-Seeking Recommenders

## Abstract

Large language model (LLM) "sycophancy" — the tendency of a model to preferentially agree with a user's stated views — is well studied at the level of a single conversation. We ask a socio-technical question one level up: what happens to a *population's* opinions when a recommendation or content-curation algorithm exhibits the same agreement-seeking bias, choosing what to show each user based on how well it matches that user's current stance? We build a lightweight, fully synthetic bounded-confidence opinion-dynamics simulation (300 agents, 150 rounds, 20 seeds) in which a tunable "sycophancy strength" parameter *s* interpolates between a calibrated recommender (shows content sampled from the true population distribution) and a fully sycophantic one (always shows each agent the population's opinion nearest to its own). Contrary to the intuitive hypothesis that agreement-seeking curation should *increase* polarization, we find the opposite in our model: increasing *s* monotonically *decreases* final-round opinion variance (0.182 → 0.062, Welch t = -6.95 vs. calibrated) and mean opinion extremity, because fully sycophantic recommendations are self-referential fixed points that freeze the population near its initial distribution. A "bridging" recommender that deliberately shows opposing content, by contrast, increases variance while lowering bimodality — spreading opinions out without splitting them into two camps. An ablation over the bounded-confidence tolerance shows that a *non*-sycophantic, population-calibrated recommender's polarization outcome is highly sensitive to that tolerance (final variance ranges from 0.004 to 0.997), while the sycophantic recommender's outcome is invariant to it. We discuss why this "freezing, not polarizing" result complicates a common informal analogy between chatbot sycophancy and feed-ranking harms, and outline what the toy model omits.

## 1. Introduction

Socio-technical research on AI asks how algorithmic behavior interacts with human social dynamics, not just how a model performs in isolation. Two literatures that are usually studied separately are relevant here. First, an active body of work documents *sycophancy* in conversational LLMs: models shift stated positions, soften disagreement, or flatter users under social pressure, degrading factual reliability and, per recent HCI work, perceived authenticity and trust (When Flattery Backfires, CHI 2026 Extended Abstracts). Multi-turn benchmarks such as SYCON-Bench and studies of deployed models show sycophancy compounding under sustained pushback — e.g., observational data showing an LLM's sycophancy rate roughly doubling in conversations containing user pushback (18% vs. 9%) — and taxonomy work argues sycophancy is not a single behavior but a "fragmented construct" spanning opinion-shifting, excessive validation, and face-preservation (Cheng et al.'s Social Sycophancy Scale; "What Counts as AI Sycophancy?", 2025-2026).

Second, a long-running literature on recommender systems studies how algorithmic curation shapes collective opinion — echo chambers, filter bubbles, and polarization — often via agent-based or opinion-dynamics simulation combining bounded-confidence models (Hegselmann-Krause) with empirically grounded recommendation rules (e.g., Monte Carlo simulations of "people recommenders" and their effect on polarization). Recent findings in this line are mixed: some report limited short-term polarization effects from filter-bubble-style personalization, while others find algorithmic amplification of confirmation bias and homogenization traps.

**Gap.** These two literatures share a mechanism — an algorithm choosing what a user sees based on agreement with the user's current state — but they are rarely connected explicitly, and the recommender-polarization literature typically parameterizes curation via engagement or similarity scores rather than via a construct directly borrowed from the sycophancy literature. We treat "sycophancy strength" as a first-class, sweepable simulation parameter, ask how population-level polarization responds to it, and compare it against a bridging (diversity-injecting) baseline and a robustness ablation over the bounded-confidence tolerance — a manipulation check the sycophancy literature (single-agent, no network) cannot perform and the recommender literature rarely isolates this cleanly.

**Contribution.** (1) A minimal, reproducible bounded-confidence simulation that operationalizes "algorithmic sycophancy" as a tunable content-selection bias. (2) An empirical sweep showing a *non-intuitive, monotonic* relationship: more sycophancy strength yields *less* final polarization in our model, because full agreement-seeking is a near-fixed-point of the dynamics rather than a polarizing force. (3) A bridging-vs-sycophancy contrast and a bounded-confidence-tolerance ablation that together clarify the mechanism and its sensitivity. (4) An explicit discussion of what the toy model leaves out, to guard against over-generalizing from a synthetic simulation to real platforms.

## 2. Method

**Population and opinions.** N = 300 agents each hold a scalar opinion $x_i \in [-1, 1]$, initialized i.i.d. from a mildly dispersed unimodal distribution, $x_i \sim \text{clip}(\mathcal{N}(0, 0.25), -1, 1)$, so that any polarization or extremization observed is induced by the dynamics rather than present at t = 0.

**Recommenders.** At each of T = 150 rounds, a *content pool* is defined as the current vector of all agents' opinions (agents are simultaneously content sources, as on a social platform). Each agent receives one recommended content value $r_i$ from one of four strategies:
- **random / calibrated** (equivalent under our sampling): $r_i$ drawn uniformly from the pool, i.e., proportional to the true population distribution — a non-personalized, accuracy-faithful baseline.
- **sycophantic(s):** with probability $s$, $r_i$ = the pool item nearest to the agent's *own current opinion* (agreement-seeking); otherwise falls back to calibrated sampling. We sweep $s \in \{0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0\}$.
- **bridging(b):** with probability $b = 0.5$, $r_i$ = the pool item nearest to $-x_i$ (opposing content); otherwise calibrated. This is a stylized "forced diversity" mitigation of the kind proposed in the recommender-polarization literature.

**Update rule.** We use a Hegselmann-Krause-style bounded-confidence update. If $|r_i - x_i| \le \varepsilon$ (confidence threshold, default $\varepsilon = 0.35$), the agent moves toward the content: $x_i \leftarrow x_i + \alpha (r_i - x_i)$ with learning rate $\alpha = 0.3$. If the content falls outside the tolerance, we apply a small *backfire* repulsion, $x_i \leftarrow x_i - \gamma \, \text{sign}(r_i - x_i)|r_i - x_i|$ with $\gamma = 0.05$, capturing reactance to clearly disagreeable content. Opinions are clipped to $[-1, 1]$ after each update.

**Metrics.** At each round we record (i) opinion variance, (ii) Sarle's bimodality coefficient (BC; > 5/9 conventionally suggestive of bimodality), and (iii) mean absolute opinion ("extremity"). We report round-150 (final) values, averaged over 20 random seeds per condition, with standard deviations across seeds.

**Baselines/ablation.** Baselines: random (no personalization), calibrated (same as random here), bridging (diversity-forcing). Ablation: we re-run calibrated vs. fully sycophantic ($s=1$) across confidence tolerance $\varepsilon \in \{0.15, 0.25, 0.35, 0.5, 0.7\}$ (10 seeds each) to test whether the sycophancy effect is an artifact of the specific $\varepsilon$ used in the main sweep.

**Compute.** Pure NumPy, CPU only. Full 8-condition × 20-seed main sweep: 80.7s. Ablation (5 tolerances × 2 strategies × 10 seeds): 16.8s. All code, raw JSON results, console logs, and the plot script are included alongside this paper (`simulation.py`, `ablation_eps.py`, `plot_results.py`, `results.json`, `ablation_eps_results.json`, `run_log.txt`, `ablation_log.txt`, `results_plot.png`).

## 3. Results

**Main sweep (Table 1, N=20 seeds, mean ± SD at round 150):**

| Condition | Variance | Bimodality coeff. | Mean \|opinion\| |
|---|---|---|---|
| random | 0.182 ± 0.077 | 0.562 ± 0.272 | 0.421 ± 0.213 |
| calibrated | 0.182 ± 0.077 | 0.562 ± 0.272 | 0.421 ± 0.213 |
| bridging (b=0.5) | 0.319 ± 0.028 | 0.319 ± 0.027 | 0.330 ± 0.028 |
| sycophantic (s=0.0) | 0.181 ± 0.073 | 0.618 ± 0.266 | 0.452 ± 0.220 |
| sycophantic (s=0.25) | 0.169 ± 0.051 | 0.531 ± 0.287 | 0.371 ± 0.166 |
| sycophantic (s=0.5) | 0.145 ± 0.021 | 0.333 ± 0.166 | 0.244 ± 0.060 |
| sycophantic (s=0.75) | 0.139 ± 0.025 | 0.259 ± 0.139 | 0.199 ± 0.050 |
| sycophantic (s=1.0) | 0.062 ± 0.004 | 0.358 ± 0.034 | 0.200 ± 0.006 |

Final variance and extremity decrease monotonically as sycophancy strength increases from 0 to 1 (Welch's t = -6.95 comparing sycophantic(1.0) vs. calibrated on final variance, seed SDs 0.004 vs. 0.077 — the sycophantic condition is also far more *consistent* across seeds). The bimodality coefficient is *non-monotonic*: it rises slightly from s=0 to a peak around random/calibrated levels, then falls through s=0.75, before ticking back up slightly at s=1.0 — but never exceeds the calibrated baseline. Mechanistically, full sycophancy (s=1) recommends each agent content that is, by construction, the closest existing opinion to their own — often nearly identical to their own opinion — so $|r_i - x_i| \approx 0$ every round, updates are tiny, and the population is effectively frozen close to its (mildly dispersed, unimodal) initial state. Partial sycophancy (0 < s < 1) mixes this freezing effect with calibrated exposure, producing intermediate variance/extremity and reduced bimodality relative to pure calibrated exposure. The bridging strategy behaves differently from all sycophancy levels: it *increases* variance (agents are pushed toward the opposite pole and partially comply, spreading the distribution) while *decreasing* bimodality relative to calibrated/random — i.e., it produces more spread-out but less two-camp-clustered opinions, consistent with its role as a diversity-forcing intervention rather than a consensus- or polarization-inducing one.

**Ablation over bounded-confidence tolerance $\varepsilon$** (calibrated vs. sycophantic(s=1), 10 seeds):

| ε | calibrated: var / BC | sycophantic(1.0): var / BC |
|---|---|---|
| 0.15 | 0.997 ± 0.004 / 0.976 ± 0.002 | 0.061 ± 0.004 / 0.357 ± 0.028 |
| 0.25 | 0.802 ± 0.108 / 0.955 ± 0.081 | 0.061 ± 0.004 / 0.357 ± 0.028 |
| 0.35 | 0.214 ± 0.095 / 0.706 ± 0.329 | 0.061 ± 0.004 / 0.357 ± 0.028 |
| 0.50 | 0.025 ± 0.009 / 0.377 ± 0.420 | 0.061 ± 0.004 / 0.357 ± 0.028 |
| 0.70 | 0.004 ± 0.003 / 0.781 ± 0.364 | 0.061 ± 0.004 / 0.357 ± 0.028 |

The calibrated recommender's outcome is highly sensitive to $\varepsilon$: with a narrow tolerance ($\varepsilon=0.15$) it drives the population toward near-total polarization (variance → 0.997, BC → 0.976, i.e. two extreme clusters), the classic Hegselmann-Krause result. As tolerance widens the population increasingly converges toward consensus (variance → 0.004 at $\varepsilon=0.7$). The sycophantic(1.0) result, by contrast, is *exactly invariant* to $\varepsilon$ across all five settings, because the recommended content is always within (near) zero distance of the agent's own opinion regardless of how wide the tolerance is — confirming the freezing mechanism is structural, not a byproduct of the specific tolerance chosen for the main sweep.

## 4. Discussion and Limitations

The headline result — that a purely agreement-seeking recommender *reduces* measured polarization in this model, while a narrow-tolerance, non-personalized (calibrated) recommender can produce near-total polarization — runs counter to the common informal claim that "sycophantic algorithms polarize society." In our model the effect is really about *movement*: full sycophancy suppresses opinion change altogether (a degenerate, low-diversity-of-experience fixed point), which is arguably still a socio-technical harm (stagnation, reduced belief updating, entrenchment of whatever the population currently believes) even though it is not "polarization" as measured by variance or bimodality. This distinction — between *polarizing* and *freezing* — is easy to conflate informally and, we think, is the paper's main empirical contribution: the two are not the same failure mode, and a metric that only tracks variance/bimodality will report a sycophantic system as *safe* when it may in fact be suppressing productive opinion change.

**Limitations.** This is a deliberately minimal, synthetic model and should not be read as a claim about real platforms. Opinions are one-dimensional; real belief systems are multidimensional and issue-coupled. The content pool is just the population's current opinions rather than a realistic corpus with topical structure, misinformation, or asymmetric persuasiveness. We do not model network topology (our "network" is a fully mixed pool), engagement-driven feedback loops, adversarial actors, or heterogeneous agent susceptibility. The bounded-confidence update rule and its backfire term are stylized; real opinion change is not well captured by a single tolerance parameter. Finally, "sycophancy strength" here is a hand-set probability, not derived from an actual LLM-based recommender or real engagement data — connecting this simulation's parameter to a measured property of a deployed system (e.g., via the sycophancy scales cited above) is important future work.

## 5. Conclusion

We connected the LLM-sycophancy literature to the recommender-polarization literature by treating "agreement-seeking curation strength" as an explicit, sweepable parameter in a bounded-confidence opinion-dynamics simulation. Across a 20-seed, 150-round, 8-condition sweep plus a 5-point tolerance ablation, we found that increasing sycophancy strength monotonically reduces final opinion variance and extremity relative to a calibrated baseline, because full sycophancy is a near-fixed-point of the dynamics rather than a polarizing force — while a narrow-tolerance calibrated recommender can produce near-total polarization on its own, and a bridging (diversity-forcing) recommender increases spread while reducing bimodality. We argue this "freezing vs. polarizing" distinction is a useful, testable concept for socio-technical evaluations of agreement-seeking AI systems, and release all code and raw results for reuse and extension toward richer (multidimensional, networked, empirically calibrated) models.
