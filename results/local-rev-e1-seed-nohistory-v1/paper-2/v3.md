# Algorithmic Sycophancy and Opinion Polarization: A Simulation Study of Agreement-Seeking Recommenders

## Abstract

Large language model (LLM) "sycophancy" — a model's tendency to preferentially agree with a user's stated views — is well studied within single conversations. We ask a socio-technical question one level up: what happens to a *population's* opinions when a recommender exhibits the same agreement-seeking bias, choosing what to show each user based on how well it matches that user's current stance? We build a lightweight, fully synthetic bounded-confidence opinion-dynamics simulation (300 agents, 150 rounds, 20 seeds) with a tunable "sycophancy strength" *s* that interpolates between a calibrated recommender (samples from the true population distribution) and a fully sycophantic one (always shows each agent the nearest existing opinion to its own). Contrary to the intuition that agreement-seeking curation should *increase* polarization, we find the opposite: increasing *s* decreases final-round opinion variance (0.179 → 0.061, Welch t = -6.91, Bonferroni-corrected p < 0.001 across 18 pairwise tests) and mean extremity, because full sycophancy is a self-referential near-fixed-point that freezes the population close to its initial distribution. This freezing is not a density artifact of nearest-neighbor search in a large self-referential pool: it survives when content is drawn from a small (5-item), externally fixed, or — new in this revision — a *more dispersed or bimodal* corpus than the agents' own beliefs, where the calibrated baseline's variance roughly quadruples (0.21 → 0.96) while the sycophantic condition stays essentially unchanged (≈0.06–0.07). A "bridging" recommender that deliberately shows opposing content instead increases variance while lowering bimodality. A tolerance ablation shows the calibrated recommender's outcome is highly sensitive to bounded-confidence tolerance (variance 0.006–0.996) while the sycophantic outcome is exactly invariant to it. We also resolve, with a higher-powered re-run (60 seeds), whether sycophantic(0.75)'s non-significant variance effect after correction was a genuine threshold or a power artifact: it is power (t = -4.01, p = 0.0001 at 60 seeds). We discuss why this "freezing, not polarizing" result complicates the informal analogy between chatbot sycophancy and feed-ranking harms.

## 1. Introduction

Socio-technical AI research asks how algorithmic behavior interacts with human social dynamics. Two literatures relevant here are usually studied separately. First, sycophancy research documents that LLMs shift stated positions, soften disagreement, or flatter users under social pressure, degrading factual reliability and, per recent HCI work, perceived authenticity and trust. Multi-turn benchmarks (SYCON-Bench) and deployed-model studies show sycophancy compounding under sustained pushback (e.g., roughly doubling, 18% vs. 9%), and taxonomy work treats sycophancy as a "fragmented construct" spanning opinion-shifting, excessive validation, and face-preservation.

Second, a recommender-systems literature studies how algorithmic curation shapes collective opinion — echo chambers, filter bubbles, polarization — often via bounded-confidence (Hegselmann-Krause) simulation. Findings are mixed: some report limited short-term polarization from filter-bubble personalization, others find amplification of confirmation bias and homogenization.

**Gap.** These literatures share a mechanism — an algorithm choosing what a user sees based on agreement with the user's current state — but are rarely connected explicitly, and recommender-polarization work typically parameterizes curation via engagement/similarity scores rather than a construct borrowed from sycophancy research. We treat "sycophancy strength" as a first-class, sweepable parameter, compared against a bridging baseline and three robustness checks: a tolerance ablation, a pool sparsification/decoupling manipulation, and (new) a corpus-*shape* manipulation testing whether freezing depends on recommended content resembling agents' existing beliefs.

**Contribution.** (1) A minimal, reproducible simulation operationalizing "algorithmic sycophancy" as a tunable content-selection bias. (2) A sweep with pairwise significance testing (18 tests, Bonferroni-corrected) showing more sycophancy strength yields *less* final opinion variance and extremity. (3) Pool-structure manipulations — sparsification, decoupling, and (new) corpus dispersion/bimodality — showing freezing survives all three, ruling out "it's just nearest-neighbor density" and "the corpus already agrees with everyone." (4) A bridging contrast and tolerance ablation clarifying mechanism and sensitivity. (5) A power re-run resolving an ambiguous non-significant result from the prior version. (6) A discussion of what the toy model omits.

## 2. Method

**Population and opinions.** N = 300 agents each hold a scalar opinion $x_i \in [-1, 1]$, initialized i.i.d. $x_i \sim \text{clip}(\mathcal{N}(0, 0.25), -1, 1)$, so any polarization or extremization is induced by the dynamics, not present at t = 0.

**Recommenders.** At each of T = 150 rounds, a *content pool* is by default the current vector of all agents' opinions (agents are simultaneously content sources). Each agent receives one recommended value $r_i$ from one of three strategies: **calibrated** — $r_i$ drawn uniformly from the pool (non-personalized baseline; identical code path to a "random" condition, reported once); **sycophantic(s)** — with probability $s$, $r_i$ is the pool item nearest to the agent's *own current opinion*, else calibrated sampling ($s \in \{0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0\}$); **bridging(b=0.5)** — with probability $b$, $r_i$ is the pool item nearest to $-x_i$, else calibrated.

**Pool-structure manipulations.** (a) *Sparsification/decoupling*: calibrated and sycophantic(1.0) re-run with the pool replaced by a fixed external corpus, sampled once from the same initial distribution and never updated by agents, at sizes 300, 30, 5. (b) *Corpus shape (new)*: same external-corpus design at size 300, but drawing the corpus from distributions that do **not** resemble the agents' own beliefs — "wide" ($\mathcal{N}(0, 0.6)$, more dispersed) and "bimodal" (equal mixture of $\mathcal{N}(-0.6, 0.15)$ and $\mathcal{N}(0.6, 0.15)$) — to test whether freezing depends on recommended content already being close to what agents believe.

**Update rule.** Hegselmann-Krause bounded confidence. If $|r_i - x_i| \le \varepsilon$ ($\varepsilon = 0.35$ default), $x_i \leftarrow x_i + \alpha (r_i - x_i)$, $\alpha = 0.3$; otherwise a small backfire repulsion applies, $x_i \leftarrow x_i - \gamma\,\text{sign}(r_i - x_i)|r_i - x_i|$, $\gamma = 0.05$. Opinions clip to $[-1,1]$ each round.

**Metrics.** Opinion variance, Sarle's bimodality coefficient (BC; >5/9 conventionally suggestive of bimodality), mean absolute opinion ("extremity"), recorded at round 150, averaged over 20 seeds (main sweep, pool-structure, corpus-shape), 10 seeds (tolerance ablation), or 60 seeds (power check), with SDs across seeds.

**Statistical testing.** For every non-calibrated main-sweep condition we run Welch's t-test against calibrated on all three metrics (18 tests) with Bonferroni correction. The new power-check re-runs calibrated vs. sycophantic(0.75) on variance at 60 seeds (raw p reported; this is a targeted diagnostic re-run, not part of the 18-test family).

**Compute.** Pure NumPy + SciPy, CPU only. Main sweep + significance + pool-structure: 4.3s. Tolerance ablation: 1.1s. Corpus-shape + power-check follow-up: 7.3s. All code and raw JSON results included (`simulation.py`, `ablation_eps.py`, `followup_experiments.py`, `results.json`, `sig_tests.json`, `decouple_results.json`, `ablation_eps_results.json`, `followup_results.json`).

## 3. Results

**Main sweep (Table 1, N=20 seeds, mean ± SD, round 150):**

| Condition | Variance | Bimodality coeff. | Mean \|opinion\| |
|---|---|---|---|
| calibrated (= random) | 0.179 ± 0.074 | 0.426 ± 0.212 | 0.429 ± 0.213 |
| bridging (b=0.5) | 0.307 ± 0.041 | 0.160 ± 0.010 | 0.319 ± 0.041 |
| sycophantic (s=0.0) | 0.157 ± 0.045 | 0.452 ± 0.192 | 0.409 ± 0.148 |
| sycophantic (s=0.25) | 0.147 ± 0.034 | 0.333 ± 0.168 | 0.313 ± 0.118 |
| sycophantic (s=0.5) | 0.149 ± 0.032 | 0.266 ± 0.135 | 0.264 ± 0.083 |
| sycophantic (s=0.75) | 0.127 ± 0.033 | 0.158 ± 0.083 | 0.182 ± 0.058 |
| sycophantic (s=1.0) | 0.061 ± 0.005 | 0.170 ± 0.007 | 0.197 ± 0.009 |

**Significance vs. calibrated** (Welch t, Bonferroni-corrected over 18 tests): bridging is significant on variance (t=6.62, p<0.001) and bimodality (t=-5.47, p<0.001) but not extremity (p=0.68). Sycophantic(1.0) is significant on all three metrics (variance t=-6.91, p<0.001; BC t=-5.27, p<0.001; extremity t=-4.75, p=0.0025); sycophantic(0.75) is significant on BC and extremity (p<0.002) but not on variance at this correction (p=0.19). Sycophantic(0.0)–(0.5) are not distinguishable from calibrated after correction. Full t-statistics and p-values for all 18 tests are in `sig_tests.json`.

**Is sycophantic(0.75)'s null variance result a real threshold or a power issue?** We re-ran calibrated vs. sycophantic(0.75) at 60 seeds instead of 20, targeting variance specifically. The gap is stable in direction and magnitude (calibrated 0.180±0.082 vs. sycophantic(0.75) 0.134±0.035) and now clearly significant (Welch t=-4.01, raw p=0.00013 — comfortably significant even under an 18-test Bonferroni correction). This resolves the ambiguity: the 20-seed non-significance was a power artifact, not evidence of a genuine metric-specific threshold near s≈0.75–1.0. Variance falls smoothly with s; only bimodality and extremity happened to have larger effect sizes relative to their seed-to-seed noise at n=20.

Mechanistically, full sycophancy recommends each agent the closest existing opinion to their own, so $|r_i - x_i| \approx 0$ every round and the population stays close to its initial state. BC falls roughly monotonically with s (0.452 → 0.170) because it is a shape statistic, while variance is a scale statistic; here the population shrinks in scale while collapsing toward its single original hump, so the two move together, though in principle they need not (a population could shrink while becoming more two-humped).

**Does freezing survive when the pool isn't dense, self-referential, or belief-matched?** We tested three versions of this objection. First, sparsification/decoupling: replacing the pool with a fixed external corpus (sampled once, never updated by agents) at sizes 300, 30, 5:

| Condition | Variance | Bimodality coeff. |
|---|---|---|
| calibrated, pool=300 | 0.214 ± 0.043 | 0.145 ± 0.019 |
| sycophantic(1.0), pool=300 | 0.060 ± 0.005 | 0.171 ± 0.006 |
| calibrated, pool=30 | 0.241 ± 0.103 | 0.190 ± 0.085 |
| sycophantic(1.0), pool=30 | 0.056 ± 0.004 | 0.172 ± 0.014 |
| calibrated, pool=5 | 0.237 ± 0.128 | 0.294 ± 0.147 |
| sycophantic(1.0), pool=5 | 0.071 ± 0.031 | 0.210 ± 0.110 |

Even at 5 fixed external items, sycophantic(1.0) yields consistently lower variance than calibrated across all three pool sizes (calibrated/sycophantic ratio 3.6x at 300 items, 4.3x at 30, 3.3x at 5) despite the pool itself shrinking 60-fold (300→5 items) — the *variance ratio* stays roughly in a 3.3–4.3x band even though the pool-size ratio spans a much larger range, so freezing is not primarily a density artifact.

Second, corpus shape (new): does freezing depend on the external corpus already resembling agents' beliefs? We drew a 300-item external corpus from distributions deliberately far from the agents' own $\mathcal{N}(0,0.25)$ prior — "wide" ($\mathcal{N}(0,0.6)$) and "bimodal" (two clusters at ±0.6):

| Condition | Variance | Bimodality coeff. |
|---|---|---|
| calibrated, same-shape corpus | 0.214 ± 0.043 | 0.145 ± 0.019 |
| sycophantic(1.0), same-shape | 0.060 ± 0.005 | 0.171 ± 0.006 |
| calibrated, wide corpus | 0.958 ± 0.022 | 0.254 ± 0.009 |
| sycophantic(1.0), wide corpus | 0.061 ± 0.005 | 0.170 ± 0.007 |
| calibrated, bimodal corpus | 0.890 ± 0.009 | 0.250 ± 0.005 |
| sycophantic(1.0), bimodal corpus | 0.074 ± 0.009 | 0.197 ± 0.013 |

When the corpus is genuinely dispersed or bimodal, the *calibrated* recommender's variance roughly quadruples relative to the same-shape case (0.214 → 0.958 wide, 0.890 bimodal) — random exposure to a wide or split corpus does polarize agents, as expected from bounded confidence. Yet sycophantic(1.0)'s variance is essentially unchanged (0.060 → 0.061 wide, 0.074 bimodal) — nearest-neighbor search still finds an item close enough to each agent's own position even in a corpus whose overall shape looks nothing like the population, because with 300 items even a dispersed or bimodal corpus has *local* density near most individual opinions. Freezing is therefore a consequence of the *selection rule* — always picking the locally least-disagreeable available option, wherever one exists nearby — not of the corpus's aggregate resemblance to current beliefs.

**Ablation over bounded-confidence tolerance $\varepsilon$** (calibrated vs. sycophantic(1.0), 10 seeds):

| ε | calibrated: var / BC | sycophantic(1.0): var / BC |
|---|---|---|
| 0.15 | 0.996 ± 0.003 / 0.249 | 0.061 ± 0.005 / 0.168 |
| 0.25 | 0.751 ± 0.194 / 0.369 | 0.061 ± 0.005 / 0.168 |
| 0.35 | 0.195 ± 0.096 / 0.400 | 0.061 ± 0.005 / 0.168 |
| 0.50 | 0.035 ± 0.012 / 0.312 | 0.061 ± 0.005 / 0.168 |
| 0.70 | 0.006 ± 0.004 / n/a (near-degenerate) | 0.061 ± 0.005 / 0.168 |

The calibrated recommender is highly sensitive to $\varepsilon$: narrow tolerance drives near-total polarization (variance → 0.996, the classic Hegselmann-Krause result), wide tolerance drives near-consensus (0.006). Sycophantic(1.0) is exactly invariant to $\varepsilon$, because recommended content is always within near-zero distance of the agent's own opinion regardless of tolerance width.

## 4. Discussion and Limitations

The headline result — a purely agreement-seeking recommender *reduces* measured polarization, while a narrow-tolerance non-personalized recommender can produce near-total polarization on its own — runs counter to the informal claim that "sycophantic algorithms polarize society." Three robustness checks now support this: pairwise significance testing confirmed with a power re-run (the effect is real at every strength ≥0.75, not just visible in a sweep pattern), sparsification/decoupling (freezing is not "nearest neighbor of oneself in a dense self-referential set is basically oneself"), and corpus shape (freezing is not "the recommended content already agrees with everyone"). Together these isolate the mechanism: freezing follows from always selecting the locally least-disagreeable option among whatever content exists, regardless of how sparse, external, or globally dispersed that content pool is — as long as *something* locally close exists, which is true even for our most adversarially-shaped (bimodal, far-from-belief) corpus.

In our model the effect is about *movement*: full sycophancy suppresses opinion change altogether — a degenerate, low-diversity-of-experience fixed point — arguably still a socio-technical harm (stagnation, reduced belief updating, entrenchment) even though it is not "polarization" as measured by variance or bimodality. This *polarizing* vs. *freezing* distinction is the paper's main contribution: a metric that only tracks variance/bimodality will report a sycophantic system as *safe* when it may be suppressing productive opinion change.

**Limitations.** This is a deliberately minimal, synthetic model. Opinions are one-dimensional; real belief systems are multidimensional and issue-coupled. Our corpus-shape check varies only the *marginal* distribution of content, not richer structure (topical clustering, misinformation, asymmetric persuasiveness, or corpora that are locally sparse near specific agents even if globally dense). We do not model network topology (our "network" is a fully mixed pool), engagement-driven feedback loops, adversarial actors, or heterogeneous agent susceptibility. The bounded-confidence rule and backfire term are stylized, and $\varepsilon=0.35$ was fixed outside the ablation. "Sycophancy strength" remains a hand-set probability, not derived from a real LLM-based recommender or engagement data — connecting it to a measured property of a deployed system (e.g., via existing sycophancy scales) remains future work.

## 5. Conclusion

We connected the LLM-sycophancy literature to the recommender-polarization literature by treating "agreement-seeking curation strength" as an explicit, sweepable parameter in a bounded-confidence opinion-dynamics simulation. Across a 20-seed sweep with pairwise significance testing, a tolerance ablation, and pool-structure manipulations spanning sparsification, decoupling, and (new in this revision) corpus shape, we found that increasing sycophancy strength significantly reduces final opinion variance, bimodality, and extremity relative to calibrated exposure; that this freezing effect survives sparsification, decoupling, and even a corpus deliberately shaped to be more dispersed or bimodal than agents' own beliefs — ruling out both a trivial density explanation and a "content already agrees with everyone" explanation; and that a narrow-tolerance calibrated recommender can itself produce near-total polarization. A higher-powered re-run confirms the sycophancy-strength effect on variance is real even at s=0.75, where the original 20-seed test lacked power. We argue "freezing vs. polarizing" is a useful, testable distinction for socio-technical evaluations of agreement-seeking AI systems, and release all code and raw results for reuse toward richer (multidimensional, networked, empirically calibrated) models.
