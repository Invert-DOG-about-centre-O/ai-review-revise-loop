# Algorithmic Sycophancy and Opinion Polarization: A Simulation Study of Agreement-Seeking Recommenders

## Abstract

Large language model (LLM) "sycophancy" — the tendency of a model to preferentially agree with a user's stated views — is well studied at the level of a single conversation. We ask a socio-technical question one level up: what happens to a *population's* opinions when a recommendation or content-curation algorithm exhibits the same agreement-seeking bias, choosing what to show each user based on how well it matches that user's current stance? We build a lightweight, fully synthetic bounded-confidence opinion-dynamics simulation (300 agents, 150 rounds, 20 seeds) in which a tunable "sycophancy strength" parameter *s* interpolates between a calibrated recommender (shows content sampled from the true population distribution) and a fully sycophantic one (always shows each agent the population's opinion nearest to its own). Contrary to the intuitive hypothesis that agreement-seeking curation should *increase* polarization, we find the opposite in our model: increasing *s* decreases final-round opinion variance (0.179 → 0.061, Welch t = -6.91, Bonferroni-corrected p < 0.001 across 18 pairwise tests) and mean opinion extremity, because fully sycophantic recommendations are self-referential fixed points that freeze the population near its initial distribution. We show this freezing is *not* merely a density artifact of nearest-neighbor search in a large self-referential pool: it persists essentially unchanged when the recommended content is drawn from a small (5-item) or externally fixed corpus decoupled from the agent population. A "bridging" recommender that deliberately shows opposing content, by contrast, significantly increases variance while lowering bimodality — spreading opinions out without splitting them into two camps. An ablation over the bounded-confidence tolerance shows a *non*-sycophantic, calibrated recommender's polarization outcome is highly sensitive to that tolerance (final variance ranges from 0.006 to 0.996), while the sycophantic recommender's outcome is exactly invariant to it. We discuss why this "freezing, not polarizing" result complicates a common informal analogy between chatbot sycophancy and feed-ranking harms, and outline what the toy model omits.

## 1. Introduction

Socio-technical research on AI asks how algorithmic behavior interacts with human social dynamics, not just how a model performs in isolation. Two literatures that are usually studied separately are relevant here. First, an active body of work documents *sycophancy* in conversational LLMs: models shift stated positions, soften disagreement, or flatter users under social pressure, degrading factual reliability and, per recent HCI work, perceived authenticity and trust (When Flattery Backfires, CHI 2026 Extended Abstracts). Multi-turn benchmarks such as SYCON-Bench and studies of deployed models show sycophancy compounding under sustained pushback — e.g., observational data showing an LLM's sycophancy rate roughly doubling in conversations containing user pushback (18% vs. 9%) — and taxonomy work argues sycophancy is not a single behavior but a "fragmented construct" spanning opinion-shifting, excessive validation, and face-preservation (Cheng et al.'s Social Sycophancy Scale; "What Counts as AI Sycophancy?", 2025-2026).

Second, a long-running literature on recommender systems studies how algorithmic curation shapes collective opinion — echo chambers, filter bubbles, and polarization — often via agent-based or opinion-dynamics simulation combining bounded-confidence models (Hegselmann-Krause) with empirically grounded recommendation rules. Recent findings in this line are mixed: some report limited short-term polarization effects from filter-bubble-style personalization, while others find algorithmic amplification of confirmation bias and homogenization traps.

**Gap.** These two literatures share a mechanism — an algorithm choosing what a user sees based on agreement with the user's current state — but they are rarely connected explicitly, and the recommender-polarization literature typically parameterizes curation via engagement or similarity scores rather than a construct borrowed from the sycophancy literature. We treat "sycophancy strength" as a first-class, sweepable parameter and compare it against a bridging (diversity-injecting) baseline and two robustness checks: a bounded-confidence-tolerance ablation, and a content-pool-structure manipulation (pool size, and self-referential vs. externally fixed corpus) that tests whether the freezing result is a trivial consequence of dense nearest-neighbor search rather than a real dynamical effect.

**Contribution.** (1) A minimal, reproducible bounded-confidence simulation operationalizing "algorithmic sycophancy" as a tunable content-selection bias. (2) An empirical sweep, now with full pairwise significance testing across all conditions and metrics, showing more sycophancy strength yields *less* final opinion variance and extremity. (3) A pool-structure manipulation showing the freezing effect survives sparsification and decoupling of the content source from the population, ruling out the simplest "it's just nearest-neighbor density" explanation. (4) A bridging-vs-sycophancy contrast and a tolerance ablation clarifying the mechanism and its sensitivity. (5) A discussion of what the toy model omits, to guard against over-generalizing from simulation to real platforms.

## 2. Method

**Population and opinions.** N = 300 agents each hold a scalar opinion $x_i \in [-1, 1]$, initialized i.i.d. from a mildly dispersed unimodal distribution, $x_i \sim \text{clip}(\mathcal{N}(0, 0.25), -1, 1)$, so that any polarization or extremization observed is induced by the dynamics rather than present at t = 0.

**Recommenders.** At each of T = 150 rounds, a *content pool* is (by default) the current vector of all agents' opinions (agents are simultaneously content sources, as on a social platform). Each agent receives one recommended content value $r_i$ from one of four strategies:
- **calibrated** (subsumes the "random" condition, which is the identical code path — uniform sampling from the pool — so we report it once rather than as two rows): $r_i$ drawn uniformly from the pool, proportional to the true population distribution, a non-personalized baseline.
- **sycophantic(s):** with probability $s$, $r_i$ = the pool item nearest to the agent's *own current opinion* (agreement-seeking); otherwise falls back to calibrated sampling. We sweep $s \in \{0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0\}$.
- **bridging(b):** with probability $b = 0.5$, $r_i$ = the pool item nearest to $-x_i$ (opposing content); otherwise calibrated.

**Pool-structure manipulation (new).** To test whether nearest-neighbor freezing is a trivial artifact of a dense, self-referential pool (300 items drawn from the same population being updated), we re-run calibrated and sycophantic(1.0) with the pool replaced by a **fixed external corpus** sampled once at t=0 from the same initial distribution and *never updated by agent behavior*, at three sizes: 300, 30, and 5 items.

**Update rule.** Hegselmann-Krause-style bounded confidence. If $|r_i - x_i| \le \varepsilon$ ($\varepsilon = 0.35$ default), the agent moves toward the content: $x_i \leftarrow x_i + \alpha (r_i - x_i)$, $\alpha = 0.3$. Otherwise a small backfire repulsion applies: $x_i \leftarrow x_i - \gamma\,\text{sign}(r_i - x_i)|r_i - x_i|$, $\gamma = 0.05$. Opinions clip to $[-1,1]$ each round.

**Metrics.** Opinion variance, Sarle's bimodality coefficient (BC; >5/9 conventionally suggestive of bimodality), and mean absolute opinion ("extremity"), recorded at round 150, averaged over 20 seeds (main sweep) or 10 seeds (ablations), with SDs across seeds.

**Statistical testing (expanded).** For every non-calibrated condition (bridging, sycophantic at each of 5 strengths) we now run Welch's t-test against calibrated on *all three* metrics (18 tests total) and apply a Bonferroni correction (multiply raw p by 18).

**Compute.** Pure NumPy + SciPy, CPU only. Main sweep + significance tests + pool-structure experiment: 4.3s. Tolerance ablation: 1.1s. All code and raw JSON results are included (`simulation.py`, `ablation_eps.py`, `results.json`, `sig_tests.json`, `decouple_results.json`, `ablation_eps_results.json`).

## 3. Results

**Main sweep (Table 1, N=20 seeds, mean ± SD at round 150):**

| Condition | Variance | Bimodality coeff. | Mean \|opinion\| |
|---|---|---|---|
| calibrated (= random) | 0.179 ± 0.074 | 0.426 ± 0.212 | 0.429 ± 0.213 |
| bridging (b=0.5) | 0.307 ± 0.041 | 0.160 ± 0.010 | 0.319 ± 0.041 |
| sycophantic (s=0.0) | 0.157 ± 0.045 | 0.452 ± 0.192 | 0.409 ± 0.148 |
| sycophantic (s=0.25) | 0.147 ± 0.034 | 0.333 ± 0.168 | 0.313 ± 0.118 |
| sycophantic (s=0.5) | 0.149 ± 0.032 | 0.266 ± 0.135 | 0.264 ± 0.083 |
| sycophantic (s=0.75) | 0.127 ± 0.033 | 0.158 ± 0.083 | 0.182 ± 0.058 |
| sycophantic (s=1.0) | 0.061 ± 0.005 | 0.170 ± 0.007 | 0.197 ± 0.009 |

**Significance vs. calibrated** (Welch t, Bonferroni-corrected across 18 tests): bridging is significant on variance (t=6.62, p<0.001) and bimodality (t=-5.47, p<0.001) but not extremity (p=0.68) at this correction level. Sycophantic(1.0) is significant on all three metrics (variance t=-6.91 p<0.001; BC t=-5.27 p<0.001; extremity t=-4.75 p=0.0025), as is sycophantic(0.75) on BC and extremity (p<0.002) though not yet on variance at this strict correction (p=0.19). Sycophantic(0.0)–(0.5) are not distinguishable from calibrated after correction — the freezing effect is a real, statistically robust phenomenon concentrated at high sycophancy strength, not an artifact visible only in uncorrected comparisons. Full per-metric t-statistics and both raw and Bonferroni-corrected p-values for all 18 tests are in `sig_tests.json`.

Mechanistically, full sycophancy (s=1) recommends each agent content that is, by construction, the closest existing opinion to their own, so $|r_i - x_i| \approx 0$ every round, updates are tiny, and the population is effectively frozen close to its initial state. On bimodality: BC falls roughly monotonically with s (0.452 → 0.170) rather than tracking variance's steeper drop, because BC is a *shape* statistic (it asks whether the distribution looks two-humped relative to its own spread) while variance is a *scale* statistic. As s rises, the population doesn't just shrink — it collapses toward a single, still mildly-dispersed hump around the unimodal prior, so BC and variance move together here, but they need not: a population could shrink in scale while *becoming more* two-humped (high s, low variance, high BC) if agents froze near two nearby local clusters rather than one. That variance and BC track together in our results is itself informative — it confirms sycophancy is suppressing movement broadly rather than locking in a specific bimodal configuration.

**Does freezing survive when the pool isn't dense and self-referential?** A natural objection is that sycophantic(1.0)'s "nearest pool item" is trivially close to $x_i$ whenever the pool is a dense sample of the same 300 opinions being updated — freezing would then be a near-tautological consequence of nearest-neighbor search, not a distinctive dynamical finding. We tested this directly by replacing the self-referential pool with a **fixed external corpus**, sampled once and never updated by the agents, at three sizes:

| Condition | Variance | Bimodality coeff. |
|---|---|---|
| calibrated, pool=300 (external) | 0.214 ± 0.043 | 0.145 ± 0.019 |
| sycophantic(1.0), pool=300 (external) | 0.060 ± 0.005 | 0.171 ± 0.006 |
| calibrated, pool=30 (external) | 0.241 ± 0.103 | 0.190 ± 0.085 |
| sycophantic(1.0), pool=30 (external) | 0.056 ± 0.004 | 0.172 ± 0.014 |
| calibrated, pool=5 (external) | 0.237 ± 0.128 | 0.294 ± 0.147 |
| sycophantic(1.0), pool=5 (external) | 0.071 ± 0.031 | 0.210 ± 0.110 |

Even with only **5 fixed external content items** — far too sparse for "nearest neighbor is essentially the agent's own opinion" to hold by density alone — sycophantic(1.0) still yields roughly 3x lower final variance than calibrated (0.071 vs. 0.237) and this gap is consistent across pool sizes down to a factor-of-4 sparsification. Freezing therefore is not primarily a density artifact of large self-referential pools; even coarse-grained, externally fixed content, when *selected* toward the agent's own position, is enough to sharply suppress opinion movement relative to random exposure. It is best understood as a consequence of the *selection rule* — always picking the locally least-disagreeable available option — rather than of pool density per se.

**Ablation over bounded-confidence tolerance $\varepsilon$** (calibrated vs. sycophantic(1.0), 10 seeds):

| ε | calibrated: var / BC | sycophantic(1.0): var / BC |
|---|---|---|
| 0.15 | 0.996 ± 0.003 / 0.249 | 0.061 ± 0.005 / 0.168 |
| 0.25 | 0.751 ± 0.194 / 0.369 | 0.061 ± 0.005 / 0.168 |
| 0.35 | 0.195 ± 0.096 / 0.400 | 0.061 ± 0.005 / 0.168 |
| 0.50 | 0.035 ± 0.012 / 0.312 | 0.061 ± 0.005 / 0.168 |
| 0.70 | 0.006 ± 0.004 / n/a (near-degenerate) | 0.061 ± 0.005 / 0.168 |

The calibrated recommender's outcome is highly sensitive to $\varepsilon$: a narrow tolerance (0.15) drives near-total polarization (variance → 0.996, the classic Hegselmann-Krause result), while wide tolerance drives near-consensus (variance → 0.006; BC is undefined at $\varepsilon=0.7$ because the population is numerically near-degenerate). The sycophantic(1.0) result is *exactly invariant* to $\varepsilon$ (var and BC identical to displayed precision across all five settings), because the recommended content is always within near-zero distance of the agent's own opinion regardless of tolerance width — confirming the freezing mechanism is structural, not a byproduct of the tolerance chosen for the main sweep.

## 4. Discussion and Limitations

The headline result — that a purely agreement-seeking recommender *reduces* measured polarization, while a narrow-tolerance, non-personalized recommender can produce near-total polarization on its own — runs counter to the common informal claim that "sycophantic algorithms polarize society." Two robustness checks added in this revision matter here: pairwise significance testing (the effect is not just a sweep pattern but statistically robust, strongest at high s) and a pool-structure manipulation (the effect is not simply "nearest neighbor of oneself in a dense self-referential set is basically oneself"). The latter isolates the mechanism: freezing follows from *always selecting the locally least-disagreeable option*, not from having many near-duplicate options to select from.

In our model the effect is really about *movement*: full sycophancy suppresses opinion change altogether (a degenerate, low-diversity-of-experience fixed point), arguably still a socio-technical harm (stagnation, reduced belief updating, entrenchment of current beliefs) even though it is not "polarization" as measured by variance or bimodality. This *polarizing* vs. *freezing* distinction is easy to conflate informally and remains the paper's main contribution: a metric that only tracks variance/bimodality will report a sycophantic system as *safe* when it may be suppressing productive opinion change.

**Limitations.** This is a deliberately minimal, synthetic model, not a claim about real platforms. Opinions are one-dimensional; real belief systems are multidimensional and issue-coupled. Our decoupled-corpus check only breaks the *self-referential* link, not more realistic corpus structure (topical clustering, misinformation, asymmetric persuasiveness). We do not model network topology (our "network" is a fully mixed pool), engagement-driven feedback loops, adversarial actors, or heterogeneous agent susceptibility. The bounded-confidence rule and its backfire term are stylized. Finally, "sycophancy strength" is a hand-set probability, not derived from an actual LLM-based recommender or real engagement data — connecting it to a measured property of a deployed system (e.g., via the sycophancy scales cited above) remains future work.

## 5. Conclusion

We connected the LLM-sycophancy literature to the recommender-polarization literature by treating "agreement-seeking curation strength" as an explicit, sweepable parameter in a bounded-confidence opinion-dynamics simulation. Across a 20-seed, 150-round sweep with full pairwise significance testing, a 5-point tolerance ablation, and a new pool-structure manipulation, we found that increasing sycophancy strength significantly reduces final opinion variance, bimodality, and extremity relative to a calibrated baseline; that this freezing effect survives sparsification and decoupling of the content source from the population, ruling out a trivial density explanation; and that a narrow-tolerance calibrated recommender can produce near-total polarization on its own while a bridging recommender increases spread while reducing bimodality. We argue this "freezing vs. polarizing" distinction is a useful, testable concept for socio-technical evaluations of agreement-seeking AI systems, and release all code and raw results for reuse and extension toward richer (multidimensional, networked, empirically calibrated) models.
