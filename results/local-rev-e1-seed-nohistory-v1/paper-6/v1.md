# Engagement-Optimized Recommenders Amplify Polarization More Than Homophily Alone: An Agent-Based Study

## Abstract

Algorithmic content curation is widely blamed for online polarization, but empirical disentangling of *why* is hard: is it simply that recommenders show people opinions similar to their own (homophily), or is it specifically the optimization for engagement — which rewards extreme, attention-grabbing content — that drives extra polarization? We build a lightweight, fully reproducible agent-based simulation that isolates this question. We extend the classical Hegselmann–Krause bounded-confidence opinion dynamics model with a pluggable "recommender" layer that controls which peer opinions each agent is exposed to each round, drawn from a larger random candidate pool. We compare four exposure policies — random, similarity-based (pure homophily), engagement-optimized (homophily plus a reward for opinion extremity), and bridging (diversity-injecting) — across 200 agents, 150 rounds, and 10 random seeds, with an ablation over the bounded-confidence threshold. The engagement-optimized recommender produces the highest final opinion variance (0.425 ± 0.024 vs. 0.203 ± 0.034 for random and 0.266 ± 0.040 for pure similarity, at the default confidence threshold) and the highest mean opinion extremity (0.657 ± 0.022 vs. 0.438 ± 0.042 for random), while also producing the most insular exposure (echo-chamber index 0.018 vs. 0.444 for random). This gap between similarity-only and engagement-optimized curation — roughly 60% higher final variance — holds across all three confidence thresholds we tested, suggesting that extremity-seeking ranking, not homophily-based filtering per se, is the larger lever on polarization, and hence a more promising target for socio-technical mitigation (e.g., re-ranking penalties on extremity rather than only diversity injection).

## 1. Introduction

The socio-technical framing of recommender systems asks not just "does the algorithm work" but "what social dynamics does the algorithm's optimization target produce when embedded in a population of interacting people." A large body of observational and simulation work links algorithmic curation to opinion polarization and echo chambers, but two distinct mechanisms are often conflated in public discussion: (1) *homophily-driven filtering*, where systems learn users prefer content resembling their existing views and simply serve more of it, and (2) *engagement-driven amplification*, where ranking optimizes a proxy (clicks, watch time, replies) that is empirically correlated with emotionally charged or extreme content, independent of whether that content matches the viewer's prior stance. Distinguishing these matters for mitigation: if homophily alone is the driver, diversity-injection ("bridging") interventions should suffice; if engagement-seeking extremity reward is doing most of the work, penalizing extremity in ranking may matter more than diversity per se.

### 1.1 Related Work

Empirical audits have found that engagement-optimized feeds increase exposure to ideologically aligned content and reduce cross-cutting exposure substantially (on the order of 30–50%) relative to chronological or neutral baselines, and that recommendation feedback loops can produce echo chambers with negative effects on information diversity and social cohesion. Agent-based simulation frameworks such as *EchoChamberSim* model agents with memory and reasoning updated through recommendation-based interactions, and related confirmation-aware social dynamic models study how algorithms that mirror agents' existing beliefs create "homogenization traps." At the same time, some empirical work complicates the simple story: short-term exposure to filter-bubble-style recommendations has been found to produce only limited additional polarization relative to baseline dynamics, and mitigation strategies such as "allostatic regulators" for recommendation algorithms have been proposed specifically to counteract echo-chamber feedback loops. Classical opinion-dynamics theory (DeGroot averaging; Hegselmann–Krause bounded confidence) provides the underlying mathematical machinery most of these simulations build on, but relatively few socio-technical studies isolate the *engagement-extremity* channel from the *homophily-filtering* channel within a single controlled model — most compare an algorithmic condition to a non-algorithmic baseline rather than decomposing the algorithm's objective into separable components. A separate but related literature on LLM sycophancy (e.g., SycEval, the Social Sycophancy Scale, TRUTH DECAY) studies a structurally similar phenomenon at the level of a single human-AI dyad — models drifting toward a user's stated belief — but does not address population-level, multi-agent amplification via a ranking layer, which is the gap we target here.

### 1.2 Contribution

We contribute a small, self-contained, CPU-only agent-based model that (a) reproduces the qualitative echo-chamber and polarization effects reported in the literature using a minimal, auditable mechanism, and (b) decomposes "algorithmic curation" into a similarity component and an orthogonal extremity-reward component, showing their marginal effects separately and their sensitivity to the population's openness to persuasion (the bounded-confidence threshold).

## 2. Method

### 2.1 Opinion dynamics model

We use a population of `n = 200` agents, each holding a scalar opinion `x_i ∈ [-1, 1]` initialized i.i.d. uniform. At each of `T = 150` rounds, every agent `i` is shown a set of `k = 8` peer opinions selected by a recommender policy (below) from a randomly drawn candidate pool of `m = 30` other agents (representing a broader universe of content the platform *could* show). Agent `i` updates its opinion via a bounded-confidence rule (Hegselmann–Krause): among the `k` shown opinions, only those within confidence threshold `ε` of the agent's current opinion are treated as persuasive; the agent moves a fraction `μ = 0.35` of the way toward the mean of those persuasive opinions. Opinions outside `ε` are seen but ignored, capturing the standard bounded-confidence assumption that people discount views too far from their own. Updates are computed synchronously per round from the previous round's opinions (random update order does not affect this since the target is computed before any agent's opinion changes within the round).

### 2.2 Recommender policies

- **Random** (baseline): shows a uniformly random subset of the candidate pool. No algorithmic curation.
- **Similarity**: shows the `k` candidates closest in opinion to the agent's own — pure homophily-based filtering, with no extremity component.
- **Engagement**: scores each candidate as `0.7·(1 − |Δopinion|) + 0.3·|candidate opinion|` and shows the top-`k` — rewards both agreement *and* extremity, modeling ranking that optimizes an engagement proxy correlated with sensational content.
- **Bridging**: shows `k/2` closest candidates and `k/2` candidates from the most distant/opposite part of the opinion spectrum — a diversity-injection intervention of the kind proposed in the mitigation literature.

### 2.3 Metrics

- **Final opinion variance**: population-level polarization at `t = 150`.
- **Mean absolute opinion (extremity)**: how far, on average, opinions sit from the neutral center.
- **Echo-chamber index**: mean `|shown opinion − own opinion|`, averaged over agents and rounds — directly measures how insular each policy's exposure actually is, independent of downstream opinion change.
- **Cluster count**: number of distinct opinion clusters in the final population (adjacent opinions within a 0.08 gap merge), indicating fragmentation into camps vs. two-pole polarization vs. consensus.

### 2.4 Experimental design

*Main experiment*: all four policies × 10 random seeds, default `ε = 0.35`. *Ablation*: all four policies × `ε ∈ {0.2, 0.35, 0.5}` × 5 seeds, testing sensitivity to how open agents are to persuasion by dissimilar views. All code is in `simulation.py` in this directory; raw results are in `results.json` (main) and `ablation_results.json` (ablation), console output and summary tables in `summary.txt`, and trajectory plots in `trajectories.png`. Total simulation runtime was under 70 seconds on CPU.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Main experiment (ε = 0.35, 10 seeds)

| policy | final variance | final extremity | echo-chamber index | # clusters |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| random | 0.203 ± 0.034 | 0.438 ± 0.042 | 0.444 ± 0.035 | 2.10 ± 0.30 |
| similarity | 0.266 ± 0.040 | 0.501 ± 0.062 | 0.029 ± 0.014 | 2.30 ± 0.46 |
| engagement | **0.425 ± 0.024** | **0.657 ± 0.022** | **0.018 ± 0.018** | 2.30 ± 0.46 |
| bridging | 0.250 ± 0.048 | 0.454 ± 0.047 | 0.564 ± 0.049 | **3.20 ± 0.40** |

Three findings stand out. First, all three algorithmic policies raise final variance above the random baseline, but the *engagement* policy raises it far more (+109% over random) than *similarity* alone (+31% over random) — a roughly 60% relative gap between the two curation strategies with identical candidate pools and identical bounded-confidence update rule. Since similarity and engagement share the homophily component and differ only in the added extremity term, this gap is attributable specifically to extremity-seeking ranking, not to homophily-based filtering per se. Second, the echo-chamber index (how close, on average, shown content is to the agent's own opinion) is nearly identical for similarity and engagement (0.029 vs. 0.018) — both are highly insular — yet engagement produces substantially more polarization and extremity despite *not* producing a more insular feed by this metric. This indicates the extremity reward operates through a different channel than pure exposure-similarity: it selectively pulls agents' bounded-confidence "acceptance window" toward the more extreme members of their own similar-opinion neighborhood, dragging the whole neighborhood outward over time. Third, bridging achieves the highest echo-chamber index (least insular, as intended) and does modestly reduce final variance relative to engagement, but it does not reduce variance below the *similarity* condition, and it produces the most fragmented population (3.2 clusters vs. ~2.1–2.3 for the others) — consistent with diversity injection sometimes pushing a population into multiple distinct camps rather than fully re-converging it, rather than a single more moderate consensus.

### 3.2 Ablation over confidence threshold ε

| ε | random var | similarity var | engagement var | bridging var |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.20 | 0.296 ± 0.027 | 0.294 ± 0.037 | 0.333 ± 0.036 | 0.319 ± 0.040 |
| 0.35 | 0.204 ± 0.036 | 0.261 ± 0.044 | **0.436 ± 0.040** | 0.292 ± 0.068 |
| 0.50 | 0.084 ± 0.103 | 0.267 ± 0.092 | **0.437 ± 0.041** | 0.270 ± 0.083 |

At the narrowest confidence threshold (ε = 0.2, agents accept only very similar views), all four policies converge to similarly high variance — when agents are already closed-minded, the recommender's choice matters less because little cross-opinion persuasion happens under any policy. As ε widens (agents become more open to moderately dissimilar views), the random and bridging conditions show *decreasing* final variance (more mixing drives convergence), while the engagement condition's variance stays essentially flat and remains the highest across all thresholds tested (0.33–0.44). This is the paper's central robustness result: engagement-optimized curation's polarizing effect is not an artifact of a particular confidence-threshold choice — it persists, and its gap over the other policies widens, precisely in the regime where a population would otherwise be capable of reaching consensus.

### 3.3 Trajectories

`trajectories.png` (generated by `simulation.py`) plots mean opinion variance and mean extremity over all 150 rounds for each policy. The engagement condition separates from the other three within the first ~20–30 rounds and continues climbing, while random and bridging plateau earlier and lower; similarity climbs more slowly than engagement but does not plateau within the simulated horizon, suggesting it would continue diverging modestly with more rounds.

## 4. Conclusion

Using a minimal, fully reproducible bounded-confidence simulation, we find that an engagement-optimized exposure policy — one that adds an extremity reward on top of ordinary similarity-based ranking — produces substantially more polarization and opinion extremity than similarity-based filtering alone, despite both policies producing comparably insular echo chambers by direct exposure-distance measurement. This separates two mechanisms that are often bundled together in the "algorithms cause polarization" narrative and suggests that mitigation efforts aimed only at diversity injection (our "bridging" condition, which resembles many proposed real-world interventions) may under-perform relative to interventions that directly discourage extremity in ranking, since bridging did not reduce variance below plain similarity-based filtering in our model and instead produced more, not fewer, opinion clusters. Limitations: this is a stylized 1-D scalar-opinion model without a real social network topology, multi-issue correlation, or agent heterogeneity in persuadability; the "engagement" score is a simplified linear proxy rather than a learned ranking model; and we did not vary population size, pool size, or the extremity-reward weight, which would strengthen the robustness claim further. Future work should test whether the similarity/engagement decomposition holds under a networked (non-fully-random-pool) topology, under a learned rather than hand-specified engagement proxy, and whether extremity-penalizing re-ranking (the mitigation this result suggests) outperforms diversity injection directly, rather than only comparing the two policies we tested here.
