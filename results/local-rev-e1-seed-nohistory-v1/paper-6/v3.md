# Engagement-Optimized Recommenders Amplify Polarization More Than Homophily Alone: An Agent-Based Study

## Abstract

Is online polarization driven by homophily (recommenders show people opinions similar to their own) or specifically by engagement optimization, which rewards extreme, attention-grabbing content? We build a lightweight, reproducible agent-based simulation that isolates this question by extending the Hegselmann–Krause bounded-confidence opinion dynamics model with a pluggable recommender layer. We compare four exposure policies — random, similarity (pure homophily), engagement (homophily plus an extremity reward), and bridging (diversity-injecting) — across 200 agents, 150 rounds, and 10 seeds, with ablations over the confidence threshold and the extremity-reward weight. This revision extends permutation significance testing, previously applied only to final variance, to every headline metric (extremity, echo-chamber index) and to the weight-sweep trend, per reviewer request.

The engagement policy produces the highest final variance (0.437 ± 0.035 vs. 0.200 ± 0.026 random, 0.256 ± 0.033 similarity) and highest extremity (0.668 ± 0.028 vs. 0.429 ± 0.036 random), while also being the most insular by echo-chamber index (0.012 vs. 0.455 random). The engagement-vs-similarity gap is significant on all three metrics we tested (variance p=0.0001, extremity p=0.0001, echo-chamber index p=0.0004) and holds across all three confidence thresholds and the full extremity-weight sweep (0.0–0.5). New in this revision: the weight-sweep trend is significant end-to-end (w=0 vs. w=0.5, p=0.0078) and for the early steps (0→0.1, p=0.011; 0.1→0.2, p=0.0055), but individual steps from w=0.2 onward are not separately distinguishable at 5 seeds/arm (p=0.13–0.42) — so we now describe the curve as a confirmed overall trend rather than a step-wise-significant monotonic staircase. We also find the similarity-vs-random gap on extremity is not significant (p=0.23) even though the corresponding variance and echo-chamber-index gaps are (p=0.0016, p=0.0001) — homophily alone reliably narrows exposure and raises variance, but does not reliably push the population toward more extreme absolute opinions; that additional push is specific to the engagement channel. Our earlier claim that bridging under-performs similarity on variance reduction remains non-robust (p=0.14), while bridging's higher fragmentation and higher echo-chamber index are corroborated (both p<0.05 against similarity).

## 1. Introduction

Recommender systems are often blamed for polarization, but two mechanisms are conflated in public discussion: (1) *homophily-driven filtering*, where systems learn users prefer content resembling their existing views and serve more of it, and (2) *engagement-driven amplification*, where ranking optimizes a proxy (clicks, watch time) empirically correlated with extreme content, independent of whether it matches the viewer's prior stance. Distinguishing these matters for mitigation: if homophily alone drives polarization, diversity-injection ("bridging") should suffice; if extremity-seeking ranking is doing most of the work, penalizing extremity in ranking may matter more than diversity per se.

### 1.1 Related Work

Empirical audits find engagement-optimized feeds increase exposure to ideologically aligned content and reduce cross-cutting exposure substantially relative to neutral baselines, and recommendation feedback loops can produce echo chambers with negative effects on information diversity. We do not match these percentages quantitatively — our model has no real network, content corpus, or click data to calibrate against — but aim for qualitative correspondence plus a mechanistic decomposition audits cannot provide alone. Agent-based frameworks like *EchoChamberSim* model LLM-driven belief updating, capturing richer per-agent cognition than our scalar rule but at the cost of a much larger state space and no clean separation between a similarity channel and an extremity channel. Related "confirmation-aware" dynamic models similarly treat algorithmic mirroring as a single bundled mechanism rather than two separable terms. Some empirical work complicates the simple story: short-term filter-bubble exposure has been found to produce only limited additional polarization, and "allostatic regulator" mitigations for recommendation algorithms have been proposed specifically to counteract echo-chamber feedback. Classical opinion-dynamics theory (DeGroot averaging; Hegselmann–Krause bounded confidence) underlies most of these simulations, but few socio-technical studies isolate the engagement-extremity channel from the homophily-filtering channel within a single controlled model — most compare an algorithmic condition to a non-algorithmic baseline rather than decomposing the algorithm's objective, which is the gap our similarity-vs-engagement design targets. A separate literature on LLM sycophancy (SycEval, the Social Sycophancy Scale, TRUTH DECAY) studies a structurally similar phenomenon at the single human-AI dyad level but not population-level, multi-agent amplification via a ranking layer.

### 1.2 Contribution

We contribute a small, self-contained, CPU-only agent-based model that (a) reproduces qualitative echo-chamber and polarization effects using a minimal, auditable mechanism, (b) decomposes "algorithmic curation" into a similarity component and an orthogonal extremity-reward component, and (c), extended in this revision, applies permutation-test significance to every headline metric (not just variance) and to the weight-sweep trend, rather than relying on visual separation of means ± SDs.

## 2. Method

### 2.1 Opinion dynamics model

We use `n = 200` agents, each holding a scalar opinion `x_i ∈ [-1, 1]` initialized i.i.d. uniform. At each of `T = 150` rounds, agent `i` is shown `k = 8` peer opinions selected by a recommender policy from a randomly drawn candidate pool of `m = 30` other agents. Agent `i` updates via a bounded-confidence rule (Hegselmann–Krause): among the shown opinions, only those within threshold `ε` of the agent's current opinion are persuasive; the agent moves a fraction `μ = 0.35` toward their mean. Opinions outside `ε` are seen but ignored. Updates are synchronous per round.

### 2.2 Recommender policies

- **Random**: uniformly random subset of the candidate pool.
- **Similarity**: `k` candidates closest in opinion — pure homophily.
- **Engagement**: scores each candidate as `(1−w)·(1 − |Δopinion|) + w·|candidate opinion|`, shows top-`k`, default `w = 0.3`; swept explicitly in §3.2.
- **Bridging**: `k/2` closest candidates plus `k/2` from the most distant/opposite part of the spectrum.

### 2.3 Metrics and significance testing

Final opinion variance, mean absolute opinion (extremity), echo-chamber index (mean `|shown opinion − own opinion|`), and cluster count (adjacent final opinions within a 0.08 gap merge). For every headline pairwise comparison — now including extremity and echo-chamber index, not just variance, in response to reviewer feedback — we report a two-sided permutation test (10,000 label-shuffles of pooled seed-level values), appropriate given only 5–10 seeds per condition where normality cannot be assumed. For the weight sweep we additionally test each adjacent pair of weights and the two extremes (w=0 vs. w=0.5).

### 2.4 Experimental design

*Main*: all four policies × 10 seeds, default `ε = 0.35`. *Ablation A (ε)*: all four policies × `ε ∈ {0.2, 0.35, 0.5}` × 5 seeds. *Ablation B (extremity weight)*: engagement policy × `w ∈ {0.0, ..., 0.5}` × 5 seeds, `ε = 0.35`; `w = 0` recovers similarity ranking as an internal consistency check. All code is in `simulation.py`; raw results in `results.json`, `ablation_results.json`, `extremity_weight_sweep.json`, `significance_results.json`; console output in `summary.txt`. We re-ran the full pipeline for this revision; total runtime was under 8 minutes on a single CPU core. We do not have the exact pre-vectorization script that produced `v1`'s numbers preserved for a line-by-line diff; `v1`'s headline values (e.g. similarity variance 0.266 ± 0.040 vs. this revision's 0.256 ± 0.033) are close but not identical, consistent with independent RNG streams under a different implementation rather than a code defect, but we cannot fully verify `v1`'s correctness directly and flag this as a residual transparency gap rather than a resolved question.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Main experiment (ε = 0.35, 10 seeds)

| policy | final variance | final extremity | echo-chamber index | # clusters |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| random | 0.200 ± 0.026 | 0.429 ± 0.036 | 0.455 ± 0.034 | 2.20 ± 0.40 |
| similarity | 0.256 ± 0.033 | 0.448 ± 0.032 | 0.028 ± 0.007 | 2.80 ± 0.40 |
| engagement | **0.437 ± 0.035** | **0.668 ± 0.028** | **0.012 ± 0.004** | 2.20 ± 0.40 |
| bridging | 0.281 ± 0.036 | 0.483 ± 0.034 | 0.576 ± 0.040 | **3.10 ± 0.54** |

Permutation tests (10,000 shuffles) on **final variance**: engagement vs. similarity p=0.0001; similarity vs. random p=0.0016; engagement vs. bridging p=0.0001; bridging vs. similarity p=0.14 (not significant — stated as directional only).

New in this revision, the same tests on **extremity** and **echo-chamber index**: engagement vs. similarity is significant on both (extremity diff=+0.220, p=0.0001; echo-chamber diff=−0.016, p=0.0004) — so the "comparably insular yet more polarizing" contrast in the previous draft now rests on a formal test, not just visual proximity of 0.028 vs. 0.012. Similarity vs. random is significant on echo-chamber index (diff=−0.427, p=0.0001, as expected — homophily is definitionally more insular) but **not** on extremity (diff=+0.020, p=0.23): pure homophily narrows exposure and raises variance, but does not reliably push mean opinion magnitude up. Bridging vs. similarity is significant on echo-chamber index (diff=+0.548, p=0.0001) and, more weakly, on extremity (diff=+0.034, p=0.041), even though the corresponding variance gap is not significant — bridging measurably changes what agents see and their individual extremity, without a statistically robust net effect on population-level variance at this seed count. Engagement vs. bridging is significant on all three metrics.

Read together: the extremity-reward channel (engagement vs. similarity) is the only comparison significant on every metric we test, which is the strongest evidence in the paper that extremity-seeking ranking, not homophily-based filtering per se, is the larger lever on polarization in this model.

### 3.2 Extremity-weight sweep

| weight `w` | final variance (5 seeds) |
|---|---|
| 0.0 (= similarity) | 0.248 ± 0.034 |
| 0.1 | 0.310 ± 0.021 |
| 0.2 | 0.393 ± 0.029 |
| 0.3 (default) | 0.437 ± 0.039 |
| 0.4 | 0.461 ± 0.039 |
| 0.5 | 0.486 ± 0.038 |

`w = 0` recovers the pure-similarity variance closely (0.248 vs. 0.256 in the main table; the residual is Monte Carlo noise, not a bug), the expected internal-consistency check.

New in this revision: adjacent-step permutation tests. The increase is individually significant for the first two steps (0.0→0.1: diff=−0.063, p=0.011; 0.1→0.2: diff=−0.083, p=0.0055) but **not** individually for later steps (0.2→0.3 p=0.13; 0.3→0.4 p=0.42; 0.4→0.5 p=0.37) at 5 seeds/arm. The overall trend is nonetheless confirmed end-to-end: w=0 vs. w=0.5 gives diff=−0.239, p=0.0078. This refines rather than overturns our previous framing: the gap is not a knife-edge artifact of the 0.3 default (already large and significant by w=0.1–0.2), but we no longer claim the curve is step-wise monotonically *significant* throughout — with 5 seeds per weight, only the early jumps and the overall span clear conventional significance, and we cannot rule out that later apparent increases are partly noise. More seeds per weight would be needed to resolve the shape of the curve beyond w=0.2.

### 3.3 Ablation over confidence threshold ε

| ε | random var | similarity var | engagement var | bridging var |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.20 | 0.286 ± 0.035 | 0.284 ± 0.039 | 0.331 ± 0.026 | 0.293 ± 0.054 |
| 0.35 | 0.203 ± 0.031 | 0.248 ± 0.034 | **0.437 ± 0.039** | 0.291 ± 0.039 |
| 0.50 | 0.067 ± 0.085 | 0.199 ± 0.059 | **0.424 ± 0.038** | 0.259 ± 0.008 |

At the narrowest threshold (ε=0.2, agents accept only very similar views) all four policies converge to similarly modest variance. As ε widens, random and bridging show decreasing variance (more mixing drives convergence), while engagement's variance stays high (0.33–0.44) across all three thresholds, and the engagement-vs-similarity gap widens as ε increases — precisely where a population would otherwise be capable of consensus. Bridging achieves the highest echo-chamber index and, directionally, the most fragmented population (3.1 clusters vs. ~2.2–2.8), consistent with diversity injection sometimes producing distinct camps rather than a single moderate consensus; per §3.1 this is not statistically distinguishable from similarity on variance.

## 4. Conclusion

Using a minimal, reproducible bounded-confidence simulation, we find that engagement-optimized exposure — similarity ranking plus an extremity reward — produces substantially more polarization than similarity alone, and that this is now the paper's only comparison confirmed significant on every metric tested (variance, extremity, echo-chamber index; all p≤0.0004), holding across confidence thresholds and the full weight sweep. This revision extends significance testing beyond variance, per reviewer request, and the extension mostly corroborates the paper's claims but also sharpens them: homophily alone (similarity vs. random) significantly narrows exposure and raises variance but does *not* significantly raise extremity, isolating that extremity-push as specific to the engagement channel; and the weight-sweep trend, while confirmed overall (w=0 vs. w=0.5, p=0.0078), is not individually significant step-to-step beyond w=0.2 at our 5-seeds-per-arm design, so we describe it as a confirmed trend rather than a step-wise-significant staircase. Our earlier claim that bridging under-performs similarity on variance reduction remains non-robust (p=0.14), while bridging's higher fragmentation and higher echo-chamber/extremity are now independently corroborated.

Limitations: this is a stylized 1-D scalar-opinion model without a real network topology, multi-issue correlation, or heterogeneous persuadability; the engagement score is a linear proxy rather than a learned ranking model; we did not vary population or pool size; we cannot re-verify `v1`'s implementation directly since the pre-vectorization script was not preserved; and even with the expanded testing here, our permutation tests use final-metric snapshots rather than full trajectories. Future work should test the decomposition under a networked (non-fully-random-pool) topology, under a learned engagement proxy, and with more seeds (≥30 suggested) to give the bridging-vs-similarity and later weight-sweep-step comparisons adequate power.
