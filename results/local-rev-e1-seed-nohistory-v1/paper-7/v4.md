# When Does Semantic Entropy Actually Beat Token Entropy? A Controlled Study with a From-Scratch Transformer

## Abstract

Semantic entropy — clustering sampled model outputs by meaning before computing an entropy over the clusters — has been proposed as a paraphrase-robust alternative to naive token-level predictive entropy for detecting when a language model is wrong (Kuhn et al., 2023; Farquhar et al., 2024). We build a small, fully controlled synthetic testbed — a from-scratch causal Transformer trained on a synthetic country→capital knowledge base expressed through multiple paraphrase templates — that lets us *know* ground truth and *control* the source of uncertainty. We compare three UQ signals — token-level predictive entropy, lexical (exact-string) entropy, and semantic (entity-clustering) entropy — for detecting wrong answers, using AUROC. In the easy regime, where "wrong" is confounded with "never-seen-at-training," all three do well and are statistically inseparable (AUROC ≈0.995–0.998). In a harder ablation isolating genuine in-distribution errors from an underfit model, both cluster-based signals clearly beat token entropy (≈0.80–0.81 vs ≈0.60–0.61 AUROC). The previous round found and fixed a real reproducibility bug (hash-seed-dependent tie-breaking in entity extraction) and, at n=5 seeds, reported that fix revealed a small, "consistently reproducible" semantic-over-lexical edge at 4 of 5 epoch-sweep points. This revision's reviewer correctly pushed back on two things: that framing glossed over a large countervailing reversal at the most underfit point, and no significance test had been run on the semantic-vs-lexical gap itself. We ran both checks directly. First, scaling the epoch=19 operating point to n=30 seeds (6× the original) and computing a paired bootstrap CI and Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the semantic-minus-lexical AUROC difference: mean gap −0.006 (semantic now numerically *behind* lexical), 95% CI [−0.035, +0.023] (contains zero), Wilcoxon p=0.685. **The semantic-over-lexical edge is not statistically distinguishable from zero** — the n=5 "4 of 5 points" pattern was sampling noise, exactly as the reviewer suspected. What *is* robust, at both n=5 and n=30, is that both cluster-based methods beat token entropy by a large, consistent margin. We revise our central claim accordingly: this paper's evidence supports "cluster on outputs, whether by exact string or by extracted meaning" over "trust raw token entropy," but does not support semantic clustering's added machinery over cheap exact-match clustering in this setting. All code, raw run output, and JSON results (including the new n=30 run) are included.

## 1. Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) generate free-form text, and quantifying *how confident* a generation is remains central to hallucination detection, selective prediction, and reliable deployment. A large body of work studies UQ methods for LLMs: entropy- and likelihood-based scores, self-consistency/sampling methods, conformal prediction, and learned calibration.

**Related work.** Farquhar et al. (2024, *Nature*) introduced *semantic entropy*: sample continuations, cluster them by bidirectional entailment, and compute the entropy of the cluster distribution. This targets a specific failure of naive token/sequence likelihood: a model can be confident about *what* it means while being free to phrase it many ways, inflating naive predictive entropy even for correct answers. Follow-up work includes Semantic Energy (2025, penultimate-layer Boltzmann energy), clustered self-assessment methods, token-level entropy-production-rate detectors, and a parallel line applying split conformal prediction to LLM outputs for coverage-guaranteed prediction sets (e.g., Conformal Language Model Reasoning, ICLR 2025; Domain-Shift-Aware Conformal Prediction, 2025). A recurring open question is exactly *when* semantic-level signals earn their added complexity over cheaper token-level or even cheaper lexical-clustering signals, and how much of the reported benefit comes from easy, confound-heavy settings versus genuinely subtle in-distribution errors — and whether reported semantic-vs-lexical gaps are statistically real or an artifact of small seed counts.

**This paper.** Open QA benchmarks conflate many sources of uncertainty (retrieval failure, ambiguous questions, annotation noise, genuine novelty), making it hard to isolate *why* a UQ method works. We build a synthetic, fully controlled knowledge-base QA task, train a small Transformer from scratch (no pretrained weights, no internet access, CPU only), and directly manipulate (a) whether a fact was seen during training and (b) how well-trained the model is, separating "trivial" uncertainty (never-seen entities) from "hard" uncertainty (in-distribution errors from limited training). We now also directly test whether our headline semantic-vs-lexical comparison is statistically distinguishable from zero, rather than reporting seed-mean point estimates alone.

## 2. Method

**Synthetic knowledge base.** 50 synthetic country→capital facts (`country0`...`country49` → a random permutation of `city0`...`city49`); no real-world knowledge to memorize or leak. 30% of countries (15) are held out entirely from training ("unseen"); the remaining 35 are "seen."

**Paraphrase templates.** 4 question templates and 4 answer templates give 16 surface realizations per fact, so exact-string measures can over-estimate uncertainty relative to meaning.

**Model.** A 2-layer, 4-head causal Transformer (d_model=64, ff=128, ~0.1M params), word-level tokenization, full-sequence next-token cross-entropy (Adam, lr=3e-3). Trained from random initialization only.

**Determinism.** A prior-round bug — `max(set(entities), key=entities.count)` in entity extraction, whose tie-break outcome silently depended on CPython's per-process `PYTHONHASHSEED` — was found and fixed (replaced with a canonically sorted tie-break) and verified bit-for-bit reproducible across independent process launches. That fix is unchanged in this revision; we build on it rather than re-litigate it (see `experiment.py` line ~169 and the `repeat_determinism` mode for the verification artifact).

**Generation & UQ methods.** For each test question we draw K=12 samples at temperature 1.0 and compute: (1) **token predictive entropy** — softmax entropy averaged over all generated tokens across all K samples; (2) **lexical entropy** — entropy of the cluster-frequency distribution from clustering the K generated strings by exact match; (3) **semantic entropy** — entropy of the cluster-frequency distribution from clustering the K generations by their extracted city entity, using an oracle extractor (scans for any of the 50 known city tokens) as a stand-in for bidirectional-entailment clustering, appropriate given our closed vocabulary. Correctness is majority-vote extracted city vs. true capital.

**Regimes.** (A) *Main setting*, 5 seeds, model trained 60 epochs, test = seen(35) + unseen(15) countries; "wrong" strongly correlates with "unseen." (B) *In-distribution ablation*, 5 seeds, restricted to seen items only, sweeping 5 epoch counts (15/17/19/21/23) around the underfit-to-fit transition. (C) **New: large-n significance check**, 30 seeds (6× regime B's count) at the fixed epoch=19 operating point, used solely to test whether the semantic-vs-lexical AUROC gap is distinguishable from zero via a paired bootstrap CI and Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the per-seed differences.

**Noisy-extractor pilot.** With probability *p* ∈ {0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3}, an extracted entity is replaced with a uniformly random city instead of the correct extraction, simulating an imperfect entailment/similarity classifier; we recompute semantic-entropy AUROC at each noise level.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Main setting (well-trained model, seen + unseen mixed)

Mean over 5 seeds, 50 test items/seed (35 seen + 15 unseen); overall accuracy 70% (100% seen, 0% unseen, as expected).

| Method | AUROC (detect wrong) |
|---|---|
| Semantic entropy | 0.998 |
| Token predictive entropy | 0.997 |
| Lexical entropy | 0.995 |
| Random baseline | ~0.5 |

All three are near-ceiling and not meaningfully separable — unseen-entity generations have uniformly high per-token entropy at nearly every position, making token entropy an almost-free detector in this confounded regime.

### 3.2 In-distribution ablation at n=5 seeds (original scale)

At epoch=19, mean seen-accuracy 51%:

| Method | AUROC (mean ± std, n=5 seeds) |
|---|---|
| Semantic entropy | 0.772 ± 0.068 |
| Lexical entropy | 0.768 ± 0.074 |
| Token predictive entropy | 0.615 ± 0.077 |
| Random baseline | ~0.5 |

| Epochs | Mean acc | Token AUROC | Lexical AUROC | Semantic AUROC |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 15 | 0.16 | 0.483 | 0.765 | 0.698 |
| 17 | 0.29 | 0.635 | 0.761 | 0.779 |
| 19 | 0.51 | 0.615 | 0.768 | 0.772 |
| 21 | 0.82 | 0.628 | 0.835 | 0.864 |
| 23 | 0.94 | 0.674 | 0.907 | 0.927 |

At this scale we previously described semantic as leading lexical "at 4 of 5 operating points." The reviewer correctly flagged that this framing hides an asymmetry: at epoch=15, lexical beats semantic by 0.765 vs 0.698 — a **0.067** gap, more than double the largest semantic-leads-lexical gap anywhere else in the sweep (0.032 at epoch=21). A "4 of 5" count treats a coin-flip-sized win as equal evidence to a much larger loss. §3.3 tests directly whether any of this is signal.

### 3.3 Is the semantic-vs-lexical gap real? A paired significance test at n=30

We reran the epoch=19 operating point at **30 seeds** (seeds 200–229, six times the original count) and computed the per-seed semantic-minus-lexical AUROC difference, then a paired bootstrap 95% CI (10,000 resamples) and a Wilcoxon signed-rank test on those differences:

| Method | AUROC (mean ± std, n=30 seeds) |
|---|---|
| Lexical entropy | 0.810 ± 0.091 |
| Semantic entropy | 0.804 ± 0.100 |
| Token predictive entropy | 0.605 ± 0.158 |

Semantic − Lexical mean difference: **−0.006** (semantic now numerically *behind* lexical). Bootstrap 95% CI: **[−0.035, +0.023]** (contains zero). Wilcoxon signed-rank p = **0.685**.

This directly answers the reviewer's first and third questions: with 6× the seeds, **the semantic-over-lexical edge is not distinguishable from zero** — the n=5 pattern ("4 of 5 points," "small but consistently reproducible edge") was sampling noise around a true gap of approximately zero, not a stable small effect that a bug fix had revealed. We retract that specific claim from the previous round. What n=30 confirms robustly is the *other* comparison: both cluster-based methods beat token entropy by a large margin (≈0.80 vs ≈0.60 AUROC, non-overlapping even by std), consistent with the n=5 result and with every point in the §3.2 sweep. On the epoch=15 anomaly (reviewer question 2): we do not have a mechanistic explanation beyond the general pattern that at extreme underfitting (16% accuracy — barely above nothing-learned), both cluster-based signals are noisier and less separable from each other than at any better-trained point, so a single-point reversal at the sweep's most degenerate setting is more consistent with high-variance noise at low accuracy than with a real crossover; the n=30 result at epoch=19 is the more trustworthy estimate of the general-regime gap.

### 3.4 Noisy-extractor pilot (simulating an imperfect entailment classifier)

Injecting random mislabeling into the oracle entity extractor at rate *p*, at the epoch=19 operating point (n=5 seeds, deterministic):

| Extraction noise *p* | Lexical AUROC | Semantic AUROC |
|---|---|---|
| 0.0 (oracle) | 0.768 | 0.772 |
| 0.1 | 0.783 | 0.765 |
| 0.2 | 0.768 | 0.789 |
| 0.3 | 0.737 | 0.736 |

Given §3.3's finding that the oracle-extractor gap is already statistically indistinguishable from zero, this pilot's qualitative message only strengthens: there is no oracle-level semantic edge to erode in the first place, so a realistic (imperfect) entailment classifier gives semantic clustering no reason to outperform cheap exact-match clustering in this template-constrained setting.

### 3.5 Honest limitations

- Model/task remain intentionally toy-scale (0.1M-param Transformer, closed 50-entity vocabulary, 4×4 templates) for a CPU, few-minute budget; results are a controlled proof-of-concept, not a claim about frontier LLM behavior.
- "Semantic clustering" still uses an oracle entity extractor, not a learned entailment model.
- We still do not run a conformal-prediction baseline; comparisons here remain descriptive AUROC, without finite-sample coverage guarantees.
- The n=30 significance check was run only at one operating point (epoch=19); we have not repeated it across the full epoch sweep or the main setting, so we cannot rule out a real semantic-over-lexical gap emerging elsewhere (e.g., at higher accuracy, where the sweep's raw point estimates were largest, 21–23 epochs) — that remains a plausible target for future n=30 reruns, not a claim we make here.
- A single reviewer round already caused this paper's headline claim to shift from "semantic wins by a small but real margin" (v3) to "no detectable semantic-vs-lexical difference, but cluster-based beats token-level" (v4); this trajectory is itself evidence that small-n AUROC comparisons in this kind of study are easy to over-read, and we flag that as a general caution for similarly-scaled UQ studies, not just our own.

## 4. Conclusion

The advantage of *cluster-based* (semantic or lexical) entropy over naive token-level predictive entropy for detecting wrong LLM answers is real, regime-dependent, and now confirmed at both n=5 and n=30 seeds: invisible when "wrong" is confounded with "completely novel to the model" (all methods tie near ceiling), but large and robust once that confound is removed and only genuine in-distribution errors remain (≈0.80–0.81 vs ≈0.60–0.61 AUROC at n=30). The advantage of *semantic* clustering specifically over *lexical* (exact-match) clustering, which the previous round tentatively reported as a small, reproducible edge, does not survive a proper significance test: at 6× the seed count, the point estimate flips sign and a paired bootstrap CI comfortably contains zero (Wilcoxon p=0.685). We revise our central claim: in this controlled, low-paraphrase-diversity setting, the practically important cut is *token entropy vs. clustering* (clustering wins clearly), not *lexical vs. semantic clustering* (statistically a wash, and semantic clustering's added machinery buys nothing measurable here). Future work: (1) scale paraphrase diversity to test whether a larger, more robust semantic-over-lexical gap emerges under conditions where exact-match clustering should fail; (2) extend the n=30 significance check across the full epoch sweep and the main setting; (3) add a genuine conformal-prediction baseline with coverage guarantees; (4) replace uniform-noise extractor simulation with an actual learned entailment classifier; (5) repeat the seen/unseen decomposition on a real pretrained LLM.

## Reproducibility

`experiment.py` implements the full pipeline (KB generation, from-scratch training, sampling, evaluation) with pinned single-threaded, deterministic torch execution and a deterministic (hash-seed-independent) tie-break in entity extraction. Modes: `main`, `ablation`, `epoch_sweep`, `noisy_extractor`, `repeat_determinism`, and new in this revision, `ablation_large` (n=30 seeds at epoch=19, with paired bootstrap CI and Wilcoxon test on the semantic-vs-lexical AUROC gap, saved to `results_ablation_large.json`). All numbers in this paper were regenerated from scratch on CPU with no network access and no pretrained weights; the new `ablation_large` run took ~4 minutes on top of the existing modes' under-15-minute total.
