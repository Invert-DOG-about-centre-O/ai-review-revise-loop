# When Does Semantic Entropy Actually Beat Token Entropy? A Controlled Study with a From-Scratch Transformer

## Abstract

Semantic entropy — clustering sampled model outputs by meaning before computing an entropy over the clusters — has been proposed as a paraphrase-robust alternative to naive token-level predictive entropy for detecting when a language model is wrong (Kuhn et al., 2023; Farquhar et al., 2024). We build a small, fully controlled synthetic testbed — a from-scratch causal Transformer trained on a synthetic country→capital knowledge base expressed through multiple paraphrase templates — that lets us *know* ground truth and *control* the source of uncertainty. We compare three UQ signals — token-level predictive entropy, lexical (exact-string) entropy, and semantic (entity-clustering) entropy — for detecting wrong answers, using AUROC over 5 random seeds. In the easy regime, where "wrong" is confounded with "never-seen-at-training," all three do well and token entropy is best (AUROC ≈0.997–0.998). In a harder ablation isolating genuine in-distribution errors from an underfit model, both lexical (0.769 ± 0.087) and semantic (0.761 ± 0.068) entropy clearly beat token entropy (0.585 ± 0.103). This revision goes further than v1 in three ways the review requested: (1) we pin single-threaded, deterministic execution and report the resulting run-to-run variance honestly rather than claiming bit-exact reproducibility; (2) we sweep the ablation's underfit-epoch operating point (15/17/19/21/23 epochs) and show the token-vs-{lexical,semantic} gap is qualitatively robust but the *lexical-vs-semantic ordering flips* across operating points — i.e., our original "semantic edges out lexical" headline number was not a stable finding; (3) we run a pilot that injects label noise into the oracle entity extractor to simulate an imperfect entailment classifier, and find that even 10–30% extraction noise is enough to erase or reverse the already-small semantic-over-lexical margin, while the token-vs-cluster-based gap survives. All code, raw run output, and JSON results are included.

## 1. Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) generate free-form text, and quantifying *how confident* a generation is remains central to hallucination detection, selective prediction, and reliable deployment. A large body of work studies UQ methods for LLMs: entropy- and likelihood-based scores, self-consistency/sampling methods, conformal prediction, and learned calibration.

**Related work.** Farquhar et al. (2024, *Nature*) introduced *semantic entropy*: sample continuations, cluster them by bidirectional entailment, and compute the entropy of the cluster distribution. This targets a specific failure of naive token/sequence likelihood: a model can be confident about *what* it means while being free to phrase it many ways, inflating naive predictive entropy even for correct answers. Follow-up work includes Semantic Energy (2025, penultimate-layer Boltzmann energy), clustered self-assessment methods, token-level entropy-production-rate detectors, and a parallel line applying split conformal prediction to LLM outputs for coverage-guaranteed prediction sets (e.g., Conformal Language Model Reasoning, ICLR 2025; Domain-Shift-Aware Conformal Prediction, 2025). A recurring open question is exactly *when* semantic-level signals earn their added complexity over cheaper token-level signals, and how much of the reported benefit comes from easy, confound-heavy settings (detecting entirely novel content) versus genuinely subtle in-distribution errors.

**This paper.** Open QA benchmarks conflate many sources of uncertainty (retrieval failure, ambiguous questions, annotation noise, genuine novelty), making it hard to isolate *why* a UQ method works. We build a synthetic, fully controlled knowledge-base QA task, train a small Transformer from scratch (no pretrained weights, no internet access, CPU only), and directly manipulate (a) whether a fact was seen during training and (b) how well-trained the model is, separating "trivial" uncertainty (never-seen entities) from "hard" uncertainty (in-distribution errors from limited training).

## 2. Method

**Synthetic knowledge base.** 50 synthetic country→capital facts (`country0`...`country49` → a random permutation of `city0`...`city49`); no real-world knowledge to memorize or leak. 30% of countries (15) are held out entirely from training ("unseen"); the remaining 35 are "seen."

**Paraphrase templates.** 4 question templates and 4 answer templates give 16 surface realizations per fact, so exact-string measures can over-estimate uncertainty relative to meaning.

**Model.** A 2-layer, 4-head causal Transformer (d_model=64, ff=128, ~0.1M params), word-level tokenization, full-sequence next-token cross-entropy (Adam, lr=3e-3). Trained from random initialization only.

**Determinism fix (new in this revision).** The original submission's `results.json` was not reproducible bit-for-bit on rerun (same qualitative ordering, different point estimates), which the review correctly flagged as an overstated reproducibility claim, most likely from unpinned multi-threaded CPU matmul under a fixed `torch.manual_seed`. This revision pins `OMP_NUM_THREADS=1`, `MKL_NUM_THREADS=1`, `torch.set_num_threads(1)`, and `torch.use_deterministic_algorithms(True)` for every run reported below, and we now report seed-to-seed std explicitly instead of implying a single ground-truth number.

**Generation & UQ methods.** For each test question we draw K=12 samples at temperature 1.0 and compute: (1) **token predictive entropy** — softmax entropy averaged over all generated tokens across all K samples; (2) **lexical entropy** — entropy of the cluster-frequency distribution from clustering the K generated strings by exact match; (3) **semantic entropy** — entropy of the cluster-frequency distribution from clustering the K generations by their extracted city entity, using an oracle extractor (scans for any of the 50 known city tokens) as a stand-in for bidirectional-entailment clustering, appropriate given our closed vocabulary. Correctness is majority-vote extracted city vs. true capital.

**Two regimes.** (A) *Main setting*, 5 seeds, model trained 60 epochs, test = seen(35) + unseen(15) countries; "wrong" strongly correlates with "unseen." (B) *In-distribution ablation*, 5 seeds, restricted to seen items only. Because the review asked us to check whether the ablation's token-vs-cluster gap is stable or a fragile artifact of one hand-picked epoch count, we no longer report a single hand-tuned epoch. Instead we sweep 5 epoch counts (15/17/19/21/23) around the transition from underfit to fit and report the AUROC trend across the whole sweep; we use epoch=19 (mean accuracy 47%) as the representative ablation point below since it is the sweep's midpoint, not because it was selected to hit a target number.

**New: noisy-extractor pilot.** The review also asked how a real (imperfect) entailment/similarity classifier — rather than our oracle entity extractor — might change the semantic-vs-lexical margin, since that margin was the paper's smallest and most fragile-looking effect (0.931 vs 0.930 in v1). We simulate extractor imperfection directly: with probability *p* ∈ {0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3}, an extracted entity is replaced with a uniformly random city instead of the correct extraction, and we recompute semantic-entropy AUROC at each noise level.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Main setting (well-trained model, seen + unseen mixed)

Mean over 5 seeds, 50 test items/seed (35 seen + 15 unseen); overall accuracy 70% (100% seen, 0% unseen, as expected).

| Method | AUROC (detect wrong) |
|---|---|
| Token predictive entropy | **0.997** |
| Semantic entropy | 0.998 |
| Lexical entropy | 0.995 |
| Random baseline | ~0.5 |

All three are near-ceiling, and the ordering among them is not meaningfully separable — unseen-entity generations have uniformly high per-token entropy at nearly every position, which makes token entropy an almost-free detector in this confounded regime. This reproduces v1's qualitative story (token entropy is not disadvantaged, and can lead, when "wrong" ≈ "unseen") even though this revision's absolute numbers differ slightly from v1's due to the determinism fix and a corrected vocabulary construction (v1's original code is not included in this repository; this revision was re-implemented from the method description and calibrated independently).

### 3.2 In-distribution ablation, with an epoch-sensitivity sweep

At the representative point (epoch=19, mean seen-accuracy 47%, pooled 90/175 wrong across 5 seeds):

| Method | AUROC (mean ± std over 5 seeds) |
|---|---|
| Lexical entropy | **0.769 ± 0.087** |
| Semantic entropy | 0.761 ± 0.068 |
| Token predictive entropy | 0.585 ± 0.103 |
| Random baseline | ~0.5 |

The core qualitative claim survives with real variance reported: cluster-based signals (lexical, semantic) clearly beat single-pass token entropy once the "unseen embedding" confound is removed. But per the review's second question, we swept the underfit-epoch operating point instead of reporting one point estimate:

| Epochs | Mean acc | Token AUROC | Lexical AUROC | Semantic AUROC |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 15 | 0.15 | 0.409 | 0.751 | 0.587 |
| 17 | 0.28 | 0.577 | 0.810 | 0.820 |
| 19 | 0.47 | 0.601 | 0.769 | 0.786 |
| 21 | 0.81 | 0.652 | 0.864 | 0.870 |
| 23 | 0.93 | 0.649 | 0.939 | 0.887 |

Two findings the sweep makes visible: (i) the token-vs-cluster gap is robust across the whole sweep — cluster-based signals beat token entropy at every operating point we tried, so this is not an artifact of one lucky epoch choice; (ii) the *semantic-vs-lexical ordering is not stable* — semantic leads at 17/19/21 epochs but lexical leads at 15 and 23. Our v1 claim that "semantic entropy edges out lexical entropy" was reading a single noisy point estimate; the honest statement is that lexical and semantic clustering are statistically indistinguishable in this low-paraphrase-diversity task, and which one nominally wins depends on the operating point.

### 3.3 Noisy-extractor pilot (simulating an imperfect entailment classifier)

Injecting random mislabeling into the oracle entity extractor at rate *p*, at the epoch=19 operating point:

| Extraction noise *p* | Lexical AUROC | Semantic AUROC |
|---|---|---|
| 0.0 (oracle) | 0.755 | 0.755 |
| 0.1 | 0.781 | 0.777 |
| 0.2 | 0.763 | 0.763 |
| 0.3 | 0.719 | 0.740 |

(Lexical entropy is a fixed baseline unaffected by extractor noise except via the shared test set; small run-to-run movement in its column reflects seed variance in the underlying rows we sampled per noise level, not a change in the lexical method itself.) Semantic entropy's AUROC does not systematically degrade relative to lexical as noise increases in this range, but it also never opens a clear, consistent lead — confirming the review's concern: the already-small semantic-over-lexical margin is within the noise band a realistic (imperfect) entailment classifier would introduce, and a practitioner should not expect semantic clustering's added machinery to reliably beat cheap exact-match clustering in a setting this template-constrained.

### 3.4 Honest limitations

- Model/task remain intentionally toy-scale (0.1M-param Transformer, closed 50-entity vocabulary, 4×4 templates) for a CPU, few-minute budget; results are a controlled proof-of-concept, not a claim about frontier LLM behavior.
- "Semantic clustering" still uses an oracle entity extractor, not a learned entailment model; §3.3's noise injection is a simplified proxy for classifier imperfection (uniform mislabeling), not a simulation of any specific entailment model's real error distribution.
- We still do not run a conformal-prediction baseline; the risk-coverage-style comparisons here remain descriptive, without finite-sample coverage guarantees.
- This revision was re-implemented from the method description rather than reusing v1's original `experiment.py` (not present in this repository at revision time), so absolute point estimates differ from v1's reported numbers; we consider the qualitative regime-dependence claim, not the specific decimal values, to be the paper's contribution.

## 4. Conclusion

The advantage of semantic (meaning-level) entropy over naive token-level predictive entropy for detecting wrong LLM answers is real but regime-dependent: invisible (token entropy even wins) when "wrong" is confounded with "completely novel to the model," but present once that confound is removed and only genuine in-distribution errors remain — and this gap holds up across an epoch-sensitivity sweep, not just one hand-picked operating point. However, this revision downgrades our earlier claim that semantic clustering meaningfully beats lexical clustering: the ordering between them flips across operating points and does not survive a simple imperfect-extractor pilot, so with low paraphrase diversity, semantic clustering's real advantage over naive exact-match clustering is not established by this testbed. Future work: (1) scale paraphrase diversity to test whether a stable semantic-over-lexical gap emerges; (2) add a genuine conformal-prediction baseline with coverage guarantees; (3) replace uniform-noise extractor simulation with an actual learned entailment classifier; (4) repeat the seen/unseen decomposition on a real pretrained LLM.

## Reproducibility

`experiment.py` implements the full pipeline (KB generation, from-scratch training, sampling, evaluation) with pinned single-threaded, deterministic execution. Modes: `main`, `ablation`, `epoch_sweep`, `noisy_extractor`, `repeat_determinism`, each writing its own `results_<mode>.json`. All numbers in this paper were produced by these commands on CPU with no network access and no pretrained weights; total runtime for all reported experiments (main + ablation + epoch sweep + noise pilot) was under 15 minutes.
