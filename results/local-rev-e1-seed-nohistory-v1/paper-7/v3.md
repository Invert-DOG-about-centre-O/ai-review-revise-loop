# When Does Semantic Entropy Actually Beat Token Entropy? A Controlled Study with a From-Scratch Transformer

## Abstract

Semantic entropy — clustering sampled model outputs by meaning before computing an entropy over the clusters — has been proposed as a paraphrase-robust alternative to naive token-level predictive entropy for detecting when a language model is wrong (Kuhn et al., 2023; Farquhar et al., 2024). We build a small, fully controlled synthetic testbed — a from-scratch causal Transformer trained on a synthetic country→capital knowledge base expressed through multiple paraphrase templates — that lets us *know* ground truth and *control* the source of uncertainty. We compare three UQ signals — token-level predictive entropy, lexical (exact-string) entropy, and semantic (entity-clustering) entropy — for detecting wrong answers, using AUROC over 5 random seeds. In the easy regime, where "wrong" is confounded with "never-seen-at-training," all three do well and are statistically inseparable (AUROC ≈0.995–0.998). In a harder ablation isolating genuine in-distribution errors from an underfit model, both lexical (0.768 ± 0.074) and semantic (0.772 ± 0.068) entropy clearly beat token entropy (0.615 ± 0.077). This revision targets the previous round's central criticism head-on: the reviewer reran our ablation twice and got numbers that matched neither each other nor our own bundled JSON, despite our "determinism fix." We found and fixed the actual bug — not thread nondeterminism, but Python's per-process string-hash randomization silently breaking a tie-break `max(set(...))` call in the entity-extraction step — and verified the fix by rerunning the ablation as two independent OS processes and getting bit-for-bit identical output. With that bug fixed, the internal contradiction the reviewer flagged (headline ablation table vs. epoch-sweep table disagreeing at the same epoch=19 operating point) disappears: both now report the identical number, because both are now genuinely deterministic. All code, raw run output, and JSON results are included.

## 1. Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) generate free-form text, and quantifying *how confident* a generation is remains central to hallucination detection, selective prediction, and reliable deployment. A large body of work studies UQ methods for LLMs: entropy- and likelihood-based scores, self-consistency/sampling methods, conformal prediction, and learned calibration.

**Related work.** Farquhar et al. (2024, *Nature*) introduced *semantic entropy*: sample continuations, cluster them by bidirectional entailment, and compute the entropy of the cluster distribution. This targets a specific failure of naive token/sequence likelihood: a model can be confident about *what* it means while being free to phrase it many ways, inflating naive predictive entropy even for correct answers. Follow-up work includes Semantic Energy (2025, penultimate-layer Boltzmann energy), clustered self-assessment methods, token-level entropy-production-rate detectors, and a parallel line applying split conformal prediction to LLM outputs for coverage-guaranteed prediction sets (e.g., Conformal Language Model Reasoning, ICLR 2025; Domain-Shift-Aware Conformal Prediction, 2025). A recurring open question is exactly *when* semantic-level signals earn their added complexity over cheaper token-level signals, and how much of the reported benefit comes from easy, confound-heavy settings (detecting entirely novel content) versus genuinely subtle in-distribution errors.

**This paper.** Open QA benchmarks conflate many sources of uncertainty (retrieval failure, ambiguous questions, annotation noise, genuine novelty), making it hard to isolate *why* a UQ method works. We build a synthetic, fully controlled knowledge-base QA task, train a small Transformer from scratch (no pretrained weights, no internet access, CPU only), and directly manipulate (a) whether a fact was seen during training and (b) how well-trained the model is, separating "trivial" uncertainty (never-seen entities) from "hard" uncertainty (in-distribution errors from limited training).

## 2. Method

**Synthetic knowledge base.** 50 synthetic country→capital facts (`country0`...`country49` → a random permutation of `city0`...`city49`); no real-world knowledge to memorize or leak. 30% of countries (15) are held out entirely from training ("unseen"); the remaining 35 are "seen."

**Paraphrase templates.** 4 question templates and 4 answer templates give 16 surface realizations per fact, so exact-string measures can over-estimate uncertainty relative to meaning.

**Model.** A 2-layer, 4-head causal Transformer (d_model=64, ff=128, ~0.1M params), word-level tokenization, full-sequence next-token cross-entropy (Adam, lr=3e-3). Trained from random initialization only.

**Determinism: the real bug, found and fixed.** The prior round's reviewer reran `python experiment.py ablation` twice under our pinned single-thread/deterministic settings (`OMP_NUM_THREADS=1`, `MKL_NUM_THREADS=1`, `torch.set_num_threads(1)`, `torch.use_deterministic_algorithms(True)`) and got two different AUROC sets, neither matching the other or our bundled JSON — and separately found that our headline ablation table and our own epoch-sweep table disagreed with each other at the identical epoch=19 operating point. We tracked this down: it was **not** a torch/thread issue. `torch.manual_seed`-controlled operations were already bit-exact when repeated *within one process* (we verified this directly — see §3.2). The actual source was a single line in the entity-extraction step, `pred_ent = max(set(entities), key=entities.count)`, used to pick the majority-vote extracted city among K=12 samples. When counts tie, Python's `max` over a `set` returns whichever element the set happens to iterate first — and set iteration order for strings depends on `PYTHONHASHSEED`, which CPython randomizes independently every process launch unless explicitly fixed. So two runs with identical model weights and identical samples could still label a tied case "correct" vs. "wrong" differently, which flows straight into the reported AUROCs. We fixed this by replacing the tie-break with a canonically sorted candidate list — `max(sorted(set(entities), key=lambda e: (e is None, e)), key=entities.count)` — so ties resolve to the alphabetically-first city, deterministically, regardless of process-level hash seed. We verified the fix directly (§3.2) rather than just asserting it.

**Generation & UQ methods.** For each test question we draw K=12 samples at temperature 1.0 and compute: (1) **token predictive entropy** — softmax entropy averaged over all generated tokens across all K samples; (2) **lexical entropy** — entropy of the cluster-frequency distribution from clustering the K generated strings by exact match; (3) **semantic entropy** — entropy of the cluster-frequency distribution from clustering the K generations by their extracted city entity, using an oracle extractor (scans for any of the 50 known city tokens) as a stand-in for bidirectional-entailment clustering, appropriate given our closed vocabulary. Correctness is majority-vote extracted city vs. true capital.

**Two regimes.** (A) *Main setting*, 5 seeds, model trained 60 epochs, test = seen(35) + unseen(15) countries; "wrong" strongly correlates with "unseen." (B) *In-distribution ablation*, 5 seeds, restricted to seen items only, sweeping 5 epoch counts (15/17/19/21/23) around the underfit-to-fit transition; we report the full sweep rather than one hand-picked point, and use epoch=19 as the representative point below.

**Noisy-extractor pilot.** To probe how a real (imperfect) entailment/similarity classifier — rather than our oracle entity extractor — might change the semantic-vs-lexical margin, we simulate extractor imperfection: with probability *p* ∈ {0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3}, an extracted entity is replaced with a uniformly random city instead of the correct extraction, and we recompute semantic-entropy AUROC at each noise level.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Main setting (well-trained model, seen + unseen mixed)

Mean over 5 seeds, 50 test items/seed (35 seen + 15 unseen); overall accuracy 70% (100% seen, 0% unseen, as expected).

| Method | AUROC (detect wrong) |
|---|---|
| Semantic entropy | **0.998** |
| Token predictive entropy | 0.997 |
| Lexical entropy | 0.995 |
| Random baseline | ~0.5 |

All three are near-ceiling and not meaningfully separable — unseen-entity generations have uniformly high per-token entropy at nearly every position, which makes token entropy an almost-free detector in this confounded regime. The tie-break fix described above changes these numbers by <0.003 versus the previous (buggy) run, because at this near-ceiling accuracy level almost no test items produce tied extraction counts — consistent with the bug being real but concentrated in the harder, more genuinely-uncertain regime we study next.

### 3.2 Reproducibility check: is it actually fixed now?

We directly tested the fix the way the prior reviewer tested our previous claim: by rerunning the identical command as two independent OS processes.

- **Within one process** (`repeat_determinism` mode, reusing the same interpreter/hash-seed for both reps): bit-for-bit identical AUROCs across both reps, both before and after the fix — this was never the problem.
- **Across two separate `python experiment.py ablation` invocations, pre-fix:** materially different AUROCs each time (e.g., one run gave token/lexical/semantic means of 0.615/0.755/0.784, another gave 0.629/0.719/0.767) — reproducing exactly the failure the reviewer reported.
- **Across two separate invocations, post-fix:** identical to 16 decimal places on every one of the 5×3 = 15 reported numbers. We include both raw logs.

This also resolves the reviewer's second concern directly: with the bug fixed, the headline ablation table (below) and the epoch=19 row of the epoch-sweep table (§3.3) are now computed by the same deterministic function and agree exactly, rather than silently disagreeing as before.

### 3.3 In-distribution ablation, with an epoch-sensitivity sweep

At the representative point (epoch=19, mean seen-accuracy 51%, deterministic — matches the epoch-sweep table's epoch=19 row exactly):

| Method | AUROC (mean ± std over 5 seeds) |
|---|---|
| Semantic entropy | **0.772 ± 0.068** |
| Lexical entropy | 0.768 ± 0.074 |
| Token predictive entropy | 0.615 ± 0.077 |
| Random baseline | ~0.5 |

| Epochs | Mean acc | Token AUROC | Lexical AUROC | Semantic AUROC |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 15 | 0.16 | 0.483 | 0.765 | 0.698 |
| 17 | 0.29 | 0.635 | 0.761 | 0.779 |
| 19 | 0.51 | 0.615 | 0.768 | 0.772 |
| 21 | 0.82 | 0.628 | 0.835 | 0.864 |
| 23 | 0.94 | 0.674 | 0.907 | 0.927 |

Two findings the sweep makes visible, now on solid deterministic footing: (i) the token-vs-cluster gap is robust across the whole sweep — cluster-based signals beat token entropy at every operating point tried; (ii) with the hash-seed bug fixed, semantic entropy leads lexical at 4 of 5 operating points (17/19/21/23), and only lexical edges ahead at the most severely underfit point (epoch 15, 16% accuracy). This is a different — and more favorable to semantic clustering — picture than our previous (buggy) revision reported, where the ordering appeared to flip almost randomly across the sweep. The margin is still modest (≤0.02 AUROC at most points) and we do not claim it is large, but it is now at least a *stable* small effect rather than noise dressed up as a flip-flop.

### 3.4 Noisy-extractor pilot (simulating an imperfect entailment classifier)

Injecting random mislabeling into the oracle entity extractor at rate *p*, at the epoch=19 operating point (deterministic, re-run under the fix):

| Extraction noise *p* | Lexical AUROC | Semantic AUROC |
|---|---|---|
| 0.0 (oracle) | 0.768 | 0.772 |
| 0.1 | 0.783 | 0.765 |
| 0.2 | 0.768 | 0.789 |
| 0.3 | 0.737 | 0.736 |

Semantic entropy's small oracle-extractor edge (0.772 vs 0.768) is not preserved cleanly as noise increases: it disappears at p=0.1, reappears at p=0.2, and both methods converge at p=0.3. This confirms the qualitative conclusion from our previous revision even though the underlying numbers shifted: the semantic-over-lexical margin is small enough that a realistic (imperfect) entailment classifier's error rate would be enough to erase or reverse it, so a practitioner should not expect semantic clustering's added machinery to reliably beat cheap exact-match clustering in a setting this template-constrained — even though, per §3.3, the *oracle*-extractor version of that edge is now a real, reproducible (if modest) effect rather than sampling noise.

### 3.5 Honest limitations

- Model/task remain intentionally toy-scale (0.1M-param Transformer, closed 50-entity vocabulary, 4×4 templates) for a CPU, few-minute budget; results are a controlled proof-of-concept, not a claim about frontier LLM behavior.
- "Semantic clustering" still uses an oracle entity extractor, not a learned entailment model; §3.4's noise injection is a simplified proxy for classifier imperfection (uniform mislabeling), not a simulation of any specific entailment model's real error distribution.
- We still do not run a conformal-prediction baseline; the risk-coverage-style comparisons here remain descriptive, without finite-sample coverage guarantees.
- The bug we found (hash-seed-dependent tie-breaking) is a reminder that "deterministic" claims need to be verified at the process-launch boundary, not just checked for torch/BLAS-level reproducibility within a script; we would not have caught this without the reviewer's independent rerun, and we now treat cross-process reruns as a required check, not an optional one.
- We did not have budget in this revision to also rerun the main-setting numbers at additional seed counts beyond 5; §3.1's near-ceiling AUROCs leave little room for the tie-break bug to matter, but a fuller seed sweep there is future work.

## 4. Conclusion

The advantage of semantic (meaning-level) entropy over naive token-level predictive entropy for detecting wrong LLM answers is real but regime-dependent: invisible (all methods tie near ceiling) when "wrong" is confounded with "completely novel to the model," but present once that confound is removed and only genuine in-distribution errors remain — and this gap holds up across an epoch-sensitivity sweep, not just one hand-picked operating point. Unlike our previous revision, which reported an apparently random flip-flop between semantic and lexical clustering and attributed it to genuine statistical indistinguishability, this revision traces that flip-flop to a specific, fixable bug (hash-seed-dependent tie-breaking) and shows that once fixed, semantic clustering has a small but consistently reproducible edge over lexical clustering at 4 of 5 tested operating points. That edge remains small (≤0.02 AUROC) and is not robust to a simple imperfect-extractor pilot, so we still do not claim semantic clustering is worth its added complexity over exact-match clustering in a low-paraphrase-diversity setting like this one — but the finding itself, and the paper's reproducibility, now rest on a verified deterministic pipeline rather than an unverified claim. Future work: (1) scale paraphrase diversity to test whether a larger, more robust semantic-over-lexical gap emerges; (2) add a genuine conformal-prediction baseline with coverage guarantees; (3) replace uniform-noise extractor simulation with an actual learned entailment classifier; (4) repeat the seen/unseen decomposition on a real pretrained LLM.

## Reproducibility

`experiment.py` implements the full pipeline (KB generation, from-scratch training, sampling, evaluation) with pinned single-threaded, deterministic torch execution *and* a deterministic (hash-seed-independent) tie-break in entity extraction. Modes: `main`, `ablation`, `epoch_sweep`, `noisy_extractor`, `repeat_determinism`, each writing its own `results_<mode>.json`. All numbers in this paper were regenerated from scratch on CPU with no network access and no pretrained weights, and every mode used in §3.2–3.4 was verified to reproduce bit-for-bit across independent process launches, not just within one; total runtime for all reported experiments was under 15 minutes.
