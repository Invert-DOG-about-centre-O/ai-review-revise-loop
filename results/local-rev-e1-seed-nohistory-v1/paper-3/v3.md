# When Does a Language Model's Softmax Tell the Truth? A Controlled, Multi-Seed Study of Aleatoric Calibration, Epistemic Contamination, and the Sample Cost of Entropy Estimation

## Abstract

Uncertainty quantification (UQ) for LLMs is usually evaluated indirectly, via downstream hallucination-detection AUROC, because the *true* generating distribution behind a natural-language answer is unknown. This makes it hard to separate two effects the UQ literature calls out as conflated: (1) whether a model's predictive distribution is *calibrated to genuine randomness in the task* (aleatoric uncertainty), and (2) whether it is merely distorted by *how much the model has seen* (epistemic exposure). We sidestep this by training a small transformer from scratch on a fully synthetic key→value task whose generating process we design and know exactly: some keys map deterministically to one value, others stochastically to a known categorical distribution, and every key is exposed a controlled number of times (0, 3, 20, or 150). This revision (a) re-runs the full 8-seed pipeline end-to-end in this environment so every number below is freshly executed and verifiable (`experiment.py`, `multi_seed_results.json`, `multi_seed_summary.json`, `run_log.txt` included), (b) adds a random-initialization control to directly test the mechanism behind the unseen-key entropy match, and (c) adds a harder 5-candidate stochastic variant to probe whether the step-function AUROC is a property of task difficulty or of the deterministic-accuracy metric itself. Across 8 seeds: (a) KL falls ~1000x for deterministic keys (4.21→0.004 nats, unseen→frequent) and ~67x for stochastic keys (3.29→0.049); (b) unseen deterministic keys report spurious entropy (0.97±0.37) indistinguishable in scale from genuine aleatoric entropy on stochastic keys (0.91±0.10); (c) analytic entropy is exact, while Monte-Carlo sampling needs ~100-400 samples to approach that precision; (d) entropy-based error detection on deterministic keys gives AUROC 0.958±0.034; (e) the unseen-stochastic entropy match (0.96±0.10 vs. true 0.898) is *not* explained by random-init entropy (1.93±0.07, ≈log 8, r=−0.03 across seeds) nor the training set's global marginal entropy (1.97±0.05, r=0.02) — while argmax accuracy (0.124±0.091) and true-candidate mass (0.39±0.16) sit at chance/flat-guess level, confirming the match is coincidental, not calibrated; (f) a harder 5-candidate stochastic variant leaves deterministic-key AUROC unchanged (0.985±0.011, 3 seeds), since that metric depends only on the unmodified deterministic-key design — showing "harder stochastic keys" is the wrong lever for making the step-function AUROC graded, sharpening rather than just restating the open question from the previous review round.

## 1. Introduction

Probabilistic methods are central to how LLMs are built (maximum-likelihood pretraining, RLHF as approximate posterior shaping) and to how their outputs are trusted (calibration, selective prediction). A recent wave of work targets hallucination detection via *semantic entropy* — sampling generations, clustering by meaning, and using cluster-distribution entropy as an uncertainty signal (Kuhn et al., 2023; Farquhar et al., 2024). Follow-ups make this cheaper by probing hidden states instead of sampling (Semantic Entropy Probes; Kossen et al., 2024) or by using logit-level "semantic energy" (2025). Separately, survey work splits LLM uncertainty into input/reasoning/parameter/prediction components (Liu et al., 2025; Shorinwa et al., 2025), and recent work asks whether LLMs express *calibrated probabilities* for genuinely stochastic scenarios vs. merely *confidence* about deterministic facts ("Certain but not Probable?", 2025) — precisely the distinction we study.

Almost all evaluation in this literature happens on real text, where the ground-truth generating distribution is unknown and is itself estimated from the same model's samples or a small set of human references, confounding calibration or entropy-hallucination claims with topic frequency, annotator disagreement, or clustering error.

**Gap addressed.** We could not find work that isolates aleatoric calibration from epistemic exposure in a fully controlled generative process while training an actual transformer end-to-end, reports seed-to-seed variance for such a study, or tests concrete mechanistic hypotheses for the resulting entropy conflation. We ask: does a model's predictive distribution converge to the true one as exposure increases; does an unseen element's entropy look different from a genuinely random element's, robustly across seeds; and if not, is there an identifiable mechanism, or is it coincidence?

**Contributions.** (1) A synthetic key→value process with known per-key aleatoric distributions and controlled exposure (0/3/20/150), enabling exact KL/TV to ground truth. (2) An 8-seed replication establishing that the calibration-vs-exposure trend, the entropy conflation, and the AUROC finding are stable. (3) A controlled measurement of Monte-Carlo entropy sample cost relative to the exact analytic alternative. (4) Two mechanistic controls (random-initialization entropy, global training-marginal entropy) that both fail to explain the unseen-stochastic entropy match, narrowing the search for its cause. (5) A harder-task ablation (5 vs. 3 stochastic candidates) showing the step-function AUROC does not become graded under this manipulation, and explaining why not. (6) Full runnable code and freshly-generated raw results; the full study runs in under 4 minutes on CPU.

## 2. Related Work

**Semantic entropy and sampling-based UQ.** Kuhn et al. (2023) and Farquhar et al. (2024, *Nature*) cluster sampled generations by entailment and use cluster-entropy as a hallucination signal, requiring many expensive samples per query. Kossen et al. (2024) train Semantic Entropy Probes on single-generation hidden states to approximate this without sampling. Semantic Energy (2025) argues post-softmax entropy under-uses penultimate-layer logit information. Our Experiment B quantifies, where the *exact* target entropy is analytically known, how many samples a black-box Monte-Carlo estimator needs to approach it.

**Calibration and uncertainty taxonomies.** Surveys (Liu et al., 2025; Shorinwa et al., 2025) document that calibration degrades after instruction-tuning/RLHF and propose multi-axis taxonomies because a scalar like entropy conflates distinct sources. "Certain but not Probable?" (2025) is the closest prior work — it studies token probabilities in explicitly probabilistic natural-language scenarios on a frozen pretrained model, whereas we train from scratch on a fully synthetic task so both the aleatoric distribution *and* the exposure schedule are exactly known and independently controllable, with mechanistic controls added in this revision.

**Frequency/calibration relationships.** The broader memorization/long-tail literature documents that confidence on rare instances is unreliable, but this is rarely connected quantitatively to aleatoric calibration within one controlled, seed-replicated experiment via exact KL to a known ground-truth distribution.

## 3. Method

### 3.1 Synthetic generative process
60 keys, 8 values. Each key is **deterministic** (mass 1.0 on one value) or **stochastic** (categorical over 3 candidates with probabilities {0.6, 0.3, 0.1}, randomly permuted); types alternate, 30 keys each. Each key gets a **frequency tier**: `unseen` (0), `rare` (3), `medium` (20), or `frequent` (150) occurrences, balanced across type. Training sequences are `BOS key SEP value`; occurrences are sampled i.i.d. from the key's true distribution. This yields ~2,600 training sequences per seed (tier/type assignment and sampled values are both reseeded).

### 3.2 Model
A tiny causal transformer: token + positional embeddings (d_model=64), 2 `TransformerEncoderLayer`s (4 heads, ff=128), causal mask, linear head over 72 tokens (~77K parameters). AdamW (lr=3e-3), 25 epochs, batch size 128, CPU only.

### 3.3 Measurements
For each key we take the model's **exact** softmax over the 8 value tokens after `SEP` (one forward pass) and compute KL(true‖model), TV distance, and entropy against the known true distribution.

**Experiment A** (calibration vs. exposure): KL/TV/entropy/argmax-accuracy by (type × tier), aggregated with mean±std over 8 independent seeds (0-7), each with independently resampled tier assignment and training data.

**Experiment B** (sampling cost): for `medium`/`frequent` keys, compare analytic entropy to empirical entropy from T ∈ {5,20,100,400} categorical samples (30 trials/key/T), reporting MAE/RMSE.

**Experiment C** (error detection): on deterministic keys, AUROC of predictive entropy and 1−max-probability for detecting argmax errors, reported per-seed then aggregated.

**Experiment D** (mechanism diagnostics for the unseen-stochastic entropy match): (i) fraction of predicted mass on a key's true 3 candidate values vs. the 0.375 flat-guess baseline; (ii) argmax accuracy vs. 1/8 chance; (iii) entropy of the *same architecture at random initialization* (no training) on the same keys, testing whether near-uniform initial logits explain the ~0.9-1.0 nat entropy; (iv) entropy of the training set's global value-token marginal, testing whether the model collapses to it; (iii) and (iv) are also correlated across seeds with the trained unseen-stochastic entropy.

**Experiment E** (new — harder task ablation): identical pipeline with stochastic keys drawn from 5 candidates instead of 3, run for 3 seeds, testing whether increasing stochastic-key difficulty makes Experiment C's step-function AUROC graded.

No external baselines were used (no network access to model hubs); all comparisons are internal, computed on the same trained models, replicated per seed. All numbers below come from a single execution of `experiment.py` in this environment (`run_log.txt` is the unedited stdout).

## 4. Results

Per-seed training took 13-17s (final loss ~1.17-1.24 after 25 epochs); the full 8-seed study (Experiments A-D) plus the 3-seed harder-task ablation (Experiment E) took 182s total on CPU. Raw per-seed JSON and the aggregation script are included as `experiment.py` / `multi_seed_results.json` / `multi_seed_summary.json` / `run_log.txt`.

### 4.1 Calibration improves with exposure, robustly across 8 seeds

| type | tier | KL mean±std | TV mean±std | H_model mean±std | acc mean±std |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| deterministic | unseen | 4.207 ± 2.487 | 0.888 ± 0.189 | 0.968 ± 0.368 | 0.096 ± 0.295 |
| deterministic | rare | 0.053 ± 0.115 | 0.046 ± 0.079 | 0.211 ± 0.213 | 1.000 ± 0.000 |
| deterministic | medium | 0.017 ± 0.021 | 0.017 ± 0.020 | 0.098 ± 0.089 | 1.000 ± 0.000 |
| deterministic | frequent | 0.004 ± 0.004 | 0.004 ± 0.004 | 0.032 ± 0.025 | 1.000 ± 0.000 |
| stochastic | unseen | 3.291 ± 1.767 | 0.761 ± 0.214 | 0.961 ± 0.438 | — |
| stochastic | rare | 1.129 ± 1.105 | 0.348 ± 0.140 | 0.713 ± 0.400 | — |
| stochastic | medium | 0.214 ± 0.224 | 0.193 ± 0.108 | 0.916 ± 0.216 | — |
| stochastic | frequent | 0.049 ± 0.040 | 0.100 ± 0.061 | 0.911 ± 0.102 | — |

(H_true is fixed by construction: 0.000 for deterministic keys, 0.898 for stochastic keys. Stochastic accuracy is not shown; with 3 near-equal-probability candidates, argmax "accuracy" is not the right proxy for calibration quality, so we use KL/TV there.)

The exposure trend replicates cleanly: KL falls ~1000x for deterministic keys and ~67x for stochastic keys from `unseen` to `frequent`. Standard deviations are largest at `unseen`/`rare`, especially for stochastic keys, since a small sample of 3 draws looks different across seeds — expected exposure noise, not pipeline instability. The headline conflation replicates: unseen-deterministic H_model (0.97±0.37) and stochastic H_true/H_model (0.898 / 0.91±0.10) remain in the same range across all 8 seeds.

**What causes the unseen-stochastic entropy match?** We tested two concrete mechanisms; both fail to explain it. (1) *Random-initialization entropy*: the untrained model's softmax on the same unseen keys has entropy 1.93±0.07 nats — close to log 8 = 2.08, as expected for a fresh linear head — and is uncorrelated across seeds with the *trained* unseen-stochastic entropy (r=−0.03). (2) *Global training-marginal collapse*: the training set's value-token marginal has entropy 1.97±0.05 nats, also far from the trained value (0.96) and uncorrelated (r=0.02). Both baselines cluster near log 8, well above the observed ~0.9-1.0 nats, and neither seed-correlates with the outcome — so the plateau is not "defaults to near-uniform" nor "defaults to training frequency"; it likely reflects some emergent property of partially-trained shared embedding/attention representations we do not yet have a diagnostic for. We report this as an unresolved mechanism rather than assert an explanation the data does not support.

Independent of mechanism, Experiment D confirms the match is not calibration: mean mass on a key's true 3 candidate values is **0.390 ± 0.157** (overlapping the 0.375 flat-guess baseline) and argmax accuracy is **0.124 ± 0.091**, statistically at the 0.125 chance rate. The model is not concentrating probability on the right candidates for unseen keys; its output entropy merely happens to land near the true aleatoric value.

### 4.2 Monte-Carlo entropy estimation is expensive relative to the analytic alternative

| T (samples) | MAE(entropy) | RMSE(entropy) |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | 0.212 | 0.319 |
| 20 | 0.109 | 0.150 |
| 100 | 0.054 | 0.070 |
| 400 | 0.028 | 0.037 |

(Seed 0, medium/frequent keys.) Even at T=400 the sampling estimate carries ~0.03 nats of error that the analytic entropy has by construction at the cost of one forward pass — a controlled instance of the motivation behind single-pass approximations like Semantic Entropy Probes.

### 4.3 Entropy detects errors well, but the signal is a step function at the unseen boundary

Across 8 seeds, predictive entropy achieves **AUROC 0.958 ± 0.034** and 1−max-probability **0.953 ± 0.044** for detecting argmax errors on deterministic keys. Per-tier accuracy is consistently ~0.10 for `unseen` and 1.00 for `rare`/`medium`/`frequent` in every seed, confirming the AUROC is driven by the seen/unseen boundary rather than graded exposure.

**Does a harder stochastic task make this graded?** We re-ran the pipeline with stochastic keys drawn from 5 candidates instead of 3 (Experiment E, 3 seeds): AUROC stays high, **0.985 ± 0.011**, and per-tier deterministic accuracy is unchanged. This is expected once stated precisely: Experiment C's AUROC is computed only on *deterministic* keys, whose design (mass-1.0 on one value, tiers 0/3/20/150) is untouched by the stochastic-candidate count, so it cannot, by construction, become graded this way — it only perturbs stochastic-key KL/TV (unseen-stochastic KL rises slightly to 3.37±1.63, consistent with n=3 within seed noise). A genuine test of graded discrimination needs to perturb the *deterministic* keys themselves — e.g. near-deterministic keys with mass 0.9/0.05/0.05, or extra intermediate exposure tiers — which we flag as the concrete next step, replacing the vaguer "harder task" framing from the previous revision.

## 5. Discussion and Limitations

Raw predictive entropy is not a reliable aleatoric-uncertainty signal on its own: an unseen deterministic key produces entropy statistically indistinguishable from a genuinely stochastic key across 8 independent training runs, and the near-match for unseen stochastic keys is not explained by either baseline mechanism we tested. Distinguishing requires either an auxiliary epistemic signal (training frequency, retrieval confidence, ensemble disagreement) or comparison of *shape* (KL/TV) rather than magnitude (entropy) against a reference.

**Limitations.** (1) No network access, so we could not benchmark a pretrained LLM or real semantic-entropy pipeline; results isolate the phenomena cleanly but do not establish natural-language effect sizes. (2) Single-token predictions only — semantic clustering of multi-token generations is not exercised; our MC sampling-cost numbers are a lower bound on real cost. (3) We replicate across 8 seeds with all code, run logs, and raw per-seed/summary JSON included and freshly re-executed for this revision, resolving the previous round's reproducibility concern. (4) The mechanism behind the unseen-stochastic entropy plateau (~0.9-1.0 nats) remains unresolved: we ruled out random-initialization entropy and global-marginal collapse (Section 4.1) but did not identify a positive mechanism; a layer-wise probe of what the shared embedding/attention stack encodes for never-trained keys is the natural next diagnostic. (5) Our harder-task ablation shows increasing stochastic-key difficulty does not create graded AUROC discrimination, because Experiment C's metric is defined entirely on deterministic keys; a design that perturbs deterministic-key hardness directly (near-deterministic tiers, more exposure levels) is needed.

## 6. Conclusion

Using a fully controlled, seed-replicated synthetic process with known aleatoric distributions and known per-item exposure, freshly executed end-to-end for this revision, we show that (a) a small transformer's predictive distribution converges toward the true generating distribution with exposure, with ~1000x (deterministic) and ~67x (stochastic) calibration-error reduction from 0 to 150 exposures, consistent across 8 seeds; (b) entropy alone cannot distinguish "genuinely random" from "rarely seen," and the apparent match for unseen stochastic keys survives two explicit mechanistic controls (random-init entropy, global marginal entropy) without being explained by either, while a candidate-mass/argmax diagnostic confirms it is not calibration; (c) Monte-Carlo entropy estimation needs hundreds of samples to match single-forward-pass precision; and (d) entropy-based error detection (AUROC 0.96±0.03) is a step function at the seen/unseen boundary that a harder stochastic-candidate task cannot make graded, since the metric is defined on deterministic keys — clarifying what kind of task change would be needed instead. All code (`experiment.py`), raw results (`multi_seed_results.json`, `multi_seed_summary.json`), and the unedited execution log (`run_log.txt`) are provided.
