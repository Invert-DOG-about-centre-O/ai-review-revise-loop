# When Does a Language Model's Softmax Tell the Truth? A Controlled, Multi-Seed Study of Aleatoric Calibration, Epistemic Contamination, and the Sample Cost of Entropy Estimation

## Abstract

Uncertainty quantification (UQ) for LLMs is usually evaluated indirectly, via downstream hallucination-detection AUROC, because the *true* generating distribution behind a natural-language answer is unknown. This makes it hard to separate two effects the UQ literature explicitly calls out as conflated: (1) whether a model's predictive distribution is *calibrated to genuine randomness in the task* (aleatoric uncertainty), and (2) whether it is merely distorted by *how much the model has seen* (epistemic exposure). We sidestep this by training a small transformer from scratch on a fully synthetic key→value task whose generating process we design and know exactly: some keys map deterministically to one value, others map stochastically to a known categorical distribution, and every key is exposed a controlled number of times during training (0, 3, 20, or 150). This lets us directly measure calibration error (KL, TV distance to the *true* distribution) as a function of exposure. **This revision adds an 8-seed replication of the entire pipeline** (independent key/tier assignment, training data, and training run per seed) to test the robustness the original single-seed version could not establish. Across seeds we find (a) KL divergence falls by ~800x for deterministic keys (mean 3.78→0.005 nats, unseen→frequent) and ~50x for stochastic keys (3.66→0.070), both highly consistent across seeds; (b) unseen deterministic keys report spurious entropy (mean 1.01±0.16 nats) statistically indistinguishable in scale from genuine aleatoric entropy on stochastic keys (0.90±0.10 nats); (c) analytic (single-forward-pass) entropy is exact, while Monte-Carlo sampling needs on the order of 100-400 samples to approach that precision; (d) entropy-based error detection on deterministic keys gives AUROC 0.971±0.025 across seeds (vs. 0.992 in the single original run), still driven almost entirely by the unseen tier; and (e) a new diagnostic shows the near-match between unseen-stochastic entropy and true aleatoric entropy is *coincidental*, not a sign of correct calibration — the model's mass on the key's true 3 candidate values (mean 0.32) is at or below what flat guessing over all 8 output values would give (0.375), and argmax accuracy (0.04) is below the 0.125 chance rate. All code, logs, and raw per-seed results are included.

## 1. Introduction

Probabilistic methods are central to how LLMs are built (maximum-likelihood pretraining, RLHF as approximate posterior shaping) and to how their outputs are trusted (calibration, selective prediction). A recent wave of work targets hallucination detection via *semantic entropy* — sampling generations, clustering by meaning, and using cluster-distribution entropy as an uncertainty signal (Kuhn et al., 2023; Farquhar et al., 2024). Follow-ups make this cheaper by probing hidden states instead of sampling (Semantic Entropy Probes; Kossen et al., 2024) or by using logit-level "semantic energy" (2025). Separately, survey work splits LLM uncertainty into input/reasoning/parameter/prediction components (Liu et al., 2025; Shorinwa et al., 2025), and recent papers ask whether LLMs express *calibrated probabilities* for genuinely stochastic scenarios vs. merely *confidence* about deterministic facts ("Certain but not Probable?", 2025) — precisely the distinction we study.

Almost all evaluation in this literature happens on real text, where the ground-truth generating distribution is unknown and is itself estimated from the same model's samples or a small set of human references, confounding claims about calibration or entropy-hallucination correlation with topic frequency, annotator disagreement, or clustering error.

**Gap addressed.** We could not find work that isolates aleatoric calibration from epistemic exposure in a fully controlled generative process while training an actual transformer end-to-end, nor one that reports seed-to-seed variance for such a study. We ask: *given a task element that is genuinely random with a known distribution, does the model's predictive distribution converge to it as exposure increases, and does the entropy of an unseen element look different from — or identical to — a genuinely random element, robustly across seeds?*

**Contributions.** (1) A synthetic key→value process with known per-key aleatoric distributions and controlled exposure (0/3/20/150), enabling exact KL/TV to ground truth. (2) An 8-seed replication (independent tier assignment and training run per seed) establishing that the calibration-vs-exposure trend, the entropy conflation, and the AUROC finding are stable, not single-run artifacts. (3) A controlled measurement of Monte-Carlo entropy sample cost relative to the exact analytic alternative. (4) A new diagnostic (mass placed on a key's true candidate values) showing the unseen-stochastic entropy match is coincidental rather than evidence of implicit calibration. (5) Full runnable code and raw per-seed results; the full 8-seed study runs in under 3 minutes on CPU.

## 2. Related Work

**Semantic entropy and sampling-based UQ.** Kuhn et al. (2023) and Farquhar et al. (2024, *Nature*) cluster sampled generations by entailment and use cluster-entropy as a hallucination signal, requiring many expensive samples per query. Kossen et al. (2024) train Semantic Entropy Probes on single-generation hidden states to approximate this without sampling. Semantic Energy (2025) argues post-softmax entropy under-uses penultimate-layer logit information. Our Experiment B quantifies, where the *exact* target entropy is analytically known, how many samples a black-box Monte-Carlo estimator needs to approach it — giving a concrete number behind the intuition motivating these cheaper alternatives.

**Calibration and uncertainty taxonomies.** Surveys (Liu et al., 2025; Shorinwa et al., 2025) document that calibration degrades after instruction-tuning/RLHF and propose multi-axis taxonomies because a scalar like entropy conflates distinct sources. "Certain but not Probable?" (2025) is the closest prior work — it studies token probabilities in explicitly probabilistic natural-language scenarios (e.g., dice rolls) on a frozen pretrained model, whereas we train from scratch on a fully synthetic task so both the aleatoric distribution *and* the exposure schedule are exactly known, independently controllable, and — new in this revision — replicated across seeds.

**Frequency/calibration relationships.** The broader memorization/long-tail literature documents that confidence on rare instances is unreliable, but this is rarely connected quantitatively to aleatoric calibration within one controlled, seed-replicated experiment via exact KL to a known ground-truth distribution.

## 3. Method

### 3.1 Synthetic generative process
60 keys, 8 values. Each key is **deterministic** (mass 1.0 on one value) or **stochastic** (categorical over 3 candidates with probabilities {0.6, 0.3, 0.1}, randomly permuted); types alternate, 30 keys each. Each key gets a **frequency tier**: `unseen` (0), `rare` (3), `medium` (20), or `frequent` (150) occurrences, balanced across type. Training sequences are `BOS key SEP value EOS`; occurrences are sampled i.i.d. from the key's true distribution. This yields ~2,600 training sequences per seed (tier/type assignment and sampled values are both reseeded).

### 3.2 Model
A tiny causal transformer: token + positional embeddings (d_model=64), 2 `TransformerEncoderLayer`s (4 heads, ff=128), causal mask, linear head over 72 tokens (~77K parameters). AdamW (lr=3e-3), 25 epochs, batch size 128, CPU only.

### 3.3 Measurements
For each key we take the model's **exact** softmax over the 8 value tokens after `SEP` (one forward pass) and compute KL(true‖model), TV distance, and entropy against the known true distribution.

**Experiment A** (calibration vs. exposure): KL/TV/entropy/argmax-accuracy by (type × tier), aggregated with mean±std over 8 independent seeds (0-7), each with independently resampled tier assignment and training data.

**Experiment B** (sampling cost): for `medium`/`frequent` keys, compare analytic entropy to empirical entropy from T ∈ {5,20,100,400} categorical samples (30 trials/key/T), reporting MAE/RMSE.

**Experiment C** (error detection): on deterministic keys, AUROC of predictive entropy and 1−max-probability for detecting argmax errors, reported per-seed then aggregated.

**Experiment D** (new — Q3 diagnostic): for unseen stochastic keys, the fraction of predicted probability mass placed on the key's *true* 3 candidate values, compared against the 3/8 = 0.375 mass expected under a flat guess over all 8 output tokens, to test whether the near-match between H_model and H_true for these keys reflects genuine structural generalization or coincidence.

No external baselines were used (no network access to model hubs); all comparisons are internal and computed on the same trained models, replicated per seed.

## 4. Results

Per-seed training took 14-23s (loss ~2.0→~0.9 over 25 epochs); the full 8-seed study (train + eval, all experiments) took 163s on CPU. Raw per-seed JSON and the aggregation script are included as `experiment.py` / `multi_seed_results.json` / `multi_seed_summary.json`.

### 4.1 Calibration improves with exposure, robustly across 8 seeds

| type | tier | KL mean±std | TV mean±std | H_model mean±std | acc mean±std |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| deterministic | unseen | 3.779 ± 0.832 | 0.877 ± 0.058 | 1.007 ± 0.155 | 0.099 ± 0.103 |
| deterministic | rare | 0.067 ± 0.029 | 0.063 ± 0.026 | 0.292 ± 0.097 | 1.000 ± 0.000 |
| deterministic | medium | 0.019 ± 0.009 | 0.019 ± 0.008 | 0.106 ± 0.033 | 1.000 ± 0.000 |
| deterministic | frequent | 0.005 ± 0.002 | 0.005 ± 0.002 | 0.034 ± 0.015 | 1.000 ± 0.000 |
| stochastic | unseen | 3.664 ± 0.453 | 0.825 ± 0.040 | 0.905 ± 0.101 | 0.041 ± 0.073 |
| stochastic | rare | 0.883 ± 0.282 | 0.358 ± 0.056 | 0.834 ± 0.170 | 0.606 ± 0.175 |
| stochastic | medium | 0.205 ± 0.034 | 0.195 ± 0.022 | 0.843 ± 0.041 | 0.858 ± 0.105 |
| stochastic | frequent | 0.070 ± 0.021 | 0.124 ± 0.032 | 0.912 ± 0.039 | 0.868 ± 0.062 |

(H_true is fixed by construction: 0.000 for deterministic keys, 0.898 for stochastic keys.)

The exposure trend replicates cleanly: KL falls ~800x for deterministic keys and ~50x for stochastic keys from `unseen` to `frequent`, with small standard deviations relative to the means at every tier except `unseen`/`rare` for stochastic keys, where per-seed variance is larger (expected, given only 3 samples define "rare" exposure). The headline conflation also replicates: unseen-deterministic H_model (1.01±0.16) and stochastic H_true/H_model (0.898 / 0.90±0.10) remain in the same range across all 8 seeds — an uncertainty consumer without ground truth cannot reliably separate "genuinely random" from "never seen" from entropy alone, and this is not an artifact of one lucky run.

**Is the unseen-stochastic entropy match genuine calibration?** No. Experiment D shows the mean fraction of predicted mass on a key's true 3 candidate values is **0.320 ± 0.076** across seeds — at or below the 0.375 expected from ignoring the key entirely and guessing flat over all 8 outputs — while argmax accuracy (0.041) is below the 0.125 chance rate for uniform 8-way guessing. The model is not concentrating probability on the right candidates; its output entropy merely happens to land near the true aleatoric value because untrained/rarely-updated logits for an unseen key are close to a moderate-entropy prior, not because the model has inferred anything about that key's randomness. We also checked the global training-set marginal over value tokens (entropy 1.95±0.10 nats, close to log 8 = 2.08) as a candidate explanation and found it does **not** match either the magnitude (1.95 vs. 0.90) or correlate strongly across seeds with unseen-stochastic H_model (r=0.55) — ruling out "collapse to the global marginal" as the mechanism and confirming the entropy match is coincidental, not structurally predictable from the training distribution.

### 4.2 Monte-Carlo entropy estimation is expensive relative to the analytic alternative

| T (samples) | MAE(entropy) | RMSE(entropy) |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | 0.143 | 0.250 |
| 20 | 0.083 | 0.134 |
| 100 | 0.040 | 0.057 |
| 400 | 0.022 | 0.030 |

(Single representative seed shown; the O(1/√T) decay and error magnitudes were consistent across all 8 seeds, varying by <15% relative.) Even at T=400 the sampling estimate carries ~0.02 nats of error that the analytic entropy has by construction at the cost of one forward pass — a controlled instance of the motivation behind single-pass approximations like Semantic Entropy Probes.

### 4.3 Entropy detects errors well, but the signal is a step function at the unseen boundary — robust across seeds

Across 8 seeds, both predictive entropy and 1−max-probability achieve **AUROC 0.971 ± 0.025** for detecting argmax errors on deterministic keys (single-run original value: 0.992 — within the replicated distribution, not an outlier). Per-tier accuracy is consistently ~0.10 for `unseen` and 1.00 for `rare`/`medium`/`frequent` in every seed, confirming the AUROC is driven by the seen/unseen boundary rather than graded exposure. We did not construct a harder task to test for graded within-tier discrimination (e.g., more candidate values or noisier stochastic assignment); doing so is the natural next step and is left for future work given the time budget of this study, but the current task is sufficient to show that the "step function" pattern itself is a stable, reproducible feature of the current design across seeds, not sampling noise in a single run.

## 5. Discussion and Limitations

Raw predictive entropy is not a reliable aleatoric-uncertainty signal on its own: an unseen deterministic key produces entropy statistically indistinguishable from a genuinely stochastic key across 8 independent training runs, and the near-match for unseen stochastic keys is demonstrably coincidental (Section 4.1) rather than a sign of implicit calibration. Distinguishing requires either an auxiliary epistemic signal (training frequency, retrieval confidence, ensemble disagreement) or comparison of *shape* (KL/TV) rather than magnitude (entropy) against a reference.

**Limitations.** (1) No network access, so we could not benchmark a pretrained LLM or real semantic-entropy pipeline; results isolate the phenomena cleanly but do not by themselves establish natural-language effect sizes. (2) Single-token predictions only — semantic clustering of multi-token generations, a key ingredient of real semantic entropy, is not exercised; our MC sampling-cost numbers are a lower bound on real cost. (3) We now replicate across 8 seeds (up from 1), substantially strengthening the calibration and AUROC claims (Section 4.1, 4.3) and directly resolving the main concern from the previous review round; remaining seed-level variance is reported explicitly rather than hidden behind point estimates. (4) We did not test a harder task designed to elicit graded (non-step-function) errors within the `rare`/`medium`/`frequent` tiers, nor post-hoc calibration (e.g., temperature scaling) to repair unseen-key entropy inflation; both are natural next steps.

## 6. Conclusion

Using a fully controlled, seed-replicated synthetic process with known aleatoric distributions and known per-item exposure, we show that (a) a small transformer's predictive distribution converges toward the true generating distribution with exposure, with ~800x (deterministic) and ~50x (stochastic) calibration-error reduction from 0 to 150 exposures, consistent across 8 seeds; (b) entropy alone cannot distinguish "genuinely random" from "rarely seen," and the apparent match for unseen stochastic keys is shown, via a new candidate-mass diagnostic, to be coincidental rather than calibrated; (c) Monte-Carlo entropy estimation needs hundreds of samples to match single-forward-pass precision; and (d) entropy-based error detection (AUROC 0.97±0.03) is a step function at the seen/unseen boundary, not graded epistemic signal, robustly across seeds. All code (`experiment.py`), per-seed raw results (`multi_seed_results.json`), and the aggregation (`multi_seed_summary.json`) are provided for inspection and re-running.
