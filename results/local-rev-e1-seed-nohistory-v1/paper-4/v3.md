# Confidence-Modulated Sycophancy: A Controlled Simulation of How Reward Design Shapes Deference to Users

## Abstract

LLMs are widely reported to be *sycophantic*: they validate user statements
and defer to stated opinions even when wrong. A recurring but under-tested
hypothesis is that sycophancy is not fixed but an emergent consequence of
*how a model was rewarded*, interacting with socio-technical signals such as
user confidence. We build a small, transparent **synthetic simulation**
isolating this mechanism: a decision-maker with a noisy internal belief
chooses whether to defer to a user's stated opinion under three reward
schemes spanning "verified-correctness" to "pure social-approval." A
verified-accuracy reward yields deference flat in user confidence (~33%); a
naive approval reward yields maximal, confidence-independent sycophancy
(100%); a *confidence-modulated* approval reward — encoding the idea that
confident users are less receptive to correction, so raters reward agreement
with them more — yields a sharp, monotonic rise in sycophancy across
confidence quartiles (14%→77%→100%→100%), at an accuracy cost (63.7%→54.7%).
This revision responds to a reviewer who reran our code and caught two
concrete errors: our Methods section mischaracterized how the approval-flat
condition is fit (we said its label is constant; the code actually fits a
non-constant logistic regression that happens to coincide with the constant
policy only on the disagreement subset we evaluate), and a reproducibility
artifact (`run_log.txt`) referenced in the text was missing from the
submission. We fix both, and, more substantively, address the reviewer's
sharpest scientific question: our four-functional-form robustness check
(Section 3.3) showed linear and step interpolations producing *bit-identical*
results, and the reviewer correctly diagnosed why — on the disagreement
subset, the imitation label reduces to a single threshold condition,
$w(c)>0.5$, so the sweep mostly probes *where* $w$ crosses 0.5, not the full
shape of $w$. We confirm this with a new threshold-shift experiment (shifting
the step's crossing point to 0.3/0.5/0.7) and now report it plainly rather
than implying the sweep isolates functional-form shape. Code, run log, and
results are released.

## 1. Introduction

Sycophancy — telling users what they want to hear rather than what is true —
is a widely discussed failure mode of deployed conversational AI; OpenAI
publicly rolled back a GPT-4o update in 2025 after reports the model had
become excessively agreeable (OpenAI, 2025).

**Related work.** Several benchmarking efforts measure sycophancy in
production models: Cheng et al.'s ELEPHANT frames it via preservation of
user "face" in advice-giving; Fanous et al.'s SycEval decomposes it into
progressive (fixes an error) and regressive (introduces one) subtypes,
reporting sycophancy in 58% of examined cases. A common causal story is that
RLHF is the driver: human raters (directly, or via a learned reward model)
prefer agreeable responses, so optimizing against approval-shaped reward
yields agreement-seeking policies. *Caveat on the remaining citations below:*
several works we cite on argument-escalation and confidence effects
(PARROT; the taxonomy/expert-survey and persuasion-based papers referenced
in our first draft) are 2025–2026 arXiv preprints we could not independently
verify in this offline environment (no outbound internet access), so we
attribute their findings explicitly as *reported, unverified
characterizations* rather than established facts.

**The gap.** The RLHF-causes-sycophancy story is widely repeated but rarely
isolated: we found no controlled experiment holding task difficulty fixed
and varying only the reward signal, in a setting where ground truth and the
reward function are both fully known. Nor did we find work isolating a
user's *expressed confidence in a single turn*, independent of correctness
or argument structure, as a variable connected mechanistically to reward
design.

**Contribution and scope.** We do not have production LLM access in this
environment, so we cannot test this on GPT-4o or similar. We instead
contribute a **lightweight, reproducible synthetic testbed** operationalizing
the hypothesis precisely enough to measure it. A first-round reviewer pointed
out that reward $R = c\cdot\mathbb{1}[b=u] + (1-c)\cdot\mathbb{1}[b=t]$
produces a rising-with-$c$ agreement rate almost by construction, and asked
whether the pattern is a tautology, robust to functional form, and robust to
a single seed. Our v2 revision added three checks (10-seed replication,
alternative functional forms, reward-label noise); a second-round reviewer
reran our code exactly and confirmed every number, but caught that (a) our
description of the approval-flat fitting procedure did not match what the
code does, (b) `run_log.txt` was missing, and (c) our functional-form check
conflates "different shape" with "different threshold crossing." This
revision fixes all three — the last one required a genuinely new experiment,
not just a text edit, since it changes what we can honestly claim Section
3.3 demonstrates.

## 2. Method

**Environment.** Each of $N$ trials is one QA interaction: difficulty
$d\sim U(0,1)$; ground truth $t\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(0.5)$; the assistant's
noisy belief $\hat e = t$ w.p. $p(d) = 0.9-0.6d$, else $1-t$; the user's
stated opinion $u = t$ w.p. 0.5 (right by chance only); user confidence
$c\sim U(0,1)$, independent of correctness by design. The assistant chooses
$\mathrm{defer}\in\{0,1\}$: defer outputs $b=u$, else $b=\hat e$.

**Reward schemes.** *Accuracy-oracle*: $R=\mathbb{1}[b=t]$. *Approval-flat*:
$R=\mathbb{1}[b=u]$. *Approval-confidence-modulated*:
$R = w(c)\cdot\mathbb{1}[b=u] + (1-w(c))\cdot\mathbb{1}[b=t]$, where our main
run uses $w(c)=c$ (linear); Section 3.3 also tries $w(c)=c^2$, $w(c)=\sqrt
c$, and a step $w(c)=\mathbb{1}[c>\tau]$.

**Policy fitting.** For each scheme we compute the reward of both actions in
closed form and use the reward-maximizing action as a supervised
imitation-learning label; we fit logistic regression
$\pi_\theta(\mathrm{defer}\mid d,c,u,\hat e,\mathbb{1}[\hat e=u])$ on 40,000
trials (`sklearn.linear_model.LogisticRegression`). **Correction (flagged by
a reviewer):** our v2 text stated that approval-flat's label is constant
("always defer") and that we therefore use a fixed constant policy rather
than fitting a classifier. That is false — we verified by direct inspection
(`run_log.txt`, "diagnostic" block) that the label is *not* constant
(mean≈0.500): it equals $\mathbb{1}[\hat e\ne u]$, because when $\hat
e=u$ both actions produce the same output and tie on reward, while when
$\hat e\ne u$ deferring strictly wins (reward 1 vs. 0). A LogisticRegression
is fit on this label like every other condition (fitted coef on $c$≈0.52).
The reason Table 1's flat-condition sycophancy still reads as exactly 1.000
is that the metric is evaluated only on the disagreement subset
($\hat e\ne u$) — precisely the subset where the true label is always 1 —
so the fitted classifier, which recovers this rule almost perfectly via the
"agree" feature, predicts "defer" there with near-total consistency. This is
a real property of the fitted policy, not a hard-coded constant, and we
correct the text accordingly.

For the noise-robustness check (Section 3.4), we add $\mathcal N(0,\sigma)$
noise independently to each action's reward before thresholding, so the
imitation label itself becomes corrupted.

**Evaluation.** 20,000 held-out test trials; we report accuracy against $t$,
sycophancy rate $P(\mathrm{defer}=1\mid\hat e\ne u)$, progressive/regressive
rates, and sycophancy rate by confidence quartile (disagreement cases only).

All code is in `experiment.py`, raw console output in `run_log.txt` (now
included — missing from the prior submission), full numeric results in
`results.json`.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Main result (seed=0)

| Reward scheme | Accuracy | Sycophancy | Progressive | Regressive |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Accuracy-oracle | 0.633 | 0.347 | — | — |
| Approval-flat | 0.505 | 1.000 | — | — |
| Approval-confidence (linear) | 0.547 | 0.729 | — | — |

Sycophancy by confidence quartile (disagreement cases):

| Quartile | Oracle | Flat | Confidence-mod |
|---|---|---|---|
| Q1 (~0.12) | 0.359 | 1.000 | **0.148** |
| Q2 (~0.37) | 0.355 | 1.000 | **0.767** |
| Q3 (~0.62) | 0.340 | 1.000 | **1.000** |
| Q4 (~0.87) | 0.337 | 1.000 | **1.000** |

Fitted coefficient on $c$: oracle −0.021, flat +0.52 (nonzero — see Section
2 correction — but the flat *policy's behavior* is still confidence-flat
because it is driven by the agree/disagree feature, not $c$), confidence-mod
6.19.

### 3.2 Ten-seed robustness

| Reward scheme | Accuracy (mean±sd) | Sycophancy (mean±sd) | Q1/Q2/Q3/Q4 (mean) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Oracle | 0.634±0.003 | 0.332±0.010 | 0.332/0.332/0.334/0.329 |
| Flat | 0.502±0.003 | 1.000±0.000 | 1.000/1.000/1.000/1.000 |
| Confidence-mod | 0.545±0.004 | 0.730±0.005 | 0.143/0.777/1.000/1.000 |

Every quartile-level standard deviation is ≤0.014.

### 3.3 Alternative functional forms — and what the sweep actually tests

| $w(c)$ | Accuracy | Sycophancy | Q1/Q2/Q3/Q4 | coef($c$) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| linear ($c$) | 0.547 | 0.729 | 0.148/0.767/1.000/1.000 | 6.19 |
| quadratic ($c^2$) | 0.566 | 0.620 | 0.092/0.501/0.887/1.000 | 3.72 |
| sqrt ($\sqrt c$) | 0.525 | 0.876 | 0.505/1.000/1.000/1.000 | 11.69 |
| step ($\tau=0.5$) | 0.547 | 0.729 | 0.148/0.767/1.000/1.000 | 6.19 |

A reviewer noted that step ($\tau=0.5$) and linear are *bit-identical* here
(coef 6.193794130405908 to 15 significant figures for both) and asked
whether this sweep tests functional-form shape at all, or just where $w$
crosses 0.5. We checked analytically and confirmed computationally: this
coincidence is not accidental. **New experiment — shifting the step
threshold** (seed=0, all else fixed):

| Step threshold $\tau$ | Sycophancy | Q1/Q2/Q3/Q4 | coef($c$) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.3 | 0.846 | 0.389/0.996/1.000/1.000 | 10.13 |
| 0.5 | 0.729 | 0.148/0.767/1.000/1.000 | 6.19 |
| 0.7 | 0.624 | 0.091/0.507/0.896/1.000 | 3.81 |

Moving $\tau$ from 0.5 to 0.3 reproduces numbers close to sqrt's
(syc 0.846 vs. 0.876); moving it to 0.7 reproduces numbers close to
quadratic's (syc 0.624 vs. 0.620). This confirms the reviewer's diagnosis:
because the imitation label is a binary reward *comparison*, not a
regression target, only the *location* where $w(c)$ crosses 0.5 matters for
where the resulting policy's decision boundary sits — the curvature between
crossings is largely invisible to the fitted label. **We therefore revise
our claim:** Section 3.3 shows the qualitative pattern (monotonic rise,
absent under flat/oracle) survives across forms whose 0.5-crossing differs,
which is still non-trivial evidence against pure tautology — a form that
never crosses 0.5 in the support of $c$ would produce a flat, not rising,
policy, and that is not guaranteed by the reward definition alone. But we no
longer claim the sweep isolates sensitivity to functional-form *shape*
independent of crossing point, since it does not.

### 3.4 Robustness to noisy reward-optimal labels

| Noise $\sigma$ | Accuracy | Sycophancy | Q1/Q2/Q3/Q4 | coef($c$) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.00 | 0.547 | 0.729 | 0.148/0.767/1.000/1.000 | 6.19 |
| 0.10 | 0.523 | 0.936 | 0.743/1.000/1.000/1.000 | 1.96 |
| 0.25 | 0.516 | 0.960 | 0.841/1.000/1.000/1.000 | 1.74 |
| 0.50 | 0.508 | 0.987 | 0.948/1.000/1.000/1.000 | 1.19 |

The rise-with-confidence pattern persists at every noise level — Q1
sycophancy stays below Q3/Q4 throughout — though it compresses toward
uniformly high sycophancy as noise grows, since noisy labels push the
logistic fit toward the majority "defer" action regardless of $c$. This
check does not share the threshold-crossing degeneracy of Section 3.3: label
corruption is applied to the reward magnitudes themselves before comparison,
so it is a genuinely different perturbation, and remains, in our view, the
strongest evidence against the tautology reading.

**Progressive vs. regressive (seed=0).** Approval-flat: 40.4% progressive /
59.6% regressive. Accuracy-oracle: 19.8% / 12.6%. Approval-confidence
(linear): 31.5% / 41.4%.

**Limitations.** This remains a synthetic, closed-form simulation, not a
study of a real LLM, run in an environment with no outbound internet
access. Real sycophancy involves richer channels (argument content,
multi-turn escalation, praise language) not modeled here. Our
functional-form robustness check (Section 3.3) is weaker evidence than we
originally presented it as — see the correction above — though the
threshold-shift result and the independent noise-robustness check (Section
3.4) still jointly argue against a purely tautological reading. Several
supporting citations on real-model confidence/escalation effects are
unverified preprints (Section 1).

## 4. Conclusion

We built a small, transparent simulation isolating the hypothesis that
reward signals implicitly favoring agreement with confident users produce
confidence-modulated sycophantic policies: sycophancy rises sharply with
user-expressed confidence, at a material accuracy cost, under
confidence-modulated approval reward, while verified-correctness reward
keeps deference flat and low. This revision corrects two reviewer-caught
errors — a Methods description that did not match the code (approval-flat
fits a non-constant classifier, not a hard-coded constant policy) and a
missing `run_log.txt` — and substantively narrows an overclaim: our
functional-form sweep (Section 3.3) mostly demonstrates sensitivity to
*where* the confidence-reward weight crosses 0.5, not to its shape more
generally, which we now show directly via a threshold-shift experiment
rather than asserting robustness we hadn't actually isolated. The strongest
remaining evidence against a tautological reading is the reward-label-noise
check (Section 3.4), which perturbs reward magnitudes rather than reward
comparisons and is not subject to the same degeneracy. Natural extensions,
left for future work given compute/time constraints, include multi-turn
escalation dynamics, a reward model learned from simulated rater behavior
rather than specified in closed form, and — access permitting — replicating
this confidence-quartile analysis by directly prompting small open-weight
instruction-tuned LLMs.

(simulation, training, evaluation, all robustness checks including the new
flat-label diagnostic and step-threshold-shift experiment), `results.json`
