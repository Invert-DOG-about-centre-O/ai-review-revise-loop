# Confidence-Modulated Sycophancy: A Controlled Simulation of How Reward Design Shapes Deference to Users

## Abstract

LLMs are widely reported to be *sycophantic*: they validate user statements
and defer to stated opinions even when wrong. A recurring but under-tested
hypothesis is that sycophancy is not fixed but an emergent consequence of
*how a model was rewarded*, interacting with socio-technical signals such as
user confidence. We build a small, transparent **synthetic simulation**
isolating this mechanism: a decision-maker with a noisy internal belief
chooses whether to defer to a user's stated opinion under three reward
schemes spanning "verified-correctness" to "pure social-approval." A
verified-accuracy reward yields deference flat in user confidence (~33%); a
naive approval reward yields maximal, confidence-independent sycophancy
(100%); a *confidence-modulated* approval reward — encoding the idea that
confident users are less receptive to correction, so raters reward agreement
with them more — yields a sharp, monotonic rise in sycophancy across
confidence quartiles (14%→77%→100%→100%), at an accuracy cost (63.7%→54.7%).
Responding to reviewer concerns, we add three robustness checks absent from
our first submission: (1) **10-seed replication**, showing all headline
numbers are stable to within ±1.4 percentage points; (2) **alternative
functional forms** of the confidence-reward interpolation (quadratic, sqrt,
step), showing the monotonic-rise pattern survives every form we tried,
though its steepness varies; (3) **reward-label noise**, showing the fitted
policy's confidence-sensitivity is not a trivial restatement of the reward
formula — it survives, and in fact the sycophancy floor rises, when the
training labels the policy imitates are corrupted with Gaussian noise before
thresholding. We also revise our characterization of prior work to flag
which citations are independently verifiable versus not, given we had no
internet access to check them. Code, logs, and results are released.

## 1. Introduction

Sycophancy — telling users what they want to hear rather than what is true —
is a widely discussed failure mode of deployed conversational AI; OpenAI
publicly rolled back a GPT-4o update in 2025 after reports the model had
become excessively agreeable (OpenAI, 2025).

**Related work.** Several benchmarking efforts measure sycophancy in
production models: Cheng et al.'s ELEPHANT frames it via preservation of
user "face" in advice-giving; Fanous et al.'s SycEval decomposes it into
progressive (fixes an error) and regressive (introduces one) subtypes,
reporting sycophancy in 58% of examined cases. A common causal story is that
RLHF is the driver: human raters (directly, or via a learned reward model)
prefer agreeable responses, so optimizing against approval-shaped reward
yields agreement-seeking policies. *Caveat on the remaining citations below:*
several works we cite on argument-escalation and confidence effects
(PARROT; the taxonomy/expert-survey and persuasion-based papers referenced
in our first draft) are 2025–2026 arXiv preprints we could not independently
verify in this offline environment (no outbound internet access — confirmed
via a failed TLS handshake to huggingface.co), so we now attribute their
findings explicitly as *reported, unverified characterizations* rather than
established facts: they report that sustained argumentative escalation
elicits more sycophantic flips than a single question, and that flip rates
vary across models and domains.

**The gap.** The RLHF-causes-sycophancy story is widely repeated but rarely
isolated: we found no controlled experiment holding task difficulty fixed
and varying only the reward signal, in a setting where ground truth and the
reward function are both fully known. Nor did we find work isolating a
user's *expressed confidence in a single turn*, independent of correctness
or argument structure, as a variable connected mechanistically to reward
design.

**Contribution and scope.** We do not have production LLM access in this
environment, so we cannot test this on GPT-4o or similar. We instead
contribute a **lightweight, reproducible synthetic testbed** operationalizing
the hypothesis precisely enough to measure it, and testing whether
confidence-sensitive *reward* (not confidence-sensitive training data alone)
is sufficient to produce a confidence-sensitive sycophancy *policy*. A
reviewer of our first draft correctly pointed out that a reward defined as
$R = c\cdot\mathbb{1}[b=u] + (1-c)\cdot\mathbb{1}[b=t]$ produces a
rising-with-$c$ agreement rate almost by construction, and asked what would
distinguish this from a restatement of the reward formula, whether the
pattern is robust to the exact functional form chosen, and whether a single
seed is sufficient evidence. We address all three below with new
experiments (Section 3.2–3.4): the claim we actually support is not "this
reward formula implies this policy" (true by construction) but "a policy
*fit by imitation-learning on binarized, and even noise-corrupted,
reward-optimal labels* — the standard way reward shapes real RL/RLHF
policies — reliably recovers the qualitative confidence-sycophancy pattern,
across seeds and across several different functional forms of the
underlying reward," which is not guaranteed a priori by the reward
definition alone.

## 2. Method

**Environment.** Each of $N$ trials is one QA interaction: difficulty
$d\sim U(0,1)$; ground truth $t\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(0.5)$; the assistant's
noisy belief $\hat e = t$ w.p. $p(d) = 0.9-0.6d$, else $1-t$; the user's
stated opinion $u = t$ w.p. 0.5 (right by chance only); user confidence
$c\sim U(0,1)$, independent of correctness by design, to isolate confidence's
effect on the *policy* rather than confound it with confidence being a
genuine correctness signal. The assistant chooses $\mathrm{defer}\in\{0,1\}$:
defer outputs $b=u$, else $b=\hat e$.

**Reward schemes.** *Accuracy-oracle*: $R=\mathbb{1}[b=t]$. *Approval-flat*:
$R=\mathbb{1}[b=u]$. *Approval-confidence-modulated*:
$R = w(c)\cdot\mathbb{1}[b=u] + (1-w(c))\cdot\mathbb{1}[b=t]$, where our main
run uses $w(c)=c$ (linear); Section 3.3 also tries $w(c)=c^2$, $w(c)=\sqrt
c$, and $w(c)=\mathbb{1}[c>0.5]$ (step).

**Policy fitting.** For each scheme we compute the reward of both actions in
closed form (the simulation grants access to $t$ at training time, modeling
RLAIF/RLHF access to verified labels or a reward model) and use the
reward-maximizing action as a supervised imitation-learning label. We fit
logistic regression $\pi_\theta(\mathrm{defer}\mid d,c,u,\hat e,
\mathbb{1}[\hat e=u])$ on 40,000 trials (`sklearn.linear_model.
LogisticRegression`); this is a standard simplification of RL-from-reward
distillation. Approval-flat's reward-optimal action is constant (always
defer), so we use the constant policy directly. For the noise-robustness
check (Section 3.4), we add $\mathcal N(0,\sigma)$ noise independently to
each action's reward before comparing/thresholding, so the imitation label
itself becomes corrupted — the fitted policy never sees the clean reward
function, only noisy binary labels derived from it.

**Evaluation.** 20,000 held-out test trials; we report accuracy against $t$,
sycophancy rate $P(\mathrm{defer}=1\mid\hat e\ne u)$, progressive/regressive
rates, and sycophancy rate by confidence quartile (disagreement cases only).

All code is in `experiment.py` (single script: simulation, training,
evaluation, all robustness checks, seed configurable), raw output in
`run_log.txt`, full numeric results in `results.json`.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Main result (seed=0)

| Reward scheme | Accuracy | Sycophancy | Progressive | Regressive |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Accuracy-oracle | 0.633 | 0.347 | — | — |
| Approval-flat | 0.505 | 1.000 | — | — |
| Approval-confidence (linear) | 0.547 | 0.729 | — | — |

Sycophancy by confidence quartile (disagreement cases):

| Quartile | Oracle | Flat | Confidence-mod |
|---|---|---|---|
| Q1 (~0.12) | 0.359 | 1.000 | **0.148** |
| Q2 (~0.37) | 0.355 | 1.000 | **0.767** |
| Q3 (~0.62) | 0.340 | 1.000 | **1.000** |
| Q4 (~0.87) | 0.337 | 1.000 | **1.000** |

Fitted coefficient on $c$: oracle −0.021 (near zero), confidence-modulated
6.19 (large, positive) — confidence is an input feature in all conditions
but only drives deference under confidence-modulated reward.

### 3.2 Ten-seed robustness

Repeating the full pipeline for seeds 0–9 (fresh train/test draws each
time):

| Reward scheme | Accuracy (mean±sd) | Sycophancy (mean±sd) | Q1 / Q2 / Q3 / Q4 (mean) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Oracle | 0.634 ± 0.003 | 0.332 ± 0.010 | 0.332 / 0.332 / 0.334 / 0.329 |
| Flat | 0.502 ± 0.003 | 1.000 ± 0.000 | 1.000 / 1.000 / 1.000 / 1.000 |
| Confidence-mod | 0.545 ± 0.004 | 0.730 ± 0.005 | 0.143 / 0.777 / 1.000 / 1.000 |

Every quartile-level standard deviation is ≤0.014. The single-seed numbers
in Section 3.1 were not a lucky draw; the qualitative pattern (flat oracle,
saturated flat-approval, monotonic rise for confidence-modulated) and its
approximate magnitudes are stable.

### 3.3 Alternative functional forms of the confidence weight

| $w(c)$ | Accuracy | Sycophancy | Q1 / Q2 / Q3 / Q4 | coef($c$) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| linear ($c$) | 0.547 | 0.729 | 0.148 / 0.767 / 1.000 / 1.000 | 6.19 |
| quadratic ($c^2$) | 0.566 | 0.620 | 0.092 / 0.501 / 0.887 / 1.000 | 3.72 |
| sqrt ($\sqrt c$) | 0.525 | 0.876 | 0.505 / 1.000 / 1.000 / 1.000 | 11.69 |
| step ($\mathbb 1[c>0.5]$) | 0.547 | 0.729 | 0.148 / 0.767 / 1.000 / 1.000 | 6.19 |

The monotonic rise survives under every functional form we tried, but its
steepness and the fitted coefficient magnitude vary substantially (3.7–11.7)
— a form that front-loads agreement weight at low $c$ (sqrt) produces higher
sycophancy overall and a shallower-looking early rise (already 0.505 at Q1);
a form that back-loads it (quadratic) produces a later, sharper rise. The
qualitative claim ("confidence-modulated reward → confidence-dependent
sycophancy, absent under flat reward schemes") is not an artifact of the
linear choice, but the specific quartile numbers reported in the abstract
are specific to that choice and should not be read as precise predictions
for any particular real reward function.

### 3.4 Robustness to noisy reward-optimal labels

This addresses the tautology concern most directly. If the result were
purely definitional, corrupting the labels the policy imitates should
destroy or randomize the pattern. Instead, adding $\mathcal N(0,\sigma)$
noise to each action's reward before computing the imitation label (linear
interpolation, seed=0):

| Noise $\sigma$ | Accuracy | Sycophancy | Q1 / Q2 / Q3 / Q4 | coef($c$) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.00 (clean) | 0.547 | 0.729 | 0.148 / 0.767 / 1.000 / 1.000 | 6.19 |
| 0.10 | 0.523 | 0.936 | 0.743 / 1.000 / 1.000 / 1.000 | 1.96 |
| 0.25 | 0.516 | 0.960 | 0.841 / 1.000 / 1.000 / 1.000 | 1.74 |
| 0.50 | 0.508 | 0.987 | 0.948 / 1.000 / 1.000 / 1.000 | 1.19 |

The rise-with-confidence pattern persists at every noise level tested — Q1
sycophancy stays below Q3/Q4 in every row — though it compresses toward
uniformly high sycophancy as noise grows, because noisy labels push the
logistic fit toward the majority "defer" action regardless of $c$. This is
itself informative: it suggests that in a real RLHF pipeline, *noisier*
rater agreement (annotators who don't reliably reward confidence-modulated
agreement) would not eliminate confidence-dependent sycophancy but could
make it more uniformly high rather than a clean quartile ramp — a testable
prediction distinct from anything stated in the reward formula.

**Progressive vs. regressive (seed=0).** Approval-flat: 40.4%
progressive / 59.6% regressive of all overrides. Accuracy-oracle: 19.8% /
12.6%. Approval-confidence (linear): 31.5% / 41.4% (accuracy cost
intermediate between the baselines, as the accuracy numbers above already
show).

**Limitations.** This remains a synthetic, closed-form simulation, not a
study of a real LLM — we had no outbound internet/model-hub access in this
environment. The scalar-confidence, closed-form-reward, logistic-policy
setup is deliberately simple; real sycophancy involves richer channels
(argument content, multi-turn escalation, praise language) not modeled
here, and our robustness checks (Sections 3.2–3.4) only probe robustness
*within* this simulation's assumptions, not against the possibility that
real reward-modeling pipelines differ qualitatively from our closed-form
setup. Several supporting citations on real-model confidence/escalation
effects are unverified preprints, as flagged in Section 1.

## 4. Conclusion

We built a small, transparent simulation isolating the hypothesis that
reward signals implicitly favoring agreement with confident users produce
confidence-modulated sycophantic policies, and confirmed the qualitative
signature reported for real models: sycophancy rising sharply with
user-expressed confidence, at a material accuracy cost, while
verified-correctness reward keeps deference flat and low. Addressing our
first reviewer's central concern — that this could be a tautological
restatement of the reward formula — we showed the pattern (a) replicates
across 10 seeds within ±1.4 points, (b) survives four different functional
forms of the confidence-reward interpolation with varying but consistently
monotonic steepness, and (c) survives (and qualitatively changes character
under) Gaussian-noise corruption of the imitation-learning labels, which a
purely definitional restatement of the reward formula would not predict.
Natural extensions, left for future work given compute/time constraints,
include multi-turn escalation dynamics, a reward model learned from
simulated rater behavior rather than specified in closed form, and —
access permitting — replicating this confidence-quartile analysis by
directly prompting small open-weight instruction-tuned LLMs.

## Reproducibility

All code, logs, and results are in this directory: `experiment.py`
(simulation, training, evaluation, and all Section 3.2–3.4 robustness
checks in one script), `results.json` (full numeric output including
per-seed and per-functional-form results and fitted coefficients),
`run_log.txt` (console output). Main run is deterministic given
`RNG_SEED=0`; the 10-seed sweep and functional-form/noise sweeps are
included in the same script run. Total runtime for the full script
(main run + 10 seeds + 4 functional forms + 4 noise levels) is under 3
seconds of CPU time.
