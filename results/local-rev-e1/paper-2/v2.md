# Personalization Drives Most of the Polarization in Human–AI Recommendation Loops — But Retraining Cadence Matters Once the Recommender Is Misspecified: An Agent-Based Study

## Abstract

Recommender systems are canonical socio-technical systems: trained on human behavior, they shape the content humans see, and are then retrained on the behavior their own recommendations produced. A common hypothesis is that this closed loop of *retraining on self-generated engagement* drives opinion polarization and shrinking content diversity. We test this with a lightweight agent-based simulation (300 agents, scalar opinions, logistic-regression recommender) comparing random, static (train-once), adaptive (periodically retrained), and adaptive-with-diversity-injection conditions. With a well-specified recommender (features that can represent the ground-truth engagement rule), static and adaptive personalization roughly triple final opinion variance relative to random (0.070 vs. 0.023) and cut content diversity ~22%; static and adaptive differ only slightly (0.0699 vs. 0.0686), though a paired test across matched seeds shows this small gap is in fact statistically reliable (t(4)=6.43, p=0.003), not indistinguishable noise as we previously reported. A retrain-frequency sweep (1–40 rounds) changes variance by <2%, and this null result persists even under an added concept-drift condition (a slowly shifting content-preference target), contrary to our own prior conjecture. Diversity injection sharply reduces polarization once it crosses a discrete per-user threshold (2 of 5 slots). Critically, we also test a **misspecified** recommender (linear-only features that cannot represent the true opinion–distance similarity rule): here retraining cadence stops being negligible and becomes the dominant factor — static variance collapses to 0.004 while adaptive variance explodes to 0.289 (paired t(2)=−25.0, p=0.0016), and the effect is non-monotonic across retrain frequencies. This shows our original headline claim — "retraining cadence barely matters" — holds only when the recommender already has the right features, and is not a general property of retraining loops.

## 1. Introduction

The socio-technical framing of AI recognizes that algorithmic systems and the humans who use them co-evolve: a model shapes user behavior, that behavior becomes training data, and the retrained model shapes behavior again (Cinus et al., 2022; survey by Wang et al., 2024). This human–AI feedback loop is widely cited as a driver of echo chambers, polarization, and declining collective content diversity, and "explicitly accounting for feedback loops remains a critical and largely open field" for empirical and simulation-based methods (recent simulation-framework literature, 2025).

Much of the existing simulation literature conflates two mechanisms: (1) *personalization* — recommending content similar to revealed preferences, which alone can induce confirmation-biased engagement even from a never-updated model; and (2) *retraining* — the recommender continuing to learn from engagement its own past recommendations produced. Prior agent-based work (Cinus et al., 2022) shows personalization increases echo chambers under network homophily; surveys (Wang et al., 2024) document individual-vs-collective diversity tension. These studies compare "personalized" against "not," or vary network structure, rather than isolating retraining cadence, and none of them ask whether the recommender's own capacity to represent the engagement rule changes the answer.

**Research question.** How much of the polarization attributed to "feedback loops" is due to (a) personalization existing at all, (b) retraining cadence, (c) diversity injection, and — new to this revision — (d) whether the retraining-cadence conclusion is robust to the recommender's feature capacity and to genuine concept drift?

**Contributions.** (1) A small, reproducible agent-based simulation cleanly separating personalization from retraining cadence. (2) A paired-seed statistical analysis (not just means ± std) showing the static-vs-adaptive gap, while small, is statistically real. (3) A concept-drift condition testing our own mechanistic conjecture directly, rather than leaving it as a claim. (4) A recommender-misspecification experiment showing the retraining-cadence-null-result is *not* general: it depends on the recommender being able to recover the ground-truth rule from a short cold start. (5) Open code and logs.

## 2. Related Work

**Feedback loops in recommenders.** Cinus et al. (2022, *arXiv:2112.00626*) show recommenders amplify echo chambers primarily when the network already has homophily, saturating once chambers exist. Our setup has no network structure, isolating the content-recommendation channel.

**Surveys of recommender impact.** Wang et al. (2024, *arXiv:2407.01630*) survey evidence that recommenders raise individual choice diversity while lowering collective diversity — a tension we also measure via content-diversity entropy.

**Simulation frameworks for systemic feedback effects.** A 2025 framework (*arXiv:2510.14857*) models the retrain-on-own-logs loop but does not isolate cadence or recommender capacity as variables; ours is complementary and narrower.

**LLM-based social/user simulation.** LLM user-simulator work documents sycophancy artifacts (*arXiv:2505.13995*, *arXiv:2505.23840*). We did not pursue this empirically — it requires hosted-API access unavailable in this compute-constrained, offline-friendly environment — and flag it as follow-up work.

**Positioning.** We keep the population/content model minimal so that retraining cadence, diversity injection, recommender capacity, and drift can each be swept as isolated variables — the last two of which, per round-1 review, prior work (and our own v1) left untested.

## 3. Method

**Population and content.** N = 300 agents hold a scalar opinion in [-1, 1], initialized from truncated Normal(0, 0.3). Each round, C = 500 candidate items are drawn uniformly from [-1, 1].

**Engagement model (ground truth).** Engagement probability is sigmoid(β₀ + β₁·(1 − |opinion − item position|)), β₀ = −1.0, β₁ = 4.0. On engagement, opinion moves 8% toward the item's position, plus noise (σ = 0.01/round).

**Recommender (well-specified).** Logistic regression on features [opinion, item, product, |opinion − item|] — expressive enough to represent the ground-truth rule exactly.

**Recommender (misspecified, new).** Logistic regression on features [opinion, item] only — cannot represent the nonlinear |opinion − item| similarity rule, mimicking a real-world recommender with an incomplete feature set.

**Conditions:** random, static (fit once after a 3-round cold start, frozen), adaptive (refit every `RETRAIN_EVERY` rounds on all accumulated logs), adaptive_diverse (adaptive + a fraction `DIVERSITY_EPS` of slots replaced with random candidates). 5 seeds for the main comparison, 3 seeds for ablations, T = 40 rounds, K = 5 recs/agent/round.

**New: concept-drift condition.** The ground-truth similarity target shifts over time: engagement probability uses |opinion − (item + 0.01·round)|, i.e., the "ideal" content position drifts by up to 0.4 over the 40-round run — a stylized evolving-taxonomy/population scenario. We compare static, adaptive (retrain every round), and adaptive (retrain every 40 rounds) under this drift.

**New: paired significance testing.** For all matched-seed comparisons (static vs. adaptive; retrain frequencies; diversity levels; misspecified static vs. adaptive) we report paired t-tests across seeds rather than only mean ± std, since conditions share seeds and cold-start logs.

**Metrics.** Final opinion variance; Sarle's bimodality coefficient; fraction |opinion| > 0.6; content-diversity entropy of engaged-item histogram.

**Implementation.** Pure NumPy + scikit-learn; `simulate.py`/`ablation.py` reproduce the v1 results, `revision_experiments.py` adds the paired tests, drift condition, and misspecified recommender (with an additional retrain-frequency sweep under misspecification). Total wall-clock for all four scripts: ~23 seconds on one CPU core.

## 4. Results

**Main comparison (mean ± std, 5 seeds, well-specified recommender):**

| Condition | Opinion Var. | Bimodality | Frac. Extreme | Content Diversity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| random | 0.0233 ± 0.0015 | 0.178 ± 0.004 | 0.000 | 2.258 ± 0.001 |
| static | 0.0699 ± 0.0054 | 0.172 ± 0.004 | 0.025 | 1.751 ± 0.034 |
| adaptive | 0.0686 ± 0.0054 | 0.172 ± 0.005 | 0.023 | 1.748 ± 0.033 |
| adaptive_diverse | 0.0243 ± 0.0010 | 0.172 ± 0.003 | 0.000 | 1.897 ± 0.011 |

Personalization triples variance vs. random and cuts diversity ~22%. **Revision to v1:** we previously called static-vs-adaptive "statistically indistinguishable" from mean ± std alone. A paired t-test across the 5 matched seeds shows the gap (0.0699 vs. 0.0686) is small but real (t(4) = 6.43, p = 0.003 for variance; t(4) = 5.13, p = 0.007 for diversity). Retraining does have a small, consistent negative effect on polarization here — the practical conclusion (retraining cadence is a low-leverage lever compared to personalization itself) is unchanged, since the effect is two orders of magnitude smaller than the personalization-vs-random gap (paired t(4) = 17.5, p < 0.001), but we no longer describe it as noise.

**Retrain-frequency ablation** (3 seeds, well-specified recommender): variance ranges from 0.0684 to 0.0698 across `RETRAIN_EVERY` ∈ {1,...,40} — a ≤2% spread, an order of magnitude below the personalization-vs-random gap.

**Concept-drift experiment (new).** Under a slowly drifting content-preference target, static, adaptive-retrain-every-round, and adaptive-retrain-every-40-rounds are still statistically indistinguishable (0.0692, 0.0688, 0.0692; paired t(2) = −0.88, p = 0.47). This directly tests our v1 conjecture that "retraining cadence should matter more under concept drift" — **it does not, at least at this drift rate**, because the adaptive recommender refits on *all* accumulated history rather than a recency-weighted or sliding window, so frequent refitting does not actually track the drifting target any better than infrequent refitting; both lag equally. This is itself an informative negative result: naive full-history retraining does not confer a drift-tracking advantage regardless of frequency, so "retrain more often" is not by itself a fix for drift in this architecture.

**Misspecified-recommender experiment (new) — the central new finding.** When the recommender can only see [opinion, item] (not the interaction/distance features needed to represent the true similarity rule), the picture inverts. Static variance is 0.0038 ± 0.0026 — *below* even the random baseline — because a linear-only model fit on a short, noisy cold-start log cannot recover the true nonlinear rule and ends up recommending close to randomly. Adaptive variance is 0.2895 ± 0.0179, over an order of magnitude higher (paired t(2) = −25.0, p = 0.0016): repeated retraining on the recommender's own increasingly biased logs lets it bootstrap a much sharper decision boundary than any single fit could, and the loop compounds. A retrain-frequency sweep under misspecification (3 seeds) shows the effect is large but **non-monotonic**: variance is 0.065 at `RETRAIN_EVERY`=1, jumps to 0.32–0.33 at 2–10, and collapses to 0.004 at 40 (≈static). So cadence is not simply "more retraining = more harm" here; intermediate frequencies are worst, plausibly because they retrain often enough to lock in early, noisy correlations before enough corrective data accumulates, while retraining every round incorporates corrective signal faster and retraining once never bootstraps the spurious signal at all.

**Diversity-injection ablation** (3 seeds): threshold-like — ε=0.0/0.1 (0 slots replaced with K=5) are identical (var 0.0686); ε=0.2 (1 slot) roughly halves variance to 0.0345; ε≥0.3 (2+ slots) drops to ≈0.022–0.024, matching random. This is an artifact of the small K=5 slate discretizing the intervention.

**Interpretation.** Our v1 claim that retraining cadence is a low-leverage lever holds *only when the recommender already has the features to recover the ground-truth engagement rule from a short cold start* — precisely the concern the review raised. When the recommender is instead misspecified (a more realistic condition, since real engagement-prediction models are never perfectly specified), retraining cadence becomes the dominant and non-monotonic driver of polarization, because the loop lets the model bootstrap spurious structure from its own increasingly filtered logs. Concept drift, at least of the mild, full-history-retraining kind we tested, does not revive the cadence effect for well-specified recommenders.

## 5. Conclusion

Using a compact, reproducible agent-based simulation, we find that whether "retraining cadence" is a meaningful policy lever depends critically on recommender specification. With a well-specified recommender, personalization alone (static or adaptive) accounts for nearly all measured polarization and diversity loss relative to random recommendations, and retraining cadence has a small but statistically real effect (contra our earlier "indistinguishable" framing) that remains two orders of magnitude smaller than the personalization effect — and this null result is robust to the mild concept drift we tested. But with a misspecified, lower-capacity recommender — arguably the more realistic case — retraining cadence becomes the dominant and non-monotonic driver of polarization, with adaptive retraining producing ~75x the variance of a single static fit. Diversity injection remains a highly effective, threshold-sensitive lever (2 of 5 slots randomized restores near-baseline variance) regardless of these caveats. For practitioners, the takeaway is now more conditional: if your recommender's features can already capture the relevant similarity structure, retraining frequency is a low-leverage target and diversity injection should be prioritized; if your recommender is under-featured (the common real case), retraining cadence and log-curation choices need explicit scrutiny, since the loop can bootstrap large, non-monotonic swings in polarization that a single frozen fit would not produce. This remains a single, minimal simulation (scalar opinions, one drift specification, one form of misspecification, no social-network structure); we flag the sycophancy-simulation extension (Section 2) and a recency-weighted/sliding-window retraining variant as natural next steps, since our current drift result may be an artifact of full-history retraining rather than evidence that drift never matters.
