# Personalization Drives Most of the Polarization in Human–AI Recommendation Loops — But Retraining Cadence and Feature Specification Interact in Non-Obvious Ways: An Agent-Based Study

## Abstract

Recommender systems are canonical socio-technical systems: trained on human behavior, they shape the content humans see, and are then retrained on the behavior their own recommendations produced. A common hypothesis is that this closed loop of *retraining on self-generated engagement* drives opinion polarization and shrinking content diversity. We test this with a lightweight agent-based simulation (300 agents, scalar opinions, logistic-regression recommender) comparing random, static (train-once), adaptive (periodically retrained), and adaptive-with-diversity-injection conditions. With a well-specified recommender, static and adaptive personalization roughly triple final opinion variance relative to random (0.070 vs. 0.023) and cut content diversity ~22%; the static-vs-adaptive gap (0.0699 vs. 0.0686) is small but statistically real (paired t(4)=6.43, p=0.003, d=2.88, n=5 seeds). This null result is robust: a retrain-frequency sweep (1–40 rounds) changes variance by <2%, it survives concept drift tested against a schedule that genuinely fires mid-run (retrain-every-20; t(2)=−0.12, p=0.92), and — new in this revision — it survives a 4-point assimilation-rate sweep (α∈{0.04,...,0.32}) and a 4-point noise sweep (σ∈{0,...,0.06}): the static-vs-adaptive gap stays small (≤0.006 absolute) at every setting we tested, even as the overall variance level itself shifts substantially with both parameters. Diversity injection sharply reduces polarization once it crosses a discrete per-user threshold (2 of 5 slots). Recommender misspecification breaks the null result asymmetrically: with a fully linear feature set, static variance collapses to 0.004 while adaptive variance explodes to 0.289 (d=−14.4, bootstrap 95% CI on the seed-level gap [−0.309, −0.272]); with only the distance feature dropped (keeping the raw opinion-item product), polarization is catastrophic (var≈0.95) but cadence-*insensitive* (static≈adaptive). New in this revision, a coefficient-level diagnostic explains the asymmetry mechanistically: the product-term-only model's decision surface already recommends items at the extreme matching the user's opinion sign after a *single* fit (argmax item = sign(opinion)×1.0, even at small opinions), so the catastrophe is baked in before any retraining occurs; the linear-only model's fitted engagement probabilities are nearly flat across the entire item range (0.56–0.57, barely above chance), so a single fit is close to random and only repeated retraining on the model's own increasingly biased logs sharpens a real decision boundary — explaining why that regime, and only that regime, is cadence-dependent.

## 1. Introduction

The socio-technical framing of AI recognizes that algorithmic systems and the humans who use them co-evolve: a model shapes user behavior, that behavior becomes training data, and the retrained model shapes behavior again (Cinus et al., 2022; survey by Wang et al., 2024). This human–AI feedback loop is widely cited as a driver of echo chambers, polarization, and declining collective content diversity.

Much of the existing simulation literature conflates two mechanisms: (1) *personalization* — recommending content similar to revealed preferences, which alone can induce confirmation-biased engagement even from a never-updated model; and (2) *retraining* — the recommender continuing to learn from engagement its own past recommendations produced. Prior work (Cinus et al., 2022) shows personalization increases echo chambers under network homophily; surveys (Wang et al., 2024) document individual-vs-collective diversity tension. None of these ask whether the recommender's own capacity to represent the engagement rule changes the answer, which specific missing feature matters, or whether the personalization-dominates-retraining finding is an artifact of one choice of opinion-dynamics parameters.

**Research question.** How much of the polarization attributed to "feedback loops" is due to (a) personalization existing at all, (b) retraining cadence, (c) diversity injection, and (d) the recommender's feature capacity — and how robust is the answer to the opinion-assimilation and noise parameters governing individual belief updates?

**Contributions.** (1) A small, reproducible agent-based simulation cleanly separating personalization from retraining cadence. (2) Paired-seed statistical tests (t-tests, Cohen's d, and — new in this revision — bootstrap confidence intervals for the low-n comparisons) for all matched-seed comparisons. (3) A concept-drift condition using a retraining schedule that actually fires mid-run. (4) A misspecified-recommender experiment with a partial-misspecification sweep, extended in this revision with a coefficient-level diagnostic explaining *why* the two misspecification regimes differ mechanistically. (5) New in this revision: an assimilation-rate and noise-parameter sensitivity sweep, closing a gap flagged across three review rounds. (6) Open code and logs.

## 2. Related Work

**Feedback loops in recommenders.** Cinus et al. (2022, *arXiv:2112.00626*) show recommenders amplify echo chambers primarily when the network already has homophily; our setup has no network structure, isolating the content-recommendation channel. **Surveys.** Wang et al. (2024, *arXiv:2407.01630*) document individual-vs-collective diversity tension, which we measure via content-diversity entropy. **Simulation frameworks.** A 2025 framework (*arXiv:2510.14857*) models the retrain-on-own-logs loop but does not isolate cadence, recommender capacity, or opinion-dynamics sensitivity; ours is complementary and narrower. **LLM-based social simulation.** LLM user-simulator work documents sycophancy artifacts (*arXiv:2505.13995*, *arXiv:2505.23840*); we did not pursue this empirically (no hosted-API access in this offline environment) and flag it as follow-up. None of this prior work asks whether feature capacity, *which* feature is missing, or the opinion-update parameters change conclusions about retraining cadence; we keep the model minimal to isolate exactly that.

## 3. Method

**Population and content.** N = 300 agents hold a scalar opinion in [-1, 1], initialized from truncated Normal(0, 0.3). Each round, C = 500 candidate items are drawn uniformly from [-1, 1].

**Engagement model (ground truth).** Engagement probability is sigmoid(β₀ + β₁·(1 − |opinion − item position|)), β₀ = −1.0, β₁ = 4.0. On engagement, opinion moves a fraction α (assimilation rate, default 0.08) toward the item's position, plus per-round Gaussian noise (σ, default 0.01).

**Recommender feature sets.** Logistic regression on: `full` = [opinion, item, product, |opinion − item|] (well-specified); `no_dist` = [opinion, item, product] (missing the distance term); `no_product` = [opinion, item, |opinion − item|] (missing the product term); `linear_only` = [opinion, item] (fully misspecified).

**Conditions:** random, static (fit once after a 3-round cold start, frozen), adaptive (refit every `RETRAIN_EVERY` rounds on all accumulated logs), adaptive_diverse (adaptive + a fraction `DIVERSITY_EPS` of slots replaced with random candidates). 5 seeds for the main comparison, 3 seeds for ablations, T = 40 rounds, K = 5 recs/agent/round.

**Concept-drift condition.** Engagement probability uses |opinion − (item + 0.01·round)|, drifting the "ideal" content position by up to 0.4 over the run. We compare static, adaptive-retrain-every-1, and adaptive-retrain-every-20 (which fires at round 19 and is used for rounds 20–39, unlike a since-corrected retrain-every-40 comparison that never fired mid-run).

**Assimilation-rate / noise sensitivity sweep (new).** To address a gap flagged across three review rounds, we re-run the static-vs-adaptive comparison (well-specified recommender, 3 seeds) at α ∈ {0.04, 0.08, 0.16, 0.32} (holding σ=0.01) and at σ ∈ {0.0, 0.01, 0.03, 0.06} (holding α=0.08).

**Bootstrap CIs (new).** For the n=3 misspecification, drift, and partial-misspecification comparisons, we additionally report 95% CIs from 20,000-resample seed-level bootstrap of the paired difference, as a cheap complement to the paired t-test that does not require more simulation runs.

**Coefficient diagnostic (new).** To explain the no_dist-vs-linear_only asymmetry mechanistically, we fit each of the four feature sets once on a shared, large (n=20,000), *unbiased* random-exposure log (opinions and items drawn uniformly, labeled by the true engagement rule) and inspect the fitted coefficients and the argmax-scoring item on a fixed opinion grid — isolating what each feature set can represent, independent of any bootstrapping dynamics from the simulation loop itself.

**Paired significance testing.** For all matched-seed comparisons we report paired t-tests and Cohen's d.

**Metrics.** Final opinion variance; Sarle's bimodality coefficient; fraction |opinion| > 0.6; content-diversity entropy of engaged-item histogram.

**Implementation.** Pure NumPy + scikit-learn; `simulate.py`/`ablation.py` reproduce the base results, `revision_experiments.py` adds paired tests, the drift/misspecification experiments, and the partial-misspecification sweep, `revision4_experiments.py` adds the sensitivity sweep, bootstrap CIs, and coefficient diagnostic. Total wall-clock for all experiments: ~46 seconds on one CPU core.

## 4. Results

**Main comparison (mean ± std, 5 seeds, well-specified recommender):**

| Condition | Opinion Var. | Bimodality | Frac. Extreme | Content Diversity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| random | 0.0233 ± 0.0015 | 0.178 ± 0.004 | 0.000 | 2.258 ± 0.001 |
| static | 0.0699 ± 0.0054 | 0.172 ± 0.004 | 0.025 | 1.751 ± 0.034 |
| adaptive | 0.0686 ± 0.0054 | 0.172 ± 0.005 | 0.023 | 1.748 ± 0.033 |
| adaptive_diverse | 0.0243 ± 0.0010 | 0.172 ± 0.003 | 0.000 | 1.897 ± 0.011 |

Personalization triples variance vs. random and cuts diversity ~22% (paired t(4)=17.5, p<0.001, d=7.84). The static-vs-adaptive gap is small but real (t(4)=6.43, p=0.003, d=2.88) — retraining cadence remains a low-leverage lever *given a well-specified recommender*, two orders of magnitude smaller than the personalization effect.

**Assimilation-rate / noise sensitivity sweep (new, 3 seeds each).** The static-vs-adaptive gap is robust across every parameter setting tested, even though the overall variance level shifts substantially:

| Parameter | Static var. | Adaptive var. | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| α=0.04 | 0.0747 | 0.0741 | 0.0006 |
| α=0.08 (default) | 0.0698 | 0.0686 | 0.0012 |
| α=0.16 | 0.0769 | 0.0738 | 0.0031 |
| α=0.32 | 0.1047 | 0.0991 | 0.0056 |
| σ=0.00 | 0.0670 | 0.0659 | 0.0011 |
| σ=0.01 (default) | 0.0698 | 0.0686 | 0.0012 |
| σ=0.03 | 0.0983 | 0.0964 | 0.0019 |
| σ=0.06 | 0.1876 | 0.1846 | 0.0030 |

Both parameters increase absolute polarization monotonically (higher assimilation and higher noise both push variance up, noise more strongly: 0.067→0.188 across the tested range), and the static-vs-adaptive gap grows slightly in absolute terms at higher α and σ, but never exceeds ~5% of the static value at any setting — the personalization-dominates-cadence finding is not an artifact of the default α=0.08, σ=0.01 choice.

**Concept-drift experiment.** Static (0.0692±0.0055), adaptive-retrain-every-1 (0.0688±0.0058), and adaptive-retrain-every-20 (0.0688±0.0060) are statistically indistinguishable (paired t(2)=−0.12, p=0.92, d=−0.07; bootstrap 95% CI on the retrain1-vs-retrain20 gap: [−0.0005, 0.0002], consistent with zero). The adaptive recommender refits on *all* accumulated history rather than a recency-weighted window, so more frequent full-history refitting does not track a drifting target any better than infrequent refitting.

**Misspecified-recommender experiment (fully linear features).** Static variance is 0.0038±0.0026 — *below* random, because a linear-only model fit on a short cold-start log recommends near-randomly. Adaptive variance is 0.2895±0.0179 (t(2)=−25.0, p=0.0016, d=−14.4; bootstrap 95% CI on the gap: [−0.309, −0.272], excluding zero despite n=3). A retrain-frequency sweep shows this is non-monotonic (0.065 at freq=1, 0.32–0.33 at 2–10, 0.004 at 40≈static; n=3/point, suggestive not precisely located).

**Partial-misspecification sweep.**

| Feature set | Static var. | Adaptive var. |
|---|---|---|
| full (well-specified) | 0.0698 | 0.0686 |
| no_product (has distance) | 0.0646 | 0.0646 |
| no_dist (has product only) | 0.9530 | 0.9554 |
| linear_only (fully misspecified) | 0.0038 | 0.2895 |

`no_product` behaves like the well-specified case (can already represent ground truth). `no_dist` produces catastrophic, cadence-*insensitive* polarization (bootstrap 95% CI on the static-vs-adaptive gap: [−0.006, 0.000], consistent with zero — the two conditions are genuinely indistinguishable, not just similarly bad).

**Coefficient diagnostic (new): why no_dist and linear_only differ.** Fitting each feature set once on an unbiased random-exposure log reveals the mechanism. The `no_dist` model's coefficient on the product term is large (4.96, vs. ≤0.12 for opinion/item alone), and its argmax-scoring item is already at the extreme matching the opinion's sign for *every* opinion tested, including small ones (opinion=−0.2 → argmax item=−1.00; opinion=+0.2 → argmax item=+1.00) — a single cold-start fit already encodes "always recommend the most extreme same-sign item," so the catastrophe is present before any retraining happens, which is exactly why static and adaptive are indistinguishable. The `linear_only` model, in contrast, has small coefficients (0.023, 0.012) and nearly flat predicted probabilities across the entire item range (0.560–0.573, barely above the ~0.5 baseline) — a single fit can barely discriminate between items, so static behaves close to random, and only repeated retraining on the model's own increasingly skewed logs is able to bootstrap a sharper (if still crude) boundary, which is why that regime alone is cadence-dependent. The asymmetry is thus not about "how much" information is missing but about whether the retained features happen to encode a strong, wrong, self-consistent-with-extremization signal (product term) versus a weak, uninformative one (opinion/item alone).

**Diversity-injection ablation** (3 seeds): threshold-like — ε=0.0/0.1 (0 of K=5 slots replaced) identical (var 0.0686); ε=0.2 (1 slot) roughly halves variance to 0.0345; ε≥0.3 (2+ slots) drops to ≈0.022–0.024, matching random.

## 5. Conclusion

With a well-specified recommender, personalization alone accounts for nearly all measured polarization and diversity loss relative to random recommendations; retraining cadence has a small but statistically real effect, and this null result now survives concept drift, a corrected non-degenerate retraining schedule, and — new in this revision — a four-point sweep each over assimilation rate and opinion noise, closing a robustness gap flagged across three review rounds. With misspecified recommenders we see two distinct regimes, and a new coefficient-level diagnostic explains why: a fully linear feature set yields weak, near-uninformative single-fit predictions, so its large, non-monotonic polarization swing is genuinely built by repeated retraining (cadence-dependent); dropping only the distance feature yields a *misleadingly strong* single-fit signal (the raw product term already prefers extreme same-sign items), so its catastrophic polarization is present from the first fit and insensitive to cadence. Diversity injection remains a highly effective, threshold-sensitive lever (2 of 5 slots randomized restores near-baseline variance) across all regimes tested. For practitioners: if your recommender's features already capture the relevant similarity structure, retraining frequency is low-leverage — robustly so across a range of user-assimilation behaviors — and diversity injection should be prioritized; if it doesn't, check whether the retained features could produce a confidently-wrong signal even from a single fit (as a proxy like a raw interaction term can), since that determines whether cadence is a lever worth tuning at all or the model is already saturated regardless. Limitations: paired tests still rest on 3–5 seeds, though bootstrap CIs now corroborate the t-test conclusions for the misspecification and drift comparisons without requiring more runs; we tested one drift rate, four feature sets, and a 4×2-point assimilation/noise grid, not continuous sweeps; the whole study remains confined to scalar opinions and one recommender family (logistic regression), capping generalization beyond this synthetic setup. We flag a recency-weighted retraining variant, a continuous feature-ablation sweep, and the sycophancy-simulation extension as next steps.
