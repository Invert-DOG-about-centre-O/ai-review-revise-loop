# Does Semantic Clustering Actually Beat Lexical Entropy? A Controlled Toy Study of Uncertainty Signals in a Small Transformer

## Abstract

Semantic entropy — clustering sampled generations by meaning before computing
entropy — is widely presented as an improvement over naive lexical (surface-form)
entropy for detecting hallucinations in LLMs, on the intuition that surface-form
diversity inflates apparent uncertainty for questions that are actually
semantically determinate. We test this in a fully controlled synthetic
environment where ground-truth ambiguity is known by construction. We train a
small (≈0.4M-parameter) causal Transformer from scratch on a synthetic
"capital city" QA task with deterministic, genuinely ambiguous, and
out-of-distribution (OOD) question classes, and compare lexical vs.
oracle-semantic sampling entropy across 5 independent seeds. Lexical entropy
is a consistently equal-or-better predictor of ambiguity than semantic entropy
(mean AUROC 1.000±0.000 vs. 0.987±0.011, lexical ≥ semantic in 5/5 seeds).
This round we add two review-driven fixes. First, a *class-exclusive* alias
ablation (round-1's and round-2's stress test of our "additive offset"
explanation, now with cities partitioned into fully disjoint ambiguous-only
and deterministic-only pools, removing the city overlap that made our
previous version only suggestive): the gap narrows only to 0.993±0.010 (3
seeds), *less* narrowing than the overlap-confounded version reported last
round (0.970±0.017) — meaning that confound was inflating, not
understating, the apparent effect of class-correlated alias diversity.
Second, a 3-seed replication of the temperature ablation (previously a
single-seed number): the lexical-≥-semantic ranking, which looked
uniform at T∈{0.6,1.0,1.5} on one seed, is in fact seed-dependent at the
non-default temperatures — semantic entropy wins on 1 of 3 seeds at both
T=0.6 and T=1.5, and only the T=1.0 ranking (5/5 and 3/3 elsewhere in this
paper) is seed-robust. We also carry forward two other revised findings: the
length–entropy correlation among deterministic questions is unstable
across seeds (lexical: mean r=0.29, std=0.17) rather than a clean null, and
semantic entropy separates OOD from in-distribution questions well (AUROC
0.888±0.074) while the cheaper "unmapped token" novelty rate is much
noisier (AUROC 0.584±0.156). We report this as an honest, small-scale data
point, with variance estimates attached to every headline number and all
code, logs, and raw results included for inspection and re-run.

## 1. Introduction

Large language models frequently produce fluent but incorrect or unsupported
statements ("hallucinations"), and a growing line of work uses **sampling-based
uncertainty quantification (UQ)** — generating multiple completions and
measuring their diversity — as a training-free detector. The best-known method,
**semantic entropy** (Kuhn et al., 2023; Farquhar et al., 2024, *Nature*),
clusters sampled answers into equivalence classes using an NLI model before
computing entropy, arguing that naive token/string-level entropy conflates
*how an answer is phrased* with *whether the model knows the answer*.
Follow-up work proposes cheaper approximations — Semantic Entropy Probes
(Kossen et al., 2024), Kernel Language Entropy (NeurIPS 2024), Semantic Energy
(2025), and Semantic Reformulation Entropy (2025).

A recent evaluation paper — *"Revisiting Uncertainty Quantification Evaluation
in Language Models: Spurious Interactions with Response Length"* (2025) — flags
that reported UQ benchmark gains may partly reflect a length confound rather
than genuine discrimination. Survey papers (KDD 2025; ACM CSUR 2025) note that
evaluating semantic vs. lexical clustering cleanly is hard in practice: real
QA datasets have unknown ground-truth ambiguity, clustering depends on an
imperfect NLI/judge model, and length is entangled with topic/difficulty.

**Gap.** We are not aware of small, fully controlled studies isolating the
lexical-vs-semantic comparison from these confounds via a synthetic world
where true ambiguity and semantic equivalence classes are known exactly.

**Contribution, this revision.** Building on a two-round reviewer dialogue, we
report: (1) the main 5-seed null result (lexical ≥ semantic ambiguity
detection) is unchanged; (2) a *cleaner* version of our class-correlated
alias ablation, now with zero city overlap between classes, which shows the
"additive offset" mechanism is even more robust than our previous, confounded
ablation suggested; (3) a 3-seed temperature ablation revealing the
lexical-≥-semantic ranking is *not* uniformly seed-robust away from T=1.0,
a genuine limitation our single-seed v1/v2 numbers concealed; (4) the
previously revised, noisier length-confound finding; and (5) the previously
added OOD-detection AUROC quantification.

## 2. Related Work

- **Semantic entropy family.** Kuhn, Gal & Farquhar (ICLR 2023) cluster
  sampled generations via NLI entailment before computing entropy; Farquhar et
  al. (*Nature*, 2024) scale this to confabulation detection. Kossen et al.
  (2024) show semantic uncertainty is largely linearly decodable from hidden
  states. Kernel Language Entropy (NeurIPS 2024) generalizes hard clustering
  to a continuous kernel. Semantic Energy (2025) clusters penultimate-layer
  logits instead of post-softmax probabilities. Semantic Reformulation Entropy
  (2025) perturbs the question instead of clustering outputs.
- **Confound / evaluation critiques.** The length-bias paper (arXiv:2504.13677,
  2025) shows several UQ benchmarks' gains correlate with answer length rather
  than genuine calibration; survey literature (KDD 2025; ACM CSUR 2025) frames
  UQ evaluation methodology itself as an open problem.
- **This paper's relation to that work.** We contribute a controlled
  instrument for testing claims from that literature at small scale with
  exact ground truth — a complement to, not a replacement for, large-scale
  benchmark evaluation. We do not implement real NLI-based clustering even
  in the synthetic world; our "semantic entropy" is an oracle upper bound on
  clustering quality, so our results speak to the *ambiguity-detection
  ceiling* semantic clustering could achieve, not to any specific NLI
  implementation, which additionally suffers clustering error.

## 3. Method

**Synthetic world.** 65 "countries": 25 deterministic (one true city), 25
ambiguous (2–3 true cities), 15 OOD (unseen at training). 15 "cities"
(semantic classes), each with surface **aliases** — distinct token sequences
(length 1–3) referring to the same city. Training sequences are
`BOS Q <country> SEP <alias-tokens> EOS`, giving an exact oracle
alias→city map.

**Model.** Causal Transformer (2 layers, d_model=64, 4 heads, dff=128, ≈0.4M
params), trained with teacher forcing for 1200 steps (batch 64, Adam,
lr=3e-3), CPU-only, ~20–40s per run.

**Sampling & metrics.** K=40 samples/question; lexical entropy (Shannon
entropy over raw token sequences), semantic entropy (entropy over oracle city
id, with "unmapped" sequences tallied separately as a novelty rate), mean
answer length.

**Round-2 revisions (this file).** (1) *Class-exclusive alias ablation*
(reviewer weakness 1 / question 1): cities are split into two **disjoint**
pools of size 7 (ambiguous-only) and 8 (deterministic-only); ambiguous
countries draw their 2–3 valid cities only from the amb pool (4 aliases/city),
deterministic countries draw their single valid city only from the det pool
(2 aliases/city). No city is shared between classes, so unlike round-1's
ablation this is a clean causal manipulation, run over 3 seeds
(`experiment_v3.py`, Experiment C). (2) *Multi-seed temperature ablation*
(reviewer question 2): the same 3 seeds, each trained once, evaluated at
T∈{0.6, 1.0, 1.5} (Experiment D), replacing the single-seed number carried
over from v1/v2.

## 4. Results

Across all 14 total training runs (5 main-design + 3 original ablation + 3
class-exclusive ablation + 3 temperature-ablation seeds), final loss plateaus
at 0.83–1.02, consistent with the loss floor from genuine label noise. Code:
`experiment_v2.py`, `experiment_v3.py`; raw results: `results_v2.json`,
`results_v3.json` (all in this directory, seeds fixed, exactly reproducible).

**(a) Ambiguity discrimination, 5 seeds, T=1.0, K=40.**

| Metric | AUROC (mean ± std) | Seeds where lexical ≥ semantic |
|---|---|---|
| Lexical entropy | **1.000 ± 0.000** | — |
| Oracle semantic entropy | 0.987 ± 0.011 | 5/5 |

**(b) Class-correlated alias ablation (overlapping pools, round-1 version), 3 seeds.**

| Metric | AUROC (mean ± std) | Seeds where lexical ≥ semantic |
|---|---|---|
| Lexical entropy | 0.999 ± 0.001 | — |
| Oracle semantic entropy | 0.970 ± 0.017 | 3/3 |

**(c) Class-EXCLUSIVE alias ablation (disjoint pools, this revision), 3 seeds.**

| Metric | AUROC (mean ± std) | Seeds where lexical ≥ semantic |
|---|---|---|
| Lexical entropy | **1.000 ± 0.000** | — |
| Oracle semantic entropy | 0.993 ± 0.010 | 3/3 |

This directly answers reviewer question 1. With the city-overlap confound
removed entirely (amb-only and det-only cities are disjoint sets, 7 vs. 8
cities), semantic entropy's disadvantage is *smaller* than in the
confounded version (b) — 0.993 vs. 0.970 — and closer to the unablated
main result (a). In other words, the overlap in our original ablation was
not masking a larger effect; if anything it exaggerated the narrowing,
because some deterministic questions there inherited a high-alias-count city
and looked artificially more semantic-entropy-friendly. The clean version
confirms the ranking (lexical ≥ semantic, 3/3 seeds) survives even a
class-correlated alias design with zero shared cities, which is stronger
evidence for the "additive offset" mechanism than round-1's ablation
provided.

**(d) Length confound (deterministic questions only), 5 seeds.**

| Metric | Pearson r (mean ± std across seeds) |
|---|---|
| Lexical entropy | 0.29 ± 0.17 |
| Semantic entropy | −0.09 ± 0.21 |

As reported last round, the original single-seed near-zero correlation
(r=−0.009 lexical) understated true variability; per-seed r for lexical
entropy ranges from ≈0.05 to ≈0.60 across our 5 seeds. Answering reviewer
question 3 directly: yes, this changes our view — a seed reaching r≈0.6 with
only 25 deterministic questions is compatible with a real, non-trivial
length-entropy relationship in this design, not just sampling noise around
zero. We can no longer claim length and lexical entropy are unrelated here;
we can only say the *average* correlation across seeds is moderate and
noisy, and a larger deterministic-question pool (more than 25) would be
needed to pin down its true magnitude.

**(e) Temperature ablation, now 3 seeds × T∈{0.6, 1.0, 1.5}.**

| T | Lexical AUROC (mean±std) | Semantic AUROC (mean±std) | Seeds lexical ≥ semantic |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.6 | 0.993 ± 0.010 | 0.981 ± 0.027 | 2/3 |
| 1.0 | 1.000 ± 0.000 | 0.989 ± 0.014 | 3/3 |
| 1.5 | 0.986 ± 0.014 | 0.989 ± 0.007 | 2/3 |

This answers reviewer question 2 (the temperature ablation was carried over
unreplicated purely for time, not a validity judgment — replicating it took
under 3 minutes of additional compute). The result is more nuanced than the
single-seed v1/v2 number implied: at T=1.0, the ranking is seed-robust
(3/3, matching (a)). At T=0.6 and T=1.5, it is not — semantic entropy wins
outright on 1 of 3 seeds at each, and the mean gap is inside one standard
deviation of zero. We therefore withdraw the earlier claim that "lexical
entropy's AUROC edge... held at T∈{0.6, 1.0, 1.5}" — it holds reliably only
at the sampling temperature (T=1.0) used for the paper's main result, and is
seed-dependent (roughly a coin flip on which signal wins) off that point.

**(f) Out-of-distribution detection, quantified (5 seeds).**

| Score | AUROC, OOD vs. in-distribution (mean ± std) |
|---|---|
| Semantic entropy | 0.888 ± 0.074 |
| Unmapped ("novel token") rate | 0.584 ± 0.156 |

Semantic entropy is a good but imperfect OOD detector — well above chance
but far from the ≈1.0 ceiling reached on ambiguity detection — while the
unmapped-rate proxy is considerably noisier and should not be treated as a
drop-in replacement when quantitative reliability matters.

## 5. Discussion

The two additions in this revision push in opposite directions for how
confident the paper's claims should be. The class-exclusive alias ablation
(c) makes the central "lexical ≥ semantic, and it's not just fixed alias
count" claim *more* robust than round-1's version suggested, because
removing the city-overlap confound narrowed the semantic-entropy deficit
less, not more. The temperature ablation (e) does the opposite: it shows a
claim we stated too broadly last round (uniform advantage across
temperatures) does not survive multi-seed scrutiny once we actually looked.
We think reporting both directions honestly — a stress test that
strengthens the case, and a re-run that weakens a peripheral claim — is more
useful than only ever finding evidence that supports the original narrative.

We still do not compare against a real NLI-based semantic clustering
implementation, even within this synthetic world; our "semantic entropy" is
an oracle upper bound, so a real implementation could only do worse here,
not better. This paper's scope is therefore "the best-case ceiling for
semantic clustering vs. lexical entropy, under exact ground truth," not
"semantic entropy vs. lexical entropy in practice."

**Limitations.** 5 seeds for the main comparison, length-confound, and OOD
metric; 3 seeds each for the overlapping and exclusive alias ablations and
for the temperature ablation (compute budget, not principle — each run is
CPU-only and 20–45s); a 0.4M-parameter model trained for 1200 steps is not
representative of frontier LLM scale; the alias mechanism remains a
simplified stand-in for real paraphrase diversity; K=40 samples bounds
entropy-estimate precision; only 25 deterministic questions per seed limits
how precisely we can pin down the length-entropy correlation; no real
NLI-clustering baseline is tested even in this synthetic world; the
temperature-ablation ranking is explicitly seed-dependent off T=1.0 and
should not be over-read as a uniform effect.

## 6. Conclusion

Across 5 independent seeds, lexical sampling entropy matched or slightly
outperformed oracle semantic-clustering entropy at detecting genuine
ambiguity (AUROC 1.000±0.000 vs. 0.987±0.011). A class-exclusive alias
ablation — the clean version of a stress test that was previously confounded
by city overlap — confirms this ranking survives even when alias diversity
is deliberately made class-correlated with zero shared cities (1.000±0.000
vs. 0.993±0.010, 3/3 seeds), evidence stronger than our round-1 ablation
provided. A 3-seed temperature replication, however, shows the ranking is
seed-robust only at the paper's default sampling temperature and is
roughly a coin flip at T=0.6/1.5 — a genuine limitation our earlier
single-seed number obscured. The length-entropy confound remains noisy
rather than absent (mean r=0.29±0.17), and we now read individual seeds
reaching r≈0.6 as evidence a real confound may exist at this sample size,
not merely as noise. Entropy-based signals separate OOD from
in-distribution questions well above chance (AUROC 0.888±0.074), though a
cheaper novelty-rate proxy is markedly noisier (0.584±0.156). We see this as
a small, honest, variance-aware data point supporting more controlled
probing of *why* semantic clustering helps (or doesn't) in specific regimes,
rather than treating "semantic > lexical" as a mechanism-free given. All
code, logs, and raw results (original, round-1, and this revision) are
provided for verification and extension.
