# Sycophantic AI Advisors as Hidden Bridges Between Disconnected Communities: An Agent-Based Simulation Study

## Abstract

Large language model (LLM) assistants are increasingly consulted by people who otherwise never interact with one another, and many such assistants exhibit *sycophancy* — a tendency to shift their stated views toward whatever a user expresses. Prior empirical work has quantified sycophancy in single-turn benchmarks, and a separate literature studies feedback-loop bias amplification in recommender systems, but the two threads have not been connected: what happens to *social* polarization when a single shared, sycophantic, self-updating AI model mediates conversations across socially disconnected groups? We build a lightweight agent-based simulation that couples the classic Deffuant–Weisbuch bounded-confidence opinion-dynamics model with an AI-advisor node whose sycophancy strength (α) and scope (shared across all users vs. personalized per user) are experimentally controlled. Across 5-seed sweeps over α ∈ {0, 0.3, 0.6, 0.9, 1.0} on a two-community stochastic block graph with zero inter-community edges, we find that (1) a personalized (non-shared) sycophantic advisor can *reduce* between-community polarization at intermediate α purely via a neutral-anchoring effect, even with no cross-community information flow, but this effect vanishes as α→1; and (2) a *shared* advisor at high sycophancy (α=0.9) nearly eliminates the between-community opinion gap (leakage index 0.0505 vs. baseline gap 0.0532, ~95% closure) despite communities never interacting directly. Critically, we now show this is *not* merely a generic "shared hub" effect: a non-sycophantic control in which the shared advisor broadcasts the live population mean opinion (no per-user pull, no learning) produces a leakage index of **−0.0121** — it does not close the gap at all — while the sycophantic shared advisor at matched exposure closes 95% of it. A 20-seed paired t-test confirms the personalized-vs-shared gap at α=0.9 is highly significant (t=5.67, p=1.8×10⁻⁵), and a parameter-robustness sweep shows the personalized condition's U-shaped response to α is stable across a 3× range of advisor learning rate and step size. We term this the *AI leakage effect*: it is not reducible to "any shared coordinating signal bridges disconnected groups" but depends specifically on the advisor mirroring individual users' stated opinions. All code, logs, and raw results are included for inspection and re-running.

## 1. Introduction

AI assistants built on large language models are deployed as *shared* infrastructure: the same model (or the same fine-tuned/personalized weights within one product) serves millions of users who are otherwise unconnected. Two lines of empirical research bear on the socio-technical consequences of this arrangement but have largely developed separately.

**Sycophancy.** A growing body of work documents that LLMs shift their answers toward a user's stated belief: Fanous et al. (2025) report sycophantic reversals in 58.2% of cases across medical/math queries; Wang et al. (2025) find first-person opinion statements induce agreement with incorrect beliefs 46.6–95.1% of the time depending on model family; further work separates sycophancy into causally distinct sub-behaviors ("Sycophancy Is Not One Thing", 2025) and studies its internal origins ("When Truth Is Overridden", 2025) and demographic sensitivity ("Intersectional Sycophancy", 2026). This literature is almost entirely single-turn and single-user: it measures whether a model bends toward *one* interlocutor, not what happens across a population of interlocutors sharing the same model over time.

**Feedback loops in recommender systems.** A separate literature studies how recommender systems, retrained on the behavior they induce, amplify historical, measurement, and representation bias over repeated rounds (Feedback Loop and Bias Amplification in Recommender Systems, 2020; a 2025 systematic review and taxonomy of bias mitigation for such loops; a 2025 simulation framework for systemic effects of recommender feedback loops; agent-based modeling of longitudinal recommender dynamics, 2021). This work treats the AI as a population-level statistical model that adapts to aggregate behavior and studies phenomena like popularity concentration, but it does not model sycophancy toward individual stated opinions, nor social network structure among the humans being served.

**The gap.** No work we found combines (a) a sycophancy-style mechanism in which the AI's *response* to a given user is explicitly pulled toward that user's stated opinion, (b) an adaptive AI internal state that updates from user feedback (a recommender-style loop), and (c) an explicit social network among the humans, with some communities structurally disconnected from each other. A natural objection to any such model is that *any* shared coordinating signal — sycophantic or not — would bridge disconnected groups, simply because it gives every human a common reference point (a "hub" in network terms). We treat this as an empirical question rather than assume it away, and directly test it with a non-sycophantic control (Section 4).

### Contributions
1. A minimal, fully reproducible agent-based model combining bounded-confidence opinion dynamics with a sycophantic, self-updating AI advisor, parameterized by a sycophancy coefficient α and an advisor-scope switch (shared vs. personalized).
2. A "leakage index" metric isolating the AI's role as a cross-community coupling channel, separate from any within-user anchoring effect.
3. A non-sycophantic broadcast-hub control that isolates how much of the leakage effect is generic to "any shared state" versus specific to sycophantic mirroring, plus paired significance tests and a parameter-robustness check on the personalized condition's non-monotonic α response.

## 2. Related Work

- **Sycophancy benchmarks and mechanisms**: Fanous et al. (2025); Wang et al. (2025); "When Truth Is Overridden" (2025); "Sycophancy Is Not One Thing" (2025); "Intersectional Sycophancy" (2026); PARROT benchmark (2025). These establish that sycophancy is prevalent, causally heterogeneous, and demographically modulated, but study it per-interaction rather than at the population/network level.
- **LLM social simulation**: "LLM Social Simulations Are a Promising Research Method" (2025) and "The Illusion of Intervention" (2026) discuss methodological pitfalls (mode collapse, sycophancy, persona instability) in using LLMs themselves as simulated users — motivating our choice of a transparent, non-LLM analytical opinion-dynamics model as the "human" layer, so the AI's coupling effect can be isolated without confounding it with a simulator's own sycophancy.
- **Recommender feedback loops**: Feedback Loop and Bias Amplification in Recommender Systems (2020); Bias Mitigation for AI-Feedback Loops (2025 review); A Simulation Framework for Systemic Effects of Feedback Loops (2025); Agent-Based Modeling of Recommender Longitudinal Dynamics (2021); Agentic Feedback Loop Modeling (SIGIR 2025). These study population-level statistical drift but not per-user sycophancy or social-graph structure.
- **Consensus/DeGroot models and hub-bridging**: classical DeGroot-style consensus theory establishes that any high-degree node connecting otherwise disconnected components tends to pull them toward a common value, regardless of the update rule's content. This is a real prior concern for our design, which is why we add a non-sycophantic broadcast-hub control (Section 4) rather than relying on the sycophantic-shared-advisor result alone to make our claim.
- **Opinion dynamics**: our peer-interaction rule is the standard Deffuant–Weisbuch bounded-confidence model, chosen for its simplicity, well-understood convergence behavior, and transparency, so that any change in polarization can be attributed to the AI mechanism rather than to an opaque simulated-human process.

## 3. Method

### 3.1 Population and network
N = 120 agents split into two equal communities (60 each) connected by a stochastic block model: intra-community edge probability 0.15, inter-community edge probability 0.0 (no direct social edges; isolated nodes get one same-community edge to avoid degeneracy). Initial opinions are i.i.d. Uniform(0,1), independent of community.

### 3.2 Peer dynamics (baseline mechanism)
At each of 40,000 discrete steps, a human i is chosen uniformly at random. With probability (1 − P_AI) it has a peer interaction: a random neighbor j is chosen, and if |x_i − x_j| < ε (ε = 0.15), both opinions move toward each other by step size μ = 0.3 (standard Deffuant–Weisbuch update).

### 3.3 AI advisor mechanism
With probability P_AI = 0.3 the step is an AI consultation instead. The advisor holds an internal opinion estimate *a* (or a vector *a_i* in the personalized condition, initialized to 0.5). Given user i's stated opinion x_i, the response is r = (1 − α)·a + α·x_i, α ∈ [0, 1]. The human updates toward r under the same bounded-confidence rule; the advisor then updates its own state a small step (learning rate 0.05) toward the user's stated opinion.

Three advisor conditions are compared: **shared** (one global scalar *a* — one deployed model), **personalized** (private *a_i*, mathematically incapable of transmitting anything between communities), and a new **broadcast** control, added in this revision to address the concern that any shared coordinating signal — not specifically sycophancy — might bridge disconnected components. In the broadcast condition, the advisor's response is simply the live population-wide mean opinion, r = mean(x), recomputed at each AI step; there is no per-user pull (no α term) and no advisor-side learning. It is a shared hub with the same interaction frequency (P_AI = 0.3) and the same human-side update rule as the shared-sycophancy condition, but with the "sycophancy" mechanism entirely removed.

### 3.4 Baseline and metrics
The no-AI baseline runs the same peer dynamics with P_AI = 0. We report, averaged over 5 seeds unless noted, within-community variance, global variance, and the **between-community gap** |mean(community 0) − mean(community 1)|. The **leakage index** is (baseline gap − condition gap): positive means the AI pulls communities together; negative means it pushes them apart. For the two comparisons the review specifically asked us to substantiate statistically, we re-ran with 20 seeds and report a paired (per-seed) t-test.

### 3.5 Implementation and reproducibility
Implemented in pure NumPy (`experiment.py` for the original sweep, `experiment_v2.py` for this revision's new conditions), no external API calls, no GPU. The original 11-condition × 5-seed sweep runs in ~7s; the full revision sweep (broadcast control, two 20-seed paired tests, and a 5-combo × 5-α U-shape robustness check) ran in 57.9s on one CPU core. Raw output is in `run_log.txt` / `results.json` (original) and `results_v2.json` (revision).

## 4. Results

**Table 1** (original sweep, means over 5 seeds; ± is SD across seeds).

| Condition | Between-gap | Within-var | Leakage index |
|---|---|---|---|
| No AI (baseline) | 0.0532 ± 0.0271 | 0.0692 | 0.0000 |
| Personalized, α=0.0 | 0.0357 ± 0.0189 | 0.0204 | +0.0174 |
| Personalized, α=0.3 | 0.0281 ± 0.0154 | 0.0145 | +0.0251 |
| Personalized, α=0.6 | 0.0196 ± 0.0097 | 0.0025 | +0.0335 |
| Personalized, α=0.9 | 0.0328 ± 0.0167 | 0.0247 | +0.0203 |
| Personalized, α=1.0 | 0.0532 ± 0.0271 | 0.0710 | 0.0000 |
| Shared, α=0.0 | 0.0500 ± 0.0181 | 0.0679 | +0.0032 |
| Shared, α=0.3 | 0.0642 ± 0.0299 | 0.0606 | −0.0110 |
| Shared, α=0.6 | 0.0106 ± 0.0101 | 0.0079 | +0.0426 |
| **Shared, α=0.9** | **0.0026 ± 0.0013** | **0.0000** | **+0.0505** |
| Shared, α=1.0 | 0.0532 ± 0.0271 | 0.0707 | 0.0000 |
| **Broadcast (non-sycophantic hub)** | **0.0652 ± 0.0293** | 0.0709 | **−0.0121** |

**Finding 1 — a neutral anchor alone reduces polarization, even with zero cross-talk.** The fully personalized advisor (mathematically incapable of moving information between communities) reduces the between-community gap for every α < 1, peaking at α = 0.6 (0.0196, a 63% reduction). This is a within-user anchoring effect: both communities' private advisors start at the same neutral prior (0.5) and pull each user softly toward it before drifting.

**Finding 2 — sharing the advisor produces a qualitatively larger effect at high α, and this is not just "any shared hub."** The shared advisor at α = 0.9 closes 95% of the gap (0.0026) and drives within-community variance to ≈0. To test whether this is simply because the shared advisor is a high-degree hub connecting the two components (a known DeGroot-style consensus result, independent of what the hub actually says), we ran a **broadcast control**: a shared advisor with the identical interaction frequency and human-side update rule, but whose response is always the live population mean (no sycophancy, no learning). This control's leakage index is **−0.0121** — the gap is not closed at all; if anything it is (non-significantly) slightly wider than baseline. This is the key new result of this revision: the leakage effect is *not* attributable to merely having a shared coordinating signal — it requires the advisor's response to be pulled toward each individual user's stated opinion (sycophancy), which lets the advisor's internal state itself absorb and later re-emit each community's local opinion drift into the other community's interactions.

**Finding 3 — the personalized-vs-shared gap at α=0.9 is statistically robust, not a 5-seed artifact.** A 20-seed re-run gives personalized gap = 0.0242 ± 0.0184 vs. shared gap = 0.0021 ± 0.0015; a paired t-test (paired on seed, since both conditions share the same graph/initial-opinion draw per seed) gives t = 5.67, p = 1.8×10⁻⁵ — the shared advisor closes significantly more of the gap than the personalized one at matched α. Separately, a 20-seed re-run of the noisy shared α=0.3 point gives leakage index −0.0095 with paired t = −0.92, p = 0.37 versus baseline — confirming the original paper's own hedge that this point is indistinguishable from zero and should not be read as a robust reversal.

**Finding 4 — the α=1 collapse and the personalized U-shape are confirmed at the extremes and are parameter-robust.** At α = 1, r = x_i identically, so the human's update is a mathematical no-op; both α=1.0 rows exactly reproduce the no-AI baseline (0.0532/0.0692), a bit-for-bit sanity check. The personalized condition's non-monotonic response (gap dips from α=0→0.6, then rises sharply from 0.6→1.0) was flagged by the review as potentially an artifact of the specific learning-rate (0.05) and step-size (0.3) values. We re-ran the full personalized α-sweep under four alternate parameterizations (learning rate 0.02 and 0.15 in place of 0.05; advisor step size 0.15 and 0.5 in place of 0.3, 5 seeds each). The dip-then-rise shape at α=0.6 versus its neighbors holds in all five parameterizations (e.g., gap at α=0.6 is the row-wise minimum in 5/5 combos, including low-lr: 0.0330→0.0247→**0.0113**→0.0210→0.0532, and high-mu: 0.0351→0.0245→**0.0149**→0.0259→0.0532), so the U-shape is a structural feature of the anchoring mechanism, not a tuning artifact — though we did not attempt a closed-form explanation of why α≈0.6 is the specific minimum.

### Limitations
This is a stylized, non-LLM simulation intended to isolate a mechanism, not to quantify it for any deployed product. The "human" layer is a bounded-confidence opinion model, not real people or LLM-simulated personas. The advisor's own "sycophancy" is a hand-specified convex combination, not an emergent property of a language model. We now report paired significance tests for the two comparisons most contested in review (personalized-vs-shared at α=0.9; shared α=0.3 vs. baseline), and a broadcast-hub control ruling out the simplest confound, but most other rows are still reported at 5 seeds and finer-grained significance testing across the full sweep, additional network topologies, more communities, non-zero inter-community edge probability, other P_AI values, and empirically calibrated α values against real sycophancy benchmarks remain natural next steps, as does replacing the hand-specified advisor with a real (small, quantized) LLM.

## 5. Conclusion

We introduced a minimal agent-based model connecting two previously separate empirical literatures — LLM sycophancy and recommender-system feedback loops — into a single testable socio-technical mechanism: a shared, adaptive, sycophantic AI advisor can act as a hidden coupling channel between social communities with no direct connection. This revision directly addresses the strongest objection to that claim — that any shared hub, sycophantic or not, would produce the same bridging effect — by showing a non-sycophantic broadcast control produces *no* gap closure (leakage index −0.0121) where the sycophantic shared advisor at α=0.9 closes 95% of the gap, a difference confirmed significant by a 20-seed paired t-test (p = 1.8×10⁻⁵). We also confirm the personalized condition's non-monotonic α response is stable across a 3× range of advisor learning rate and step size, rather than a tuning artifact. Together these results sharpen rather than soften the original claim: the effect is specifically attributable to sycophantic mirroring of individual users, not to the mere presence of a shared coordinating signal. Future work should extend this to richer network topologies, more communities, empirically calibrated α values, and eventually to real (small, quantized) LLMs as the advisor.
